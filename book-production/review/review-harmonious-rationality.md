# Review: *The Manual of Harmonious Rationality*

**Scope reviewed:** full text, 1,325 pp. / ~207,000 words / 49 chapters across 9 books, plus back matter.

---

## 1. The system, stated compactly

**Metaethics — "Constructivist Realism."** Values are constructed by conscious agents but constrained by three objective sources: valence (the physical reality of suffering/flourishing), game theory (the mathematics of coordination), and evolved cognition (the actual hardware). Bridge analogy: physics doesn't design the bridge, it vetoes bad designs.

**Foundation — one axiom.** *The suffering of a conscious being matters.* Defended by a claimed three-layer grounding: (1) wide reflective equilibrium, (2) performative incoherence, (3) inference to the best explanation. The book claims these are independent, so an opponent must defeat all three.

**Diagnosis — Moloch.** Most unnecessary suffering comes not from malice but from coordination failure. Morality is the technology invented to escape bad Nash equilibria.

**Architecture — dual-process ethics.** Mode A (pre-committed rules + twelve virtues) is the default. Mode B (explicit consequentialist override) requires five substantive conditions (catastrophic irreversible harm, attributable to rule-following, last resort, articulable to a skeptical third party, self-interest absent or corrected) plus three procedural gates (bright-line trigger, mandatory deliberation period, mandatory post-hoc review).

**Value theory.** Welfare is a six-dimensional vector: hedonic tone, autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, meaning.

**Applications.** Politics: error-correction primacy, subsidiarity, constrained democracy, strong free-speech default. Economics: Hayek + Pigou + Ostrom + a Sen-style capability floor. Frontier: x-risk, AI, bioethics, environment, digital.

**Defense + meta.** Nine head-to-head chapters against rival systems, a Position Registry with confidence ratings, and a twelve-clause Oath.

---

## 2. What genuinely works

**The Mode B gating protocol is the best idea in the book.** Almost every threshold deontology in the literature stops at substantive conditions — "unless the consequences are catastrophic." This book adds a *procedural* layer that treats the agent as an unreliable evaluator of their own case: bright-line categorization before evaluation, a mandatory 24-hour delay outside physical emergencies, and documented post-hoc review regardless of outcome. That is a real contribution. The insight that emotional urgency mimics genuine crisis, and that the fix is architectural rather than exhortative, is the kind of thing most moral philosophy gestures at and never operationalizes.

**Coordination failure as the organizing diagnosis is productive.** It explains why morality has the *shape* it has — rules, reputation, punishment, costly signaling — rather than just what it demands. It also generates the political and economic material non-arbitrarily, which is more than most ethical systems manage when they extend into policy.

**Explicit axiom placement.** Naming exactly where the is-ought bridge sits, rather than smuggling normativity in through "rational agency" or "human nature," is better practice than most of the canon.

**The concession sections are real.** "What utilitarianism gets right," "what anti-realists get right," "what virtue ethics gets right" contain substantive admissions, not token ones. The Modal Utilitarian section in Ch. 39, which essentially concedes that a sufficiently modified utilitarian *is* the framework, is intellectually honest to the point of self-harm — I'd keep it.

**Confidence labels on positions** (AXIOM / STRONG / MODERATE / PROVISIONAL). Rare and correct.

## 3. Structural problems

### 3.1 The three grounding layers are not independent — this is the central defect

The Ch. 3 architecture claims that distributing justification across three layers means "to defeat the axiom, an opponent must defeat all three simultaneously." Read the layers side by side and they collapse into roughly one.

Layer 1 (reflective equilibrium) adduces: our considered judgments, the convergence of independent traditions, the impossibility of constructing a stable moral system without the axiom, and explanatory power across domains.

Layer 3 (IBE) adduces: universal harm-avoidance norms, the independent convergence of traditions, moral progress, and the impossibility of a stable cooperation-sustaining system.

These are the same facts twice, labeled once as coherence and once as abduction. Layer 3 is not an independent line of support; it is Layer 1's evidence base rewritten in abductive syntax.

Worse, all three layers share a single vulnerable premise. Layer 2's admitted limit is stated well in the book — the error theorist grants psychological inescapability and denies that it constitutes a moral fact. But that same reply defeats Layers 1 and 3. Coherence with *our* considered judgments, convergence among *human* traditions, and explanatory power for *human* moral practice are all facts about the evaluative dispositions of one evolved primate. The error theorist's move is identical in each case: yes, and none of that is a fact about the universe's normative structure.

So the claimed defensive architecture doesn't exist. One objection reaches all three layers. The book should either defend the shared premise directly or drop the "three independent pillars" framing — which, as written, reads as a rhetorical firewall rather than an argument.

### 3.2 Valence realism carries the system and is the least defended claim in it

The reply to Street's Darwinian Dilemma is the right *shape*: reclassify the axiom from an evaluative attitude to a claim about physical reality, so the debunking argument misses. But the thermometer analogy answers the easier half of the dilemma. It shows our *awareness of* pain is causally connected to pain. Street's target is not the descriptive belief "I am in pain" — nobody debunks that. Her target is the evaluative attitude "pain is bad." The analogy would need a thermometer whose readings track not temperature but temperature's *badness*, and no causal story is offered for that.

The book also slides between two claims that need separating:

- (a) Pain states are physically real states of physical systems. *Uncontroversial.*
- (b) The badness of pain is a physical property of those states. *This is the whole game, and it is asserted rather than argued.*

The bridge/physics analogy inherits the same problem. Physics vetoes bridge designs *given* the goal of not collapsing. Valence vetoes moral systems only *given* that valence matters. The analogy presupposes what it is deployed to establish.

Finally, the book concedes in Ch. 38 that the Street reply "works if valence realism is correct" and that illusionists (Dennett, Frankish) break it, then defers to Chs. 3 and 5 — where Ch. 5 explicitly declines to settle consciousness. The foundation is therefore conditional on an open question in philosophy of mind, and the layered architecture that was supposed to insulate against exactly this doesn't (§3.1).

### 3.3 The egoism-to-impartiality gap is never closed

The performative incoherence argument establishes that no conscious agent can be indifferent to *their own* suffering. Chapter 1 then moves to universality by burden-shifting: the denier "needs a principled reason why others' suffering doesn't [matter], which puts the burden of proof squarely on the denier."

The rational egoist has that reason ready, and it is not obscure. It is the indexical one: my pain is bad-*for-me*, yours is bad-*for-you*, and there is no view from nowhere at which either is bad *simpliciter*. This is Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason, the problem Parfit spent *On What Matters* on. Shifting the burden is not answering it.

This matters more than any other single gap, because everything universalist downstream depends on it: the nativism critique, the expanding moral circle, global obligations, animal welfare, the whole scope argument. The book needs a real argument from "matters to me" to "matters," and currently has a rhetorical one.

### 3.4 The value vector is asserted, and it does enormous unearned work

The axiom names one thing. Five more dimensions — autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, meaning — appear without derivation. (They look like Self-Determination Theory's triad plus Nussbaum plus a meaning term, which is fine as a source but not as a justification.)

This forces a dilemma the book never faces:

- **If the five are irreducible to hedonic tone**, they are five additional normative axioms, and the claim that the framework rests on "the smallest possible normative assumption" is false.
- **If they are reducible** (they matter because being deprived of them feels bad), the vector is a scalar in disguise, and every defense that depends on non-aggregability fails.

That second horn is expensive, because the vector is what blocks the utility monster, the repugnant conclusion, and the transplant case. Three of the framework's most-advertised advantages over utilitarianism rest on a move that is currently a stipulation.

Related: **there is no aggregation rule.** "Evaluate the full vector" is not a decision procedure, and Specification 2 demands one. Is the structure lexical? Threshold-per-dimension (Nussbaum's answer)? Weighted sum with diminishing returns? Without an answer, the framework's verdicts in hard cases are unconstrained, which is precisely the failure it charges virtue ethics with.

### 3.5 Moloch monism under-describes the enemy

"The enemy is not human wickedness but coordination failure" is the organizing claim of Book II, and it sets up mechanism design as the master tool. But a large share of historical atrocity is *coordination success*: genocides, slave economies, cartels, and conquest are groups solving their internal coordination problem extremely well at the expense of an outgroup. That is not Moloch. It is a well-functioning coalition with a misdrawn moral circle.

The framework has the resources to say this — it's a scope-of-valence problem, not a coordination problem — but the architecture doesn't reflect it. Mechanism design is the wrong instrument for the coordinated-predation case; better incentive alignment among the perpetrators makes it worse. The "What Moloch Cannot Explain" section acknowledges the issue and then moves on. It deserves a chapter and a second diagnostic axis, because it changes which tools apply.

### 3.6 Mode B's safeguards are asymmetric in the wrong direction

Every one of the five conditions and three gates filters **false positives** — overriding a rule when you shouldn't. Not one addresses **false negatives** — following a rule when you shouldn't.

But the moral catastrophes the framework most cares about are dominated by false negatives: bureaucratic compliance, Milgram, institutional cover-ups, the "moral mazes" Ch. 18 itself analyzes. And the tension is sharpest inside the framework's own scenario. Gate 1(c) opens Mode B for institutional capture — the whistleblower's gate — and then Gate 2 imposes a deliberation period and Gate 3 mandates documented post-hoc review, in the one case where delay destroys the opportunity and documentation endangers the agent.

What's needed is an explicit asymmetry analysis: expected cost of false positive vs. false negative, *by domain*. In private life with reversible stakes, heavy anti-override bias is right. Inside a captured institution facing irreversible harm, it is backwards. The current protocol applies one calibration everywhere.

### 3.7 The novelty claim is overstated; the honest version is stronger

Chapter 1 says the dual-process architecture "is not a compromise between deontology and consequentialism — it is an architecture that explains when each is appropriate and why." Hare's two-level utilitarianism (intuitive level / critical level) is structurally the same proposal, and Hare gets no chapter. Neither does Brandt. Neither does Parfit, despite the convergence thesis in the Modal Utilitarian section being essentially Parfit's climbing-the-same-mountain argument.

The book's own Ch. 39 admits the modal utilitarian "is… approximately the framework." Given that, the defensible claim is narrower and better: *the innovation is the procedural gating, not the dual-process structure.* Nobody else has specified bright-line triggers, mandatory delay, and post-hoc review as architecture. Claim that; drop the larger claim, which a hostile reviewer will puncture in one paragraph.

### 3.8 Convergence is used as evidence three times without addressing that it's debunkable

Convergence of traditions appears in Layer 1, in Layer 3, and again in the Modal Utilitarian section as evidence of tracking mind-independent moral features. But convergence among theories built by cognitively similar agents from cognitively similar intuitions is exactly what Street's dilemma predicts on the anti-realist horn. The book engages the dilemma in Ch. 38 and then keeps using convergence elsewhere as if it hadn't. Either the debunking reply generalizes to cover convergence, or convergence stops being evidence. Currently the book wants both.

### 3.9 The applied politics and economics are imported, not derived

The claim is that Books V–VI are "derived from the same foundations, not imported from a separate ideology." Partly true: error-correction primacy, subsidiarity, and mechanism-design-over-ideology follow reasonably. The specific policy bundle does not.

EITC over minimum wage, school choice, carbon tax with dividend, universal catastrophic coverage with consumer choice below the threshold, zoning reform over rent control, prediction markets, constitutional originalism as "a reasonable interpretive approach," skepticism of identity-based policy — this is a recognizable and specific cluster (roughly: rationalist-adjacent neoliberalism, GMU/Niskanen-flavored). Those positions may well be right. They are not entailed by valence realism plus game theory. They are the outputs of contested empirical judgments, and presenting them as derivations obscures where the real disagreement lives.

Two symptoms confirm this:

**The stress-tests are not symmetric.** "Against Progressivism" delivers five substantive breaks with concrete policy consequences. "Against Conservatism" rejects religious grounding of public policy, climate denial, and nativism — three positions the book's priors already disfavor — while endorsing institutional conservatism essentially wholesale. A real stress-test would break with something the framework is attached to: the strong free-speech default, prediction markets, or Caplan's rational irrationality thesis (which is treated as established and is in fact heavily contested — and is doing the work of justifying constraints on democracy).

**Empirical confidence spikes exactly where the priors are.** Minimum-wage disemployment is stated flatly ("reduce employment among the least-skilled workers"); the post-Card–Krueger literature is genuinely unsettled and the modern consensus is closer to "modest-to-null effects at moderate levels." Rent control's supply effects are on firmer ground; the minimum wage claim is not. This is the book's own Chapter 2 diagnosis — motivated reasoning tightening confidence where interest is engaged — operating on the book. Flagging it explicitly, with the confidence downgraded, would be more persuasive than the current assertion.

### 3.10 The rival-engagement roster has a revealing asymmetry

Nine "Against" chapters, all against Western systems. Confucianism, Buddhism, and Ubuntu appear only in Ch. 46 — the "what we take from each" chapter — that is, as **donors**, never as **opponents**. Western frameworks are steel-manned and defeated; non-Western frameworks are mined for compatible parts. Whatever the intent, the structure reads as treating non-Western traditions as resources rather than as competitors capable of winning, which inverts the book's own Rule 2.

Also missing as rivals:

- **Korsgaard.** A framework named *Constructivist Realism* has to engage Kantian constructivism, which owns the term and reaches different conclusions. Its absence is conspicuous.
- **Street as constructivist**, not just as debunker — her Humean constructivism is the closest live rival to this book's metaethics.
- **Dancy / moral particularism** — a direct attack on the entire Mode A rules architecture, unaddressed.
- **Williams** — the integrity objection is the strongest general objection to systematizing ethics, and it appears only in passing.
- **Scanlon** — cited as a tool, never engaged as a competitor.

### 3.11 The community clause creates the error-correction failure it diagnoses

Oath clause VII asks the reader to "seek contexts in which my commitments are reinforced and my failures noticed." Combined with the syllabus, the virtues, and the oath itself, the sociological shape here is a movement.

The book anticipates "moral superiority" as a failure mode, which is good, but misses the structural risk: an error-correction mechanism staffed by co-adherents will correct errors *within* shared priors and be blind outside them. That is exactly the tribal-epistemology / moral-mazes failure the book diagnoses in institutions. Real error-correction requires adversarial review by people who reject the axiom. Clause VII currently specifies the opposite. One paragraph would fix this.

## 4. Production and craft defects

**Cross-references are systematically off by one from ~Ch. 25 onward.** Rawls is Ch. 42, referenced as 41. Marxism is 44, referenced as 43. The Syllabus is 48, referenced as 47. Divine Command is 37, referenced as 36. Existential risk is 32, referenced as 31. Chapter 1's architecture preview lists Book VII as 31–35, VIII as 36–45, IX as 46–48 — all off by one against the actual TOC. The Position Index inherits it (POS-EC01 → CH-25 should be CH-26; POS-F01 → CH-31 should be CH-32). Almost certainly caused by inserting Ch. 25 (Economic Policy Evaluation) after the cross-references were written. Dozens of instances; mechanically fixable, but it directly undercuts the "trace any claim" promise.

**No bibliography, no notes, no citations — zero.** The Preface states: "Direct quotes from primary sources appear throughout. The reader should be able to trace any major claim to its source and evaluate the argument independently." They cannot. Quotes carry author and title but no page; there is no reference list anywhere. For a book whose core methodological claim is epistemic accountability, this is the single most damaging omission. It is also the easiest large win available.

**The Position Registry is not in the book.** It's advertised in Ch. 1 Rule 1 as the self-correction engine, given its own back-matter section, and made Oath clause XI — and the Position Index then points to an external `Positions/POSITION_REGISTRY.md`. The vulnerability ratings and rebuttals, which are the entire point, are absent. Inline it or stop claiming it.

**Draft artifacts left in.** Title page reads `[Author Name]`. Glossary says "Page numbers to be added during typesetting." Chapter 3 refers to "the first edition of this chapter" and "the second edition" inside a book labeled First Edition.

**Length.** ~207k words with heavy redundancy. Most chapters have both a closing section and a Summary; Ch. 46 (Synthesis) and Ch. 47 (Complete Map) substantially overlap; Ch. 47 then re-summarizes the summaries. A 25–30% cut is available with no content loss, and would materially improve the book's odds of being finished by readers.

## 5. Verdict

**As synthesis and pedagogy:** strong. Better organized, more honest about its own uncertainty, and more willing to concede ground than most moral philosophy written for general readers. The steel-manning discipline is real. Someone who worked through this would come out with a genuinely usable map of the terrain.

**As original philosophy:** thinner than 1,325 pages implies. Two real contributions — procedural gating of the consequentialist override, and coordination failure as the organizing diagnosis. Both are good. Both would fit in 150 pages. The metaethics is a competent restatement of territory Railton, Boyd, Brink, and Parfit already occupy, presented with more confidence than it has earned, and the "three independent layers" framing actively obscures how much weight sits on one unargued claim.

**The highest-value revision, if you only do one:** stop claiming three independent grounding layers and defend the one move that is actually novel and actually load-bearing — that valence realism is a claim about physical properties rather than evaluative attitudes. It needs to survive illusionism, and it needs to survive the indexical egoist. If it survives both, the system stands on one pillar and doesn't need three. If it doesn't survive, no amount of layering rescues it.

**Ranked revision list:**

1. Close the egoism→impartiality gap with an argument, not a burden shift (§3.3).
2. Defend valence realism directly against illusionism; drop or heavily qualify the independence claim (§3.1, §3.2).
3. Derive or axiomatize the five non-hedonic value dimensions, and specify an aggregation rule (§3.4).
4. Add citations and a bibliography (§4).
5. Fix the off-by-one cross-references (§4).
6. Inline the Position Registry or remove the claim (§4).
7. Add the false-negative analysis to Mode B, calibrated by domain (§3.6).
8. Narrow the novelty claim to procedural gating; add Hare, Parfit, Korsgaard (§3.7, §3.10).
9. Add a second diagnostic axis for coordinated predation alongside Moloch (§3.5).
10. Downgrade confidence on the contested empirical claims, minimum wage first (§3.9).
11. Rewrite Oath clause VII to require adversarial rather than reinforcing review (§3.11).
12. Cut 25–30% (§4).
