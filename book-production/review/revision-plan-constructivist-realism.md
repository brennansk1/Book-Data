# Revising *Harmonious Rationality* for Maximum Strength

**Goal state:** a framework that a metaethicist at a research department would treat as a live position rather than an amateur synthesis, that survives adversarial refereeing, and that a reader who disagrees politically can still adopt.

The central problem is not that the arguments are bad. It's that the book is **structurally miscast**: it presents itself as a foundationalist system (one axiom → everything) while running on coherentist machinery, and it claims realism it hasn't cashed out while its actual content is compatible with a far more defensible position. Nearly every fix below follows from correcting that mismatch.

---

## Move 1 — Pick a metaethical position by name and defend it

"Constructivist Realism" currently straddles four distinct positions in the literature: Cornell realism (Boyd, Brink), Railton-style naturalist reductionism, Humean constructivism (Street), and quasi-realism (Blackburn). A professional reader identifies this in the first ten pages and stops taking the label seriously. Worse, the name is already owned — **Korsgaard's Kantian constructivism** and **Street's Humean constructivism** are the two things "constructivism" means in metaethics, and both reach conclusions this book rejects.

The strongest available position, and the one the book is actually reaching for:

**Phenomenal value realism.** The badness of suffering is not a further fact appended to the phenomenology; it is constitutive of it. What it is to be in pain is to be in a state whose felt character is bad. This makes moral epistemology partly a matter of introspective acquaintance rather than inference from evolved attitudes.

This is a real, live, defended position. The literature to engage and build on:

- **Sinhababu**, *Humean Nature* — the phenomenal introspection argument. This is the closest thing to a rigorous version of what your book asserts.
- **Kahane**, "Pain, Dislike and Experience" — on whether pain's badness is intrinsic or attitudinal.
- **Bramble**, "The Distinctive Feeling Theory of Pleasure."
- **Chalmers** on the epistemic status of introspective acquaintance.
- **Crisp**, *Reasons and the Good* — hedonism defended at professional standard.

Why this position is strategically right for your framework: it **immunizes against Street's Darwinian Dilemma properly**, which the current thermometer analogy does not. Street targets evolved *evaluative attitudes*. If badness is a phenomenal property and moral epistemology is acquaintance-based, the dilemma misses — not because evolution built a reliable tracker, but because there's no tracking relation of the debunkable kind. That's a genuine answer instead of a deflection.

Objections you must handle explicitly:

| Objection | Source | Required response |
|---|---|---|
| Queerness — intrinsically prescriptive properties don't exist | Mackie | Phenomenal consciousness already violates Mackie's ontological expectations independently; the queerness argument was a priori and the a priori has a bad record here |
| Open question — "it feels bad, but is it *bad*?" | Moore | OQA fails against a posteriori identity claims (water/H₂O); this is that kind of claim, not a conceptual analysis |
| Illusionism — no phenomenal properties, so no phenomenal value | Frankish, Dennett | See Move 2 |
| Attitudinal theories — badness is the *dislike*, not the sensation | Heathwood, Feldman | Concede this is live; show the framework survives on either reading |

### Move 2 — Build a graceful-degradation tier instead of "three independent layers"

Replace the three-pillar architecture (which doesn't work — the layers share a premise and fall to one objection) with a **conditional cascade**. This converts your worst vulnerability into a robustness claim, and it's honest:

- **Tier 1 — phenomenal realism true.** The framework is a naturalist realist theory grounded in the intrinsic badness of suffering. Full strength.
- **Tier 2 — illusionism true, representationalism about valence.** The relevant property is representational content that is intrinsically avoidance-generating. Weaker metaphysics; identical practical content.
- **Tier 3 — strong anti-realism true.** The framework degrades into Blackburn-style quasi-realism plus a coordination theory. You lose "constrained by objective reality"; you keep every normative and institutional conclusion in Books III–VII.

The rhetorical move: *the framework's practical content is invariant across the entire live space of metaethical positions.* That is a much better claim than "our foundation is triple-secured," it's true, and it's the kind of thing referees respect. It also directly serves the wide-appeal goal — a Christian, a Buddhist, and an error theorist can all use Books III–VII without settling Chapter 3.

## Move 3 — Derive impartiality from the coordination layer, not from the axiom

This is the most valuable single change available to you, because you already own the machinery and aren't using it.

The current route — "your suffering matters to you, so you owe a principled reason why others' doesn't" — is a burden shift, and the indexical egoist has the reply ready (Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason; Parfit spent two volumes on it). Don't try to win that fight on metaethical ground.

**Instead, split the derivation across your two foundations:**

1. **The axiom supplies the currency.** Valence is what has value. This is all Chapter 3 needs to establish.
2. **Book II supplies the impartiality.** Agents facing an indefinitely iterated game, uncertain of their future bargaining position, converge on norms that count valence impartially — because partial norms are not stable equilibria among agents who cannot guarantee they'll stay on the favored side. This is Gauthier/Binmore/Skyrms territory, and your version dodges Gauthier's fatal problem (why should the powerful bargain?) precisely because you have a *value* base rather than a preference-satisfaction base.

Then patch the remaining gap — beings who cannot reciprocate (infants, animals, the severely disabled, future people) — with a distinct and, as far as I can tell, genuinely novel argument you should claim as your own:

> **Arbitrary moral-circle boundaries are unstable Schelling points.** Once valence is conceded as the currency, every boundary short of "all valence" requires a defensible line. Every historically proposed line — tribe, race, nation, sex, species, cognitive threshold — has proven unstable under pressure, because none is a natural focal point and each is exploitable by anyone with an interest in redrawing it. "All valence" is the only Schelling point that doesn't erode. Circle expansion is therefore not moral fashion; it is equilibrium selection.

That is a coordination-theoretic argument for the expanding moral circle, built entirely out of parts you already have, that answers the problem the burden-shift fails to answer. It's publishable on its own.

## Move 4 — Replace the value vector with a specified aggregation structure

The six-dimensional vector is currently doing three jobs (blocking utility monster, repugnant conclusion, transplant case) on no derivation, and it has no aggregation rule, so it doesn't actually yield verdicts.

**Recommended structure:**

**Value monism, goods pluralism.** Only valence is intrinsically valuable. Autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, and meaning are *robustly necessary conditions* of sustained positive valence in beings constituted like us — empirically load-bearing, not axiomatically independent. This preserves the minimal-axiom claim, which is one of your genuine selling points, and it's defensible: Self-Determination Theory gives you the empirical backing for the first three.

Then do the anti-aggregation work properly, with named machinery rather than with vector geometry:

- **Prioritarianism** — moral weight of a valence increment increases as the recipient's baseline falls. (Parfit, "Equality or Priority?"; Arneson.) Blocks the utility monster without any appeal to incommensurability, because the monster's marginal gains are weighted down.
- **A sufficientarian threshold** — the capability floor you already endorse, now formally specified as lexically prior to aggregate maximization below the line. (Crisp; Nussbaum's threshold structure; Frankfurt.)
- **Separateness-of-persons constraints** — the transplant case is blocked by a rights structure derived from coordination stability, not by welfare arithmetic. You already argue this in Ch. 19; make it the official answer.
- **Repugnant conclusion** — pick one and defend it: person-affecting restriction, critical-level view, or bite the bullet (Huemer, Tännsjö). Don't leave it to the vector. Population ethics is where reviewers will probe hardest, because it's where every welfarist theory bleeds.

Net effect: you go from "welfare is a vector, evaluate holistically" — which is not a decision procedure — to a specified structure that produces determinate verdicts and cites the literature that already defends each piece.

## Move 5 — Declare the theory coherentist and stop apologizing for it

You already run on wide reflective equilibrium. Say so in Chapter 1. The axiom becomes "the most stable node in the web," which is what Layer 1 literally argues.

Costs: "one axiom" sounds cleaner in a preface. Benefits: you stop making a claim (three independent pillars) that a referee will dismantle in a paragraph, you inherit a well-developed defense (Sayre-McCord, DePaul, Daniels on wide RE), and your error-correction architecture — Position Registry, revisability — becomes *entailed by* your epistemology rather than bolted on.

Foundationalist framing is also what forces the fake-independence move in the first place. Drop the framing, the problem dissolves.

## Move 6 — Two-axis diagnosis: Moloch is half the enemy

Book II's toolkit doesn't map onto Book II's diagnosis. Fix it by splitting the diagnosis:

- **Axis 1 — Coordination failure (Moloch).** Individually rational choices, collectively catastrophic. Tool: mechanism design.
- **Axis 2 — Circle failure.** Successful coordination *against* excluded parties. Slavery, genocide, cartels, conquest are coordination *victories* for the in-group. Tool: rights, constitutional constraint, circle-expansion arguments. Mechanism design here makes things *worse* — better incentive alignment among perpetrators intensifies the harm.
- **Axis 3 — Epistemic failure.** Suffering from false belief, independent of both. Tool: the epistemic commons, free speech, prediction markets, institutional truth-seeking.

Now every instrument in the book has a diagnostic home, and Ch. 21's free-speech material stops looking like a political preference and starts looking like a derivation. This also gives Book II a much stronger originality claim: a *typology* of suffering-generators with matched interventions is a contribution; "coordination failure explains most suffering" is a restatement of Scott Alexander.

## Move 7 — Mode B: add the false-negative side and role relativity

Two additions:

**A duty to override.** Currently all five conditions and three gates filter false positives. Add the symmetric analysis: expected cost of wrongful compliance vs. wrongful override, calibrated by domain. In private life with reversible stakes, heavy anti-override bias is correct. Inside a captured institution facing irreversible harm, it inverts. Milgram, My Lai, and every institutional cover-off are false-negative failures; your framework currently has nothing to say to the person sitting in one.

**Role-relative thresholds.** A physician, a soldier, a civil servant, and a private citizen have different override profiles, because the rules they're bound by have different justificatory bases and different capture risks. Make this explicit rather than leaving one calibration to cover everyone.

**Fix the whistleblower contradiction.** Gate 1(c) opens Mode B for institutional capture; Gates 2–3 then impose delay and documented review in the exact case where delay forecloses the opportunity and documentation endangers the agent. Allow sealed or delayed documentation, and exempt Gate 1(c) from the deliberation requirement where the capture is itself time-sensitive.

Then engage the two rivals who own this terrain and are currently absent: **Hare's two-level utilitarianism** (structurally your nearest neighbor — the intuitive/critical distinction is Mode A/Mode B) and **Dancy's particularism** (a direct attack on the whole rules architecture).

## Move 8 — Quarantine the policy

Move every specific policy commitment — EITC vs. minimum wage, school choice, carbon dividends, zoning, originalism — out of the core and into a clearly separated applied volume, each tagged with the contested empirical premises it depends on and a confidence rating that reflects the actual state of the literature rather than your priors.

This does three things at once:

1. **Protects the core.** A reader who thinks you're wrong about the minimum wage currently discards the metaethics too.
2. **Delivers the wide appeal you want.** The framework becomes something a social democrat and a libertarian can both adopt while disagreeing downstream — which is the actual test of a non-ideological method.
3. **Makes the "derived, not imported" claim honest.** Error-correction primacy and subsidiarity *are* derived. The policy bundle isn't, and pretending otherwise is the book's most visible credibility leak.

**Then add the demonstration the book most needs and doesn't have:** a worked case in which two readers apply the seven-step method faithfully and *diverge*, because they hold different defensible empirical priors — followed by a specification of what evidence would settle it. Nothing else you could write would do as much to establish that this is a method rather than a conclusion with a method attached.

Concretely: downgrade the minimum-wage claim first. It's stated flatly and the post-Card–Krueger literature doesn't support that confidence. It's also the clearest instance of your own Chapter 2 diagnosis operating on you, which a hostile reviewer will enjoy pointing out.

## Move 9 — Rebuild the rival roster to professional standard

Currently nine chapters, all against Western systems, with non-Western traditions appearing only as donors in Ch. 46. Add as full adversarial chapters:

**Mandatory:**
- **Korsgaard** — Kantian constructivism. You cannot use the word "constructivist" in your title and not engage her.
- **Street** — as a *rival constructivist*, not just as the source of a debunking argument.
- **Parfit**, *On What Matters* — the Triple Theory and the convergence thesis, which is the argument you're already making in the Modal Utilitarian section without attribution.
- **Williams** — integrity, internal reasons, and the critique of systematizing ethics as such. This is the deepest threat to the entire enterprise and deserves the longest chapter in Book VIII.
- **Hare** — your nearest structural neighbor (see Move 7).
- **Scanlon** — contractualism proper, not as a borrowed tool.

**High value:**
- **Dancy** — particularism vs. the rules architecture.
- **Nietzsche** as a genuine rival, not scene-setting for the "ruins."
- **Buddhist anātman ethics** — this is the sharpest non-Western challenge available to you and it's *specifically* dangerous to a valence-grounded theory: if there is no persistent subject, whose suffering matters, and across what boundaries does aggregation run? Parfit's reductionism about personal identity makes the same attack from inside the analytic tradition. Handling both together would be one of the strongest chapters in the book.
- **Confucian role ethics** (Ames & Rosemont) — a genuine competitor with a different answer to the relational-self question, currently mined for parts.
- **Care ethics** (Held, Noddings) — as a rival, not a donor.

The donor-vs-opponent asymmetry is the kind of thing that reads as condescension whether or not it's meant that way. Fixing it costs you little and buys real credibility.

## Move 10 — Epistemic hygiene retrofit

- **Full citation apparatus.** Notes, page numbers, bibliography. Non-negotiable for the audience you want; currently zero.
- **Inline the Position Registry** with vulnerability ratings and standing rebuttals, or delete every claim about it. Advertising a self-correction engine that lives in an external file is the single most damaging credibility item in the book.
- **"What would change my mind"** for each STRONG and MODERATE position. One or two sentences. This is cheap and it's the most persuasive thing on the page.
- **Confidence intervals on empirical claims**, matched to the literature rather than to conviction.
- **Adversarial review before publication.** Send Chapter 3 to a committed illusionist and Chapter 38 to a committed error theorist, and print their strongest objections with your replies. This is the strongest possible answer to the Oath-clause-VII problem (an error-correction mechanism staffed by co-adherents corrects only within shared priors) and it converts your biggest sociological liability into a demonstrated virtue.
- **Pre-register predictions.** "Morality is coordination technology" generates testable claims about which norms appear in which ecological and institutional conditions. Stating them in advance is what separates a framework from a story.

## Move 11 — Split the book

One 1,325-page volume serves no audience well.

- **Volume I — *The Theory*** (~200 pp). Metaethics, the two-axis diagnosis, the decision architecture, the aggregation structure. Aimed at professionals. This is the volume that gets refereed and cited.
- **Volume II — *Applications*** (~250 pp). Politics, economics, frontier problems, with empirical premises and confidence ratings exposed.
- **Volume III — *The Practice*** (~150 pp). Personal ethics, virtues, development, the Oath. Reframed explicitly as an optional practice with an adversarial-review requirement built in.

The Oath and the community material are actively harmful to professional reception in the current arrangement — they read as movement-building rather than philosophy. Isolated in Volume III and labeled as a practice, they're fine and even attractive to a general audience.

## What to claim as the contribution

The book currently buries four genuinely novel or near-novel moves under several hundred pages of competent restatement. State them up front, in this order:

1. **Procedurally gated dual-process ethics.** Bright-line triggers, mandatory deliberation, post-hoc review. Nobody else has operationalized the override this way.
2. **The two-axis diagnosis** — coordination failure vs. circle failure vs. epistemic failure, with matched interventions.
3. **The coordination-theoretic derivation of impartiality** — the axiom supplies the currency, the iterated game supplies the impartiality.
4. **The instability-of-arbitrary-boundaries argument** for moral circle expansion as equilibrium selection.

Four contributions is more than most books have. The current framing — "a comprehensive moral framework that gets more right than any existing alternative" — invites the reviewer to test the weakest claim in the book. The framing above invites them to test the strongest.
