1. The duel's date, hour, and location — Saturday, 21 March 1829, 8 a.m., Battersea Fields, Surrey.
   Wikipedia, "Wellington–Winchilsea duel"; British History on This Day; King's College London's own
   "Duel Day" feature. Verified per `reviews/ch-04/verify.md` item 1 (21 March 1829 was in fact a
   Saturday).

2. Wellington as sitting Prime Minister at the time of the duel. Undisputed across all sources
   consulted. Verified per verify.md item 2.

3. Winchilsea's letter in *The Standard*, 14 March 1829, quoted directly: "an insidious design for
   the infringement of our liberties and the introduction of Popery into every department of the
   State." Exact phrase converges verbatim across Wikipedia, the King's College London archives, and
   the Woolley & Wallis auction house's account (drawing on its own Finch-Hatton family manuscript
   holdings). Verified per verify.md item 3.

4. Wellington's retraction demand and Winchilsea's public refusal (Winchilsea privately conceded he
   had overstepped). Verified per verify.md item 4.

5. Seconds: Sir Henry Hardinge for Wellington; Edward Boscawen, 1st Earl of Falmouth, for
   Winchilsea. Confirmed by name across sources. Verified per verify.md item 5.

6. Twelve paces. Converged across all sources. Verified per verify.md item 6.

7. Wellington fired first and missed. Undisputed. Verified per verify.md item 7.

8. The fire-first ambiguity, as the chapter presents it — Wellington's own claim of a deliberate
   miss against his reputation as a poor shot. Verified per verify.md item 8: one source
   (utterlyinteresting.com, via a physician's account) reports that some observers sympathetic to
   Winchilsea believed Wellington had aimed to kill and missed through incompetence — i.e., the
   record supports an even more contested reading than the chapter states, so the chapter is if
   anything conservative here.

9. Winchilsea deloping — raising his pistol and firing into the air, prearranged with his second.
   Confirmed by Woolley & Wallis and the utterlyinteresting.com physician's account. Verified per
   verify.md item 9 (PASS with a note: one early secondary snippet encountered this session gave a
   garbled account implying Winchilsea never fired at all; the fuller sources resolve this as
   fire-into-the-air, matching the audio text).

10. The written apology withdrawing "disgraceful and criminal motives," rendered in the audio text
    with its actual hedge intact ("as the motives the Duke stated he had been charged with"). The
    period text itself: "I do not hesitate to declare, of my own accord, that, in apology, I regret
    having unadvisedly published an opinion which the Duke states to have charged him with
    disgraceful and criminal motives in a certain transaction." **PASS-WITH-NOTE**, per verify.md
    item 10 — the apology's own wording hedges rather than flatly owning the retraction; the audio
    text's phrasing ("hedged, in the actual wording...") reflects the Verifier's suggested softening
    rather than an earlier draft's "without qualification," which slightly overstated the apology's
    directness.

11. **WEDDING DETAIL — single-sourced, flagged in the text itself.** Wellington attended
    Winchilsea's 1834 wedding to Emily Bagot, Wellington's own great-niece. PASS per verify.md item
    11 (Wikipedia confirms the fact), but sourced to Wikipedia alone and not independently
    cross-checked this session — the audio script accordingly hedges it in the narrator's own voice
    ("The detail comes from a single source I couldn't independently confirm, so hold it loosely"),
    which the Verifier calls "appropriately cautious even though the underlying fact checks out."

12. Dueling was illegal in England; no scandal in 1829 large enough to end Wellington's career or
    standing. Consistent with the record — Wellington faced no prosecution and his standing was, if
    anything, strengthened. Verified per verify.md item 12.

13. "No credit bureau" — no credit-reporting institution existed in Britain in 1829 in any form
    resembling a modern bureau. Verified per verify.md item 13.

14. "No enforceable contract between two gentlemen who'd shaken hands on a debt" — gaming and
    wagering debts were not legally enforceable in English courts in this period (formalized later
    by the Gaming Act 1845, but the underlying non-enforceability of debts of honor at law predates
    it). Verified per verify.md item 14.

15. "No real police interest in what happened between men of a certain class, provided nobody
    important got hurt" — consistent with the historical record and directly demonstrated by the
    duel itself: a sitting Prime Minister committed an illegal act in public and faced no police or
    prosecutorial consequence. Verified per verify.md item 15.

16. That the credit/collateral passage reads as flagged authorial speculation rather than a sourced
    claim. This is the chapter's Gate 3 item: the passage's load-bearing mechanism — reading the
    duel as a credit-and-collateral signal — was found during review to be synthesized during
    drafting and attributed to nobody (`reviews/ch-04/decisions.md`, Gate 3). The binding resolution
    was to own it explicitly as the author's own conjecture rather than source it or cut it. Verified
    per verify.md item 16: the passage names no source for the inference itself and labels itself "a
    guess of my own... weight it accordingly." The audio edit adds one further verbal closing marker
    not present in the print text ("That's the whole guess. What comes next is back to the record.")
    so a listener without visible paragraph breaks can hear exactly where the speculation ends —
    consistent with `AUDIO_SPEC.md`'s allowance for added signposting around a flagged conjecture.

17. The ridicule mechanism, as characterized. Davis's *New York Review of Books* review confirms
    the direct Appiah quotation "ridicule at last did more than morality to kill dueling," and
    independent reviews (*Philosophical Quarterly*) confirm the exclusivity mechanism (dueling
    stopped signaling gentlemanly membership once it spread down the social scale) as Appiah's
    actual argument. Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen* (New York:
    W. W. Norton, 2010). The chapter paraphrases rather than quotes Appiah directly, since no primary
    text was available to either the Researcher or the Verifier this session. Verified per verify.md
    item 17.

18. That Appiah's book runs the same argument through footbinding among upper-class Chinese women
    and the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade. **PASS-WITH-NOTE**, per verify.md item 18 —
    accurate as stated; noted for completeness that the book has a fourth case (honor killing in
    contemporary Pakistan) which Appiah treats differently in kind, as an open test of the theory
    rather than a completed "moral revolution." The audio text doesn't need or mention the fourth
    case.

19. The "moral revolutions" framing — Appiah's own book subtitle. Verified per verify.md item 19.

20. The roughly-1850s end date for English dueling. **PASS-WITH-NOTE**, per verify.md item 20:
    individually-dated facts converge cleanly (army banned dueling among officers 1844; last fatal
    English-vs-English duel on English soil, Hawkey–Seton, 20 May 1845; last duel of any kind on
    English soil, Cournet–Barthélemy, 1852), but "by the 1850s the whole apparatus is essentially
    finished" is a fair characterization of that span rather than a precise sourced statistic — which
    is how the audio text treats it.

21. "Two centuries of sermons." **PASS-WITH-NOTE**, per verify.md item 21 — rhetorical scene-setting,
    not a citable statistic, and not presented as one. English anti-dueling proclamations and church
    opposition trace back to at least the early seventeenth century (arguably to Elizabeth I's 1571
    condemnation), so "two centuries" before 1829 is a defensible approximation; no single source was
    found this session stating "two centuries" as an agreed figure, but none contradicts it either.

22. The David Brion Davis attribution. Verified per verify.md item 22, flag resolved: Davis's NYRB
    review confirms his monocausality critique and the "ridicule... did more than morality" quotation
    are explicitly about the dueling material, not the footbinding or slave-trade chapters — his own
    words: "while it is clear that major changes in social class were a prerequisite for dissolving
    the honor code that maintained British dueling... I fail to see either the irrelevance of moral
    argument or the centrality and persistence of a new code of honor." One correction surfaced to
    the Researcher's packet (not the manuscript): a Cardinal Newman detail the packet attributed to
    Davis's review does not appear in the review as fetched. This never reached the manuscript, so no
    fix was needed in the text.

23. State monopoly on violence as a rival explanation for dueling's decline. A real, recognized
    argument in the literature (Norbert Elias's "civilizing process" scholarship applies this
    directly to dueling). Verified per verify.md item 23.

24. Officer-class composition as a rival explanation. Confirmed as a real argument in the
    literature — a shifting, increasingly bourgeois officer corps is cited as a factor in dueling's
    decline in East India Company and Continental contexts. Verified per verify.md item 24.

25. Legal and insurance alternatives to honor remedies, as rivals the narrator says he "hasn't read
    carefully enough to weigh." **PASS-WITH-NOTE**, per verify.md item 25 — the legal half is well
    confirmed (libel actions functioned as an alternative remedy for the rising middle class); the
    insurance half was not independently confirmed this session (no source located specifically
    connecting life/liability insurance to dueling's English decline, though it appears in some
    Continental duelling historiography). The audio text's hedge already covers this and does not
    overclaim.

26. A newly aggressive press as a rival explanation. Confirmed — contemporary sources describe the
    growing print media of the period publicizing and reshaping honor disputes. Verified per
    verify.md item 26.

27. The Maghribi coalition mechanism — a closed network, agents drawn from within the group,
    multilateral reputation punishment, courts playing a minor role relative to reputation. Avner
    Greif, "Reputation and Coalitions in Medieval Trade: Evidence on the Maghribi Traders," *Journal
    of Economic History* 49, no. 4 (1989): 857–882; Greif, "Contract Enforceability and Economic
    Institutions in Early Trade: The Maghribi Traders' Coalition," *American Economic Review* 83, no.
    3 (1993): 525–548. Confirmed directly against Greif's own papers. Verified per verify.md item 27.

28. The Palermo/Cairo cross-Mediterranean reputational consequence, as narrated ("Cheat a man in
    Palermo and you'd lose your livelihood in Cairo"). Not a quotation — a fair compression of
    Greif's documented example: a circa-1050 letter from an agent in Palermo to a merchant in
    Fustat/Cairo shows exactly this kind of consequence in the correspondence Greif studied. Verified
    per verify.md item 28.

29. The Edwards–Ogilvie dispute, as hedged in the text ("at least on his telling"). Jeremy Edwards
    and Sheilagh Ogilvie's published critique (CESifo Working Paper No. 2254, 2008; Cambridge Working
    Papers in Economics No. 0928, 2009; *Economic History Review*, 2012) argues Greif overstates how
    much enforcement ran through reputation versus courts; their position shifted across the three
    iterations toward acknowledging reputation mechanisms operated "alongside" legal ones. Verified
    per verify.md item 29: directly located Edwards and Ogilvie's own claim ("not a single empirical
    example adduced by Greif shows that any 'coalition' actually existed") independent of Greif's
    summary — firmer sourcing than the Researcher's packet had. The chapter's hedge ("at least on his
    telling") is doing real, necessary work, since this session's own read of Greif's rebuttal did not
    find as clean a concession from Edwards/Ogilvie as Greif's characterization implies.

30. The reverse-dominance claim, attributed to the 1993 paper only (the 1999 book is never invoked
    for anything specific). Christopher Boehm, "Egalitarian Behavior and Reverse Dominance
    Hierarchy," *Current Anthropology* 34, no. 3 (June 1993): 227–254. The term "reverse dominance
    hierarchy," the four-stage escalation (ridicule/criticism, noncompliance, ostracism, execution by
    consensus), and the "political achievement, not a natural state" framing are all confirmed
    directly from the 1993 paper. Verified per verify.md item 30.

31. The ethnographic societies cited (San groups in southern Africa, the Hadza in East Africa,
    Australian Aboriginal bands) and the chapter's own hedge about post-contact field reports.
    **PASS-WITH-NOTE**, per verify.md item 31 — the Hadza and Australian Aboriginal examples are
    directly confirmed in secondary treatments of the 1993 paper; the San/Bushmen example was not
    independently re-confirmed against the paper itself this session (time-limited search), though it
    is the single most commonly cited society in this literature. The chapter's own hedge ("I'm less
    sure than that summary sounds") is honest regardless of the San detail specifically.

32. The Mensur civil-service statistic, confirmed absent from the text. The audio script explicitly
    says the narrator found the specific figure in only one place and is leaving it out; no number
    appears anywhere in the passage. Verified per verify.md item 32. For the record, not a correction:
    the statistic itself — roughly one-fifth of senior Prussian civil-service positions held by former
    Kösener-federation fraternity members by 1928 — is in fact cross-checkable across at least two
    independent sources found this session, so it is not actually single-sourced if a later revision
    wants it; the current text is right to have left it out absent that check at drafting time.

33. Mensur's origin, "since the early nineteenth century." Sources place the practice's development
    across the late eighteenth into the nineteenth century, with the nineteenth century being when it
    became institutionalized and widespread. Verified per verify.md item 33.

34. General description of the Mensur — sharpened blades, face and scalp unprotected, a referee,
    first blood, the *Schmiss*. Matches all sources consulted. Verified per verify.md item 34.

35. Cairo Geniza background — the Ben Ezra Synagogue, Solomon Schechter's 1896–97 expedition, an
    estimated two hundred thousand fragments, S. D. Goitein's foundational scholarly work turning the
    collection into usable history. Well-established, uncontested historical fact, consistent across
    every source touched this session. Verified per verify.md item 35.

36. Greif's own underlying citations (*Journal of Economic History*, 1989; *American Economic
    Review*, 1993) — see item 27 above for the full citations; not separately restated here since
    verify.md notes nothing in the manuscript prose itself carries an in-text citation number to check
