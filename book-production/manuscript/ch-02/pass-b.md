Marisol —

You told me something outside courtroom 4B last month that I haven't been able to put down. Twenty minutes before a hearing, eating a granola bar you'd apparently found in a coat pocket, you mentioned that the ADA you've been sparring with for six years brought you coffee that morning, because you'd both had the same miserable week. You said it like you'd been caught doing something a little embarrassing. I want to try to talk you out of the embarrassment. Not out of the coffee.

Here's the claim, plainly, before I earn it: cooperation doesn't require anyone to be good. It requires the expectation of meeting again. And that expectation is not a fact about human nature you either have or don't. It's something you can build, on purpose, with people who have every reason to hate you.

The best evidence I know of is a chess tournament that wasn't about chess. In the late 1970s a political scientist named Robert Axelrod, at Michigan, got curious about a specific question: when should you cooperate, and when should you be selfish, in a relationship that doesn't end after one round. Instead of writing another paper about it, he ran an experiment: invited game theorists to submit computer programs to play the Prisoner's Dilemma against each other, round robin, two hundred moves a game. Fourteen entries came in, from psychology, economics, political science, math, sociology. One asked to remain anonymous. That program came in second to last.

The winner was four lines long. Anatol Rapoport, a psychologist at Toronto, submitted a program called TIT FOR TAT: cooperate on the first move, then do whatever the other player did last time. That's the whole program. It beat entries built by people who'd modeled their opponent's psychology and calculated expected payoffs, entries that assumed the worst going in and got punished for it.

Then Axelrod ran it again, publicly, after telling everyone exactly what had won and why. Sixty-two entries this time, six countries. Everyone had read the report. Everyone knew TIT FOR TAT existed and was free to just submit a copy of it; the rules explicitly allowed that. Exactly one person did: Rapoport, again. Everybody else tried to beat it instead. It won again.

I keep sitting with that. It's the opposite of a story about a clever trick nobody could reverse-engineer. The mechanism was published, explained, and handed to sixty-one people who wanted to beat it, and the honest four-line answer still won, because the thing doing the work wasn't cleverness.

What was doing the work, in Axelrod's own accounting, was a bundle of four properties, and they only make sense together. TIT FOR TAT was nice: never the first to defect, which kept it out of fights that didn't need to happen (p. 33). Niceness alone gets you eaten, so it was also retaliatory, punishing an unprovoked defection immediately, no waiting, no strategic patience (p. 44). Retaliation alone traps you in a feud forever, so it was forgiving too: one punishment, then back to cooperating, letting the other player earn its way back rather than paying for one bad move indefinitely (p. 36; a rule called FRIEDMAN never forgave anything and scored worse for it). None of the first three matter if the other side can't read what you're doing, which is the fourth property. TIT FOR TAT was clear, legible, easy to notice, easy to trust once recognized (pp. 53–54). Axelrod's own summary of the bundle: "its niceness prevents it from getting into unnecessary trouble. Its retaliation discourages the other side from persisting whenever defection is tried. Its forgiveness helps restore mutual cooperation. And its clarity makes it intelligible to the other player, thereby eliciting long-term cooperation" (p. 54).

Read that again as a description of a working relationship instead of a subroutine and it stops sounding like computer science.

Axelrod has a phrase for the mechanism underneath all of it: the shadow of the future. Cooperation holds when the future looms large enough over the present that it's worth eating a short-term loss to protect it (pp. 124–126). Shrink that shadow, make the next meeting unlikely, and the same two players who cooperated for years will defect on their way out the door. Which means the shadow isn't fixed; you can lengthen it on purpose. Axelrod's own example is almost embarrassingly ordinary: a wedding is a public ritual whose entire function is to make a relationship harder to walk away from (p. 129). You do the same thing every time you end up in front of the same judge, the same ADA, in a courthouse too small to let anyone disappear into anonymity. You're not more virtuous than a stranger passing through. You just can't get away from each other.

Now the part I actually sat down to tell you, which isn't the tournament at all. Men trying to kill each other on the Western Front built, independently, in dozens of sectors, without anyone ordering it, a working system of not killing each other. It started small: a ration party gets careless because the guns go quiet at the same hour every night, and the quiet just continues (pp. 77–78). It grew into something close to a treaty nobody signed. German snipers would put a string of shots into a cottage wall just to prove they could, not because they wanted to hurt anyone but because a side that can't demonstrate it's dangerous can't be trusted when it says it's holding back (p. 79). The British artillery did the same thing with shells, so regular that German soldiers called it something close to "the evening gun" — you could set your watch by it, and men climbed up out of the trench a little before seven just to watch it land exactly where it always landed (p. 86). They weren't being decent to each other. They were being predictable, on purpose, at real cost, because predictable was the only thing the system needed anybody to be.

One scene from inside it I can't shake. British troops jeering at a German salvo that did no damage, and a German soldier standing up on his own parapet, in range, to shout an apology: "we are very sorry about that, we hope no one was hurt, it is not our fault, it is that damned Prussian artillery" (pp. 84–85). Axelrod's own gloss is that this goes past strategy into something closer to moral regret, and I think he's right, and I don't think it undoes anything I've told you. Something that starts as bare incentive can still become more than that, given enough repetition between the same two sides. I'm not chasing that thread here — there's a whole further argument in the ecological data, generations of copied strategies eating each other's lunch, and I'm leaving it alone.

It ended from above, by people who couldn't see what they were breaking.

Headquarters couldn't tell whether ordinary shelling was real aggression or theater, so they invented something they could verify: the raid, ten to two hundred men sent to kill or capture in the enemy's own trench, producing prisoners or bodies that couldn't be faked (p. 82). You cannot fake a raid the way you can fake a near miss. Once raids were ordered and monitored from headquarters instead of managed locally, nobody nearby could quietly de-escalate the retaliation the way they'd de-escalated everything else for years. One order removed the thing that had built the whole arrangement: repeated contact between people free to adjust to each other.

So. Is this just self-interest with a longer memory? You'll ask that, and it isn't an unreasonable question. I want to answer it straight instead of talking around it, because you'll smell it if I dodge. The game theory shows why cooperative behavior is stable: why niceness and forgiveness and legibility survive and spread even among people who owe each other nothing. It does not show that those things are right. It explains the mechanics of a fact; it doesn't tell you the fact is good. That gap is real, I'm not papering over it, and it's large enough that a later part of this book exists mostly to try to climb into it. I'm not resolving it in a letter.

TIT FOR TAT has a flaw, and it isn't small. Axelrod's tournaments ran on perfect information, no static, no misread signals. Real life has noise: you send a cooperative move and it arrives looking like a defection, or the other side just makes a mistake. Under noise, plain TIT FOR TAT is fragile, and one accidental defection can set off an echo of retaliation neither side wanted, both of you locked into punishing an error nobody committed on purpose. Researchers who picked this up after Axelrod, Nowak and Sigmund, found that the fix isn't retaliating more carefully. It's a fixed, small dose of unearned forgiveness: cooperate anyway, sometimes, after a defection, even when you can't tell whether it was deliberate. That isn't a patch bolted on from outside. It's the forgiving property, taken more seriously than the original tournament had reason to take it.

I found this result in my twenties and used it to justify something close to permanent retaliation against a friend who'd genuinely wronged me once, told myself I was being principled, and was in fact just being unforgiving. I hadn't finished reading the book. Axelrod's own numbers would have told me flatly that permanent retaliation loses. Not to the nice programs. To almost everything.

You didn't do anything shameful with that coffee. You did the oldest trick there is, in a building specifically engineered to keep making you and that ADA meet again, and it works because he learned the trick too. That's not romance. It's closer to plumbing. But it's yours, and it's real, and I don't think you should give the credit away to some theory of human goodness neither of you needs.

— B.
