On the evening of January 27, 1986, engineers at Morton Thiokol's plant in Brigham City, Utah, gathered around a speakerphone in a windowless conference room. Patched in on the other end were NASA managers at Marshall Space Flight Center in Alabama, among them Lawrence Mulloy and George Hardy, preparing for the next morning's launch of the space shuttle Challenger from Kennedy Space Center in Florida. The forecast for the Cape that night called for temperatures near freezing — colder than any previous shuttle launch. No shuttle had ever flown below 53 degrees Fahrenheit.

Six months earlier, on July 31, 1985, Thiokol seal engineer Roger Boisjoly had written to Robert Lund, the company's vice president of engineering, warning that erosion in the solid rocket boosters' O-rings could lead to "a catastrophe of the highest order — loss of human life," and asking that a team be assigned to the problem before "we stand in front of a Board of Inquiry." The memo went into the file.

On the call that night, Thiokol's engineers — Boisjoly among them, along with Arnie Thompson — laid out O-ring erosion data from earlier flights and recommended against launching below 53 degrees. It was the temperature floor for every flight the shuttle had made. Lawrence Mulloy, NASA's project manager for the solid rocket boosters, pushed back hard. According to later testimony, he asked Thiokol when it wanted him to launch — next April?

Thiokol's managers asked to go off-line and caucus privately, away from NASA on the phone. They said they needed five minutes. It ran close to thirty. No new engineering data was gathered in that half hour, in that room, on that phone line. According to testimony given afterward, Jerry Mason, Thiokol's senior vice president, turned to Lund and told him to take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat.

Lund changed his vote.

Boisjoly later described the moment this way: "At that moment I felt totally helpless and felt that further argument was fruitless, so I, too, stopped pressing my case."

When the caucus ended, the room rejoined the call. Four senior Thiokol managers — Mason, Lund, Joe Kilminster, and Cal Wiggins — gave NASA a verbal recommendation to launch. The engineers who had argued against it, Boisjoly and Thompson among them, were not asked to vote.

Challenger lifted off from Kennedy Space Center at 11:38 the next morning, January 28, 1986. Seventy-three seconds later, it broke apart. All seven crew members were killed.
