Priya —

You told me about the Wednesday-night code over coffee that had gone cold before you finished the story, and I've been chewing on it since, so forgive the length here. I know you're going to read this at eleven p.m. between charting and actual sleep, but I want to get it down while it's still shaped like an argument and not just a feeling.

Here's the thing that's been bothering me. You said, about overriding the DNR discussion with that family, "I knew it was the right call in the moment." And I believe you. That's exactly the problem. Everyone who has ever done something catastrophic in the name of an exception also knew it was the right call in the moment. Mulloy knew it. The Thiokol managers who took off their engineering hats and put on their management hats knew it, or told themselves they did, in a room that had been arguing for hours and wanted to stop arguing. The feeling of being obviously right does not discriminate between the people who are and the people who are about to end up in a Rogers Commission transcript. You already know the Challenger story, so I won't retell it — I just want you to notice that "I knew it was the right call" is not evidence. It's what everyone says. It's what everyone says right before and right after, which are the only two times anyone says anything.

So if you can't trust your own judgment about when you're entitled to break a rule — and I don't think you can, I don't think I can, I don't think anyone competent enough to be dangerous can — then the fix people usually reach for is a better test. A sharper definition of catastrophic. A clearer sense of what counts as last resort. More rigor in the moment of deciding. And I used to think that was the answer too, until I noticed that the moment of deciding is exactly the moment you can't trust, so refining the test just gives you a better instrument pointed at a broken sensor. The actual fix isn't a better test. It's a procedure that runs before you're allowed to apply the test at all — something that doesn't ask "is this justified," because that question is rigged, but asks a cruder, earlier question first.

That earlier question is a trigger, and it has to be bright-line, embarrassingly bright-line, almost stupid in how little judgment it requires: is there imminent, large-scale, irreversible harm, or has the normal error-correcting machinery itself been disabled — is there, in other words, no one left upstream to appeal to. Not "this seems really bad." Not "I have a strong intuition." A threshold dumb enough that you can check it while furious, while exhausted, while certain, because those are exactly the states in which your judgment about subtler questions is worst. If the trigger doesn't clear, you don't get to the interesting part of the conversation, which is annoying and is also the entire point.

If it does clear, the second gate is a delay, and this is the part people hate most, and it's the part I'd fight hardest to keep. There's a concept from a Carnegie Mellon economist named George Loewenstein — hot-cold empathy gaps, he called it in a couple of papers in the mid-nineties and again in 2005 — describing how badly people in an emotionally "hot" state predict what a "cold" version of themselves would decide, and vice versa. You are never colder than during a code and never hotter than in the ninety seconds after. The delay isn't bureaucracy for its own sake. It's a crude technology for putting a cold version of you back in the room before the override gets executed, because the hot version is, provably, not a reliable witness to his own reasoning. And then, no matter what you decide, no matter how it turns out, there's a review — written down in advance that there will be one, not contingent on anyone catching you.

Only after all three of those does the actual substance start mattering: whether the harm was really caused by the rule and not just sitting next to it when something else went wrong, whether you actually exhausted the alternatives or just got tired of looking for them, whether you could say what you did out loud to someone with no stake in your being right, whether your own interest in this outcome has been scrubbed out or is quietly steering. None of that is worth asking until the first three have already been cleared, because a person capable of failing the substance is equally capable of failing to notice they've failed it — that's the whole diagnosis.

I can hear you already, and I want to take it seriously rather than wave it off: this is bureaucracy applied to conscience. You didn't become a doctor to run a checklist past a delay timer before you're allowed to act like a human being with functioning moral instincts. I get that, and honestly — this is the part I don't love admitting — I've used exactly this kind of procedural language before to avoid a decision I was just scared to make, dressed up caution as rigor because rigor sounds better than fear. So I'm not going to pretend the objection isn't real. What I'd say back is that the procedure isn't a substitute for your instincts, it's a guard against the specific failure mode where your instincts are worst, which is precisely when the stakes are highest and the clock is shortest — the same conditions, weirdly, that make instincts feel most trustworthy. That's not a coincidence. That's the mechanism.

Here's the harder one, though, and it's the one that actually worries me, because you'll get there before I finish the paragraph: Peter Buxtun. Buxtun found Tuskegee, went through the internal channel exactly the way you'd want a person to, filed a formal objection in 1966, got told the study wasn't finished yet, filed again in 1968, got told no again — by the review process itself, the very thing that was supposed to catch this. Six years. My gates, as I've described them, run the deliberation and documentation and review through the institution. What happens when the institution is the thing that's captured? Your objection would have gone straight to a committee stacked with the people running the study. That's not a hypothetical failure of my architecture. That's a documented one, over six years, with a body count.

So the fix has to include this, plainly: when the trigger is institutional capture — when the normal error-correcting machinery is what's disabled — the documentation gets sealed or delayed rather than routed through the captured channel, and the deliberation clock that protects you from your hot self doesn't apply, because the delay that protects against rash override is the same delay Tuskegee used to bury Buxtun for six years. And underneath that is something I'd rather not have needed to write into a chapter about restraint: sometimes the failure isn't overriding when you shouldn't. Sometimes the failure is complying — filing the correct form, through the correct channel, twice, and getting the correct rejection both times, and going home. Cynthia Cooper had an audit committee that actually worked and used it; Buxtun had the same kind of channel and it ate his objection alive. The procedure has to know the difference between those, which means it can't be one procedure with one threshold — it has to flex by role, because what a physician can appeal to and what a private citizen can appeal to and what a soldier can appeal to are structurally different animals, with different people upstream and different reasons those upstream people exist at all.

I don't have this fully solved, and I want to say that honestly rather than tie it off with something reassuring: I suspect a version of these gates would have slowed down some of the good rule-breakers of history along with all the Mulloys, and I don't have a clean way to make it slow down only the second group. I'd guess — and I'll put a real date on this so you can hold me to it — that by 2036 most hospital ethics structures will have quietly built some version of a sealed, non-routed channel precisely because Tuskegee-shaped failures keep recurring in slightly different clothes. Robert Jackall, who spent years embedded with corporate managers and is bleaker about all of this than I am, has a line I think about constantly: "What is right in the corporation is what the guy above you wants from you." I think he's too cynical, actually — I think he undersells how many people in a hierarchy are quietly building the Cooper version of that channel rather than the Buxtun version — but I understand exactly why he landed where he landed.

Anyway. Tell me about the patient, if you can without the chart in front of you. And drink something that isn't cold coffee.

— B.
