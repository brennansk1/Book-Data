I've been trying to figure out why bad things keep happening that nobody, individually, wanted. Not bad things caused by bad people — we have a whole vocabulary for that, and it's mostly the right vocabulary. I mean the other kind. The kind where you go back afterward and interview everybody involved and every single one of them was doing something defensible, sometimes something smart, and the sum of all that defensible behavior is a disaster nobody would have signed up for if you'd shown them the ending first. I used to think that was rare. I now think it's one of the main engines of the whole thing, and it has a shape you can learn to see coming, the way you can look at a foundation and know the deck is going to sag in four years even though nothing looks wrong yet.

Here's the case that finally made me sit down and write this. Northern cod, off Newfoundland. Five hundred years this fishery ran — Cabot wrote home about how thick the fish were, you could apparently slow a ship down just sailing through them. On July 2, 1992, the federal fisheries minister, a Newfoundlander named John Crosbie, announced a moratorium. Every boat, stopped. About 30,000 fishers and plant workers out of work in a single announcement, something like 12 percent of the province's entire labor force, gone at once. The day before, at a Canada Day event in a town called Bay Bulls, a crowd of maybe a thousand people who'd already heard the rumor cornered him on the wharf. There's video. Someone yells at him and he yells back, "Why are you yelling at me? I didn't take the fish from the goddamn water, so don't go abusing me." Somebody off-camera shouts, "Well, who took it then?"

I want to tell you it was the guys yelling at him. It wasn't.

That's the part I got wrong on my first pass through this, and I'm telling you because it's an admission and not a flattering one — I wanted a villain, found one that fit the shape I already believed in, and only slowed down when the actual research kept refusing to cooperate. Here's what it says instead. The small-boat inshore fishermen, the ones standing on that wharf, get substantially cleared in the peer-reviewed literature. One of the researchers who tracked this closest, a sociologist named Peter Sinclair, put it almost that bluntly: overfishing by local inshore fishers "must be discounted as a major factor" even though they "seem to bear the brunt" of what happened. They're not the criminals in this story. They're closer to the first responders who show up and get blamed for the fire.

So who did it. Before 1977, mostly foreign trawlers, working water Canada didn't yet control. Then Canada declared a 200-mile economic zone, pushed the foreign fleet out — and its own offshore dragger fleet expanded fast to take up the slack. That's the part almost nobody remembers: the dominant driver after 1977 wasn't some outside villain, it was Canada's own industrial fleet, each company running boats it was, from where it sat, entirely reasonable to run. Meanwhile the government's own stock model was quietly poisoned by something almost boring: it used catch-per-effort as a stand-in for how many fish were actually out there, and catch-per-effort lies to you when technology improves, because better sonar and bigger nets let a shrinking number of fish get caught at the same steady rate for years, right up until they don't. In 1987 the inshore fleet paid out of pocket for an independent audit — they didn't trust the official numbers because their own boats were finding less — and the audit found the government's math wrong. It took years to even locate the specific error in the model. By 1989 the department's own scientists were recommending catch cuts of more than half. The government didn't cut that far. The quota Crosbie set for 1992 — 187,969 tonnes — was actually higher than the entire industry's catch the year before, 129,033 tonnes. He authorized more fish to be taken than anyone had even managed to find and land the previous season.

Every one of those choices makes sense from inside it. The inshore boats were reasonable to keep fishing — it's their whole economy, and they weren't the ones causing the collapse anyway. The offshore fleet was reasonable to expand into water the foreign boats had vacated. The scientists were reasonable to trust a catch-based model until they had a specific, hard-won reason not to. And Crosbie — a Newfoundlander, not some Ottawa villain shipped in to do the dirty work — was reasonable too, in the ugliest sense of the word: cutting the quota meant handing four hundred communities an immediate, concrete, front-page catastrophe in exchange for a fish population that might recover in a decade, might not, and wouldn't show up as a win for anyone currently in office. He had to pay the entire local cost of a diffuse future benefit, on a Tuesday, with his name on it. Almost nobody does that voluntarily. The stock collapsed to under five percent of its already-depleted 1990 level within two years. The "temporary" moratorium didn't meaningfully lift until 2024. Thirty-two years for a "two-year" fix.

That's the structure I keep telling you about at dinner. Individually reasonable, collectively ruinous, and — this is the part that matters — nobody in the story was confused about where it was headed. The inshore fishermen weren't wrong. The scientists, eventually, weren't wrong. It happened anyway, in slow motion, in front of everybody, for fifteen years.

Okay. Deep breath. Second one, because one example is an anecdote and two is a pattern and you'll ask for a second one anyway.

2017, Amazon announces it's opening a second headquarters and invites North American cities to bid. Two hundred and thirty-eight of them do. Within days a congressman named Ro Khanna is tweeting that the whole contest is turning into a "race to the bottom" — publicly, in real time, before the worst offers had even landed. He wasn't alone; a governor said almost the identical thing on the record a couple years later. And here's the detail I can't get past: cities kept bidding anyway, including ones whose own mayors would go on to use that exact phrase about their own behavior. Chicago put together something like two billion dollars in tax breaks and free land while the "race to the bottom" framing was already public knowledge, being discussed on the news, being said by people running the bid. New York eventually offered around $1.5 billion. Virginia won with roughly $573 million and quietly kept the whole thing.

New York's site collapsed in February 2019 after local political blowback got loud enough that Amazon walked rather than fight it out — half the announced prize, gone, after the city had already committed the subsidy on paper.

You're going to say: this is just greed. People wanting more, companies exploiting it, nothing structural about it at all. I want to give that its full weight, because it's not a dumb objection — greed is real and I'm not here to pretend it isn't. But greed doesn't explain the one detail that actually breaks the case open: these people said, out loud, on the record, that they knew where this led, while they kept doing it. A mayor doesn't warn the public about a race to the bottom and then lose his own city's bid by accident. He knows exactly what he's doing and does it anyway, because unilaterally dropping out doesn't stop the race — it just means his city loses for sure instead of maybe. The inshore fishermen weren't being unreasonable. The scientists weren't being unreasonable. Crosbie wasn't being unreasonable. Nobody here is stupid and nobody here is a monster; they're all climbing the same ladder, and every rung looks sane from where the person standing on it is standing.

That's the shape. Not a formula, not a chart — I'm not going to draw you one of those grids with the little numbers in the corners, you'd fall asleep and honestly so would I. Just two versions of the same trap, worth being able to recognize: one where the cost of restraint is yours alone and the benefit is shared and future, so nobody restrains; one where you're racing people who won't stop, so stopping just means losing alone.
