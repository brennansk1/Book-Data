Bay Bulls is a fishing community about thirty kilometres south of St. John's, and on the first of July, 1992, a crowd of roughly a thousand people filled the wharf there for what was supposed to be a Canada Day event. Fishers, plant workers, wives, husbands, kids in Canada Day shirts. Fog held over the harbor most of the morning and the air smelled the way it always did in a place that had processed fish for five hundred years — salt and diesel and something underneath both. John Crosbie, the federal fisheries minister, came to speak. He was a Newfoundlander himself, St. John's West, but he arrived in a navy wool coat and a tie, dressed for Ottawa, and stood among a crowd in ball caps and bright nylon jackets who by then knew, or badly suspected, what he was going to tell them the next day.

They didn't wait for the next day. They started yelling at him on the wharf.

Crosbie told the crowd there was no need to abuse him. A voice came back. Someone asked whether he was a Newfoundlander or whether Ottawa mattered more to him now. Crosbie answered:

"Why are you yelling at me? I didn't take the fish from the goddamn water, so don't go abusing me."

A voice off to the side asked, well, who took it then. Another answered him directly: you and your goddamn people took it. You and your people took it.

The next day, July 2, Crosbie announced a moratorium on the Northern cod fishery. He called it temporary, something in the range of two years, time enough for the stock to come back. It put about thirty thousand fishers and plant workers out of work in a single announcement, the largest mass layoff in Canadian history, and it emptied out more than four hundred communities that had no other major employer. The moratorium did not lift, even partially, until 2024.

None of this arrived without warning. Inshore fishermen — the ones working small boats and fixed gear, close to shore — had been watching their catches fall for years and getting told by the Department of Fisheries and Oceans that the stock was fine. They didn't believe it, and in 1987 the Newfoundland Inshore Fisheries Association paid an American fisheries scientist, D. L. Alverson, to run an independent audit of the government's own numbers. Alverson found a mathematical fault in the model DFO had been using to count fish. By 1989, DFO's own scientists were recommending the total allowable catch be cut by more than half. The government did not fully act on it. The quota Crosbie himself had set for 1992, the one still in effect the week he stood on that wharf, was 187,969 tonnes — more than the entire Canadian fleet, offshore trawlers and inshore boats together, had managed to catch in all of 1991, which came to 129,033 tonnes. It was a quota for fish that, on the numbers already sitting in Ottawa, might not have existed.

Crosbie went back to St. John's that evening. The fog was still down over the water at Bay Bulls, and the crowd went back to whatever was left of their Canada Day.
