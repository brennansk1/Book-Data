Grace,

You said it in my kitchen, coat still on, my daughter's birthday cake going stale on the counter behind you: "Communes are nice. This doesn't scale." You'd flown in that morning from the water-pricing conference in Denver, and you had that specific red-eye directness where the diplomatic filter just goes offline. I didn't have a good answer that night. I have one now, and it's not the answer you're expecting, because I'm going to concede most of your point before I take the rest of it away from you.

It doesn't scale simply. There's no version of a Valencia water court that governs a national grid, and if I told you otherwise you'd be right to stop reading. But "doesn't scale simply" and "doesn't work" are different claims, and you, of all people, price water for a living, so you should want to know that communities have managed exactly this resource, without markets and without a state doing the pricing, for something like seven hundred years, and that the conditions under which it holds together aren't folklore. They're catalogued. Somebody did the fieldwork.

That somebody was Elinor Ostrom, and the sentence that made her the only political scientist to win a Nobel in economics is on the first page of her book: neither the state nor the market, she wrote, is uniformly successful at getting people to sustain the resources they depend on. I'll admit I came to her work assuming it was basically well-funded anthropology: nice stories about villagers, not a thing you could actually build an incentive structure from. That was lazy, and I was wrong, and I'd rather say so now than have you catch me at it later.

The case she kept coming back to is a court in Valencia that still meets every Thursday at noon outside the cathedral, eight farmers in black work smocks, their own field clothes and not judicial robes, which is somehow the more impressive detail. They adjudicate water disputes out loud, no lawyers, no transcript of the arguments. Only the decisions themselves get written down. What she found there wasn't a nice story. It was a design, and three pieces of it explain most of what makes it hold.

Start with the boundary, because nothing else works without it. In the Valencia huerta the right to water isn't attached to a person, a household, or a permit you renew. It's attached to the land itself. A given parcel is either regadiu, irrigated land with a standing claim, or secano, dry land with none, and everyone for a mile in either direction knows which is which, because it's been true since before anyone alive was born. That sounds too simple to be a "principle," and Ostrom herself warns against reading it that way, since closing the boundary isn't sufficient on its own, but it's necessary. Without a clear answer to who's allowed to draw from a resource, nobody can be blamed for taking more of it, and everybody has a private incentive to. You can't monitor a boundary that doesn't exist. You can't fine someone for breaking a rule nobody agreed defined the crime.

Boundaries first, because the other two principles are downstream of it.

Once the boundary exists, you need to know when someone's crossing it, and this is my favorite part. A farmer waiting his turn on the canal has nothing to do but watch. He tends his own plot near the ditch so he's ready when the water arrives. Miss your moment and you wait for the next rotation, so while he waits, he's staring straight at the farmer ahead of him and at the ditch-rider collecting the toll. Nobody's paid to watch. Watching is just what waiting looks like. It's a stag hunt, if you want the game-theory name: everyone shows up at their gate on time because everyone else is counted on to show up too, and the belief becomes self-fulfilling once one generation has it. Ostrom's own line about it is the cleanest sentence in the whole book: monitoring is a by-product of people's own motivation to use their turn fully, not a bureaucracy layered on top. You don't hire inspectors. You arrange the line so that standing in it is the inspection.

Here's where it gets genuinely weird, and my favorite part of the whole case. You'd expect a system built on constant mutual surveillance to need harsh penalties to make the surveillance worth anything: catch a cheat, make an example, deter the next one. The opposite happened. The fine books for the closely related huerta at Castellón record hundreds of cases a year, and the historian Glick found the fines were low and variable, "a few pennies at the most."

Pennies.

It worked because it was small. A farmer caught taking water out of turn in a bad year, crop failing, family hungry, isn't a criminal, he's a person under pressure, and hitting him with a punishing fine on top of a bad season breeds exactly the resentment that makes someone stop cooperating out of spite the following year. Keep the fine tiny and detection near-certain and you get what Ostrom calls quasi-voluntary compliance: people follow the rule because they trust it produces a shared benefit and trust that almost everyone else is following it too, not because the fine terrifies them. Two-thirds of the people fined in the Castellón books in a given year never appear in the record again. One mistake, mild correction, back in the fold. The system assumed most rule-breaking was circumstance, not character, and built its punishment to match that assumption instead of pretending everyone's a criminal until proven otherwise.

One paragraph of digression, because you'll like this stuff and it isn't load-bearing: a single Castellón farmer turns up in the fine book eighteen times in one year, five as accuser and thirteen as accused, half of it just two families going at each other. The historian who dug this up calls how rare that kind of mess was a tribute to how well the rest of the system worked. One family feud, catalogued for posterity, while the huerta quietly did its job around them.

Now the part I won't soften, because a system that only tells you its wins is a sales pitch, not an argument. There's a fishing village in Sri Lanka called Mawelle where the villagers built rules every bit as careful as Valencia's, a rotation for who launches their net from which beach spot, tuned to weather, tide, and price, so well designed that the anthropologist who lived there two years never once recorded a dispute about the sequence itself, in a village that otherwise saw three murders and seventeen serious assaults during his stay. They got the rules right. Sit with that before the rest of the story ruins it: the working rules existed, they held, and none of it saved them.

A colonial-era law capped the number of nets at thirty-two. Enforcement lapsed, the number started climbing, and by the mid-1940s there were seventy-one nets working a beach that could support maybe thirty. The villagers petitioned to get their own cap enforced, three separate times, and mostly got nowhere. Then in 1964 a man named David Mahattea, who couldn't buy into the existing nets, went around the local official who'd turned him down and got his own Member of Parliament to authorize new licenses anyway. Two factions built twenty-four more nets between them. It ended in a brawl on the water in 1966, boats capsizing, and it took three jeeps of riot police the entrepreneur had arranged in advance to keep it from getting worse. The government froze the count at a hundred and eight, nearly four times the original legal limit and something like five times what the beach could actually support. Nobody in that village was foolish or lazy or bad at cooperation. A politician with a favor to sell walked in from outside and the local rules simply had no standing against him.

That's your answer, or the honest half of it: local governance isn't self-sufficient at any scale, including the small scale. It needs a layer above it that recognizes its authority and doesn't override it on a whim, which is a state, functioning the way you'd actually want a state to function: permissive, not intrusive. Valencia had that. Medieval Aragon left the huerta's institutions alone, more or less, for centuries. Mawelle didn't, and lost in eighteen years what Valencia had held for the better part of a millennium.

This is also, it turns out, Ostrom's own answer to your objection, not a patch I'm bolting on afterward. Her eighth design principle is nested enterprises: the same governance logic repeated at several layers, canal rules folded into district rules folded into regional and national jurisdiction, each level handling what it's actually positioned to see. She's blunt that skipping this produces an incomplete system that won't survive. So no, it doesn't scale the way you meant it. You can't take the Tribunal's exact procedure and run a continental water grid on it. What scales is the logic, nested, which is the eighth principle, on par with the other seven. Interstate rivers, aquifers three states are drawing from at once, anything where the users can't see each other let alone watch each other in line: those are jobs for a state, because the whole mechanism I just described depends on people who can observe one another's turn at the gate.

You're not wrong that most things bigger than a huerta need something more than a Thursday court. You were wrong that this makes the huerta a nice story instead of a working part.
