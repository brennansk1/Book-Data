Thursday, noon. The bell they call the Micalet tolls twelve times from the cathedral tower, and by the last stroke eight men are standing on the small stone landing outside the Apostles' Gate, in black smocks — the same rough cloth any farmer of the Valencia huerta wears into his own fields, not judicial robes, though the Tribunal's own literature says a man wears his with the dignity of a magistrate's. No wigs. No lawyers. A crowd gathers on the steps and along the wall, because the Tribunal de las Aguas has never once, in living memory, closed its doors to whoever wants to watch.

An officer of the court calls the canals in turn — Quart, Mislata, Rascanya, and the rest of the eight that draw from the river Turia — and each syndic answers for his own water. If the matter concerns his own canal, he steps back; he cannot judge himself. What follows takes minutes, not hours. A farmer is accused, say, of opening his gate before his turn, of taking water that belonged to the man behind him in the rotation — the ordinary temptation of a dry country, and the syndics have heard it before. The presiding syndic questions him. He questions whoever else knows anything worth knowing. There is no transcript. The court's own decisions are written down; the arguments that produced them are not, and never have been. Then judgment, on the spot, according to the customary rules of that one canal — because the eight communities do not share a single code, only a shared Thursday.

The fine, when there is one, tends to be small.

The clearest surviving numbers on this come not from Valencia's own huerta but from its close neighbor, Castellón, whose fine books have survived in a way Valencia's own did not: 441 infractions recorded in one year of the fifteenth century, 499 in another, more than one violation logged nearly every day the court sat. The money attached to nearly all of them was, in the record's own words, "a few pennies at the most." The syndic kept two-thirds of it. The accuser — whoever brought the charge — kept the rest.

How old is any of this? Older than anyone can document, and that has to be said carefully. What survives on paper is a royal charter from 1321 confirming the right to Turia water, and a set of articles drawn up by eighty-four irrigators in 1435 who sat down at the monastery of St. Francis and wrote out, for the first time, exactly how water would be shared in a dry year and a wet one. The court itself, and the habits it enforces, are older than either document — how much older is tradition, not record. Valencia held a civic anniversary in 1960 for what it called the Tribunal's thousandth year. No document from the year 960 backs that up. The honest version is: at least six centuries, proven; close to ten, believed, and believed by scholars who say so carefully.

It was into this — a court with no lawyers, a huerta with no property titles in the ordinary sense, fines too small to ruin anyone and somehow enough to keep them mostly honest — that a political scientist from Indiana arrived, on paper rather than in person, in the book she published in 1990. Elinor Ostrom had spent the better part of her career on a single, oddly specific question: how do people share something valuable, at scale, without a market to price it and without a state to police it? She went looking in the archive that Castellón's fine books had left behind, and in the Valencia scholarship built on top of them, and found the same pattern on page after page. Hundreds of infractions a year. Almost all of them minor. Fines too small to matter, and compliance, across centuries of record-keeping, running close to total.
