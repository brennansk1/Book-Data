Dana —

You're going to enjoy this one, because it's a letter where I tell you that you were right, and then spend most of it explaining why that's a weirder win for you than it sounds like.

I've been living for two months with a duke, a dead trading network, and a guy named Boehm, and all three keep dragging me back to the thing you said at Ellen's wedding, standing by the bar with your tie already off. You said: if a rule only exists because it's useful, it isn't a moral rule, it's a strategy wearing one's clothes. You can drop a strategy the moment it stops paying. You can't drop an obligation just because it gets inconvenient — that's what makes it an obligation and not a preference. I said something about how usefulness, sustained over enough generations, starts to look like more than a strategy. You said that's exactly what a strategy would say. I didn't have a comeback then. I spent a long time telling myself you'd gotten lucky with the line, which was easier than admitting you'd found the soft spot on the first try. I don't think that anymore.

Here's what two months of reading has actually given me: not an answer to you, but two much better versions of your objection than the one you made at the bar.

Start with the duke. Wellington, sitting Prime Minister, rode out to Battersea Fields in March of 1829 and fired a pistol at a fellow peer over a newspaper letter. This was not a man losing his temper. It was a man executing a protocol — twelve paces, seconds, a fixed hour — that he'd have followed exactly the same way if he'd felt nothing at all. And the protocol worked, in the sense that protocols work: it let two men who despised each other walk off the field with their standing intact, at the cost of nobody's life, most of the time. The duel was a machine for manufacturing a specific kind of trust. In a world with no credit bureaus, no enforceable contracts between gentlemen, no real police interest in what happened between men of a certain class, your word was the only collateral you had, and the willingness to stand at twelve paces was the collateral behind the collateral — proof, expensive and hard to fake, that you meant what you said and weren't afraid of the consequences of having said it. Take away the willingness to duel and a gentleman's promise was just a sentence.

Then it died. Not slowly — Appiah's chapter on this is the one honest thing I'll say I stole outright, so credit where it's due — but in about a generation. Wellington fires his pistol in 1829; by the 1850s the whole apparatus is essentially finished in England. What's interesting, and this is the part that should worry you more than it worries me, is what killed it. Not moral argument — clergymen had been calling dueling a sin for two hundred years and nobody stopped for a sermon. What killed it, Appiah argues, was ridicule. Once dueling drifted down the social scale, once tradesmen and clerks started fighting them too, the whole point of the exercise collapsed, because the point had never been the violence. It had been the exclusivity. A signal that anyone can send stops being a signal, and the gentry dropped the practice within a generation of the moment it stopped telling them apart from everyone else. I'll flag, because you'd ask anyway: some historians think Appiah leans too hard on ridicule alone and underweights the new codes of respectable manhood rising alongside it. Probably both are true.

Now the merchants, and this is the one I actually trust more, because an economic historian named Avner Greif built it out of eleventh-century letters instead of a single reviewer's paraphrase. Jewish traders working the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean out of North Africa — the Maghribi traders — had the oldest problem in long-distance commerce: you have to hand your goods to an agent in a port you will never visit, who can simply steal from you, and there is no court that reaches both cities and no way to get a message there faster than a ship can sail. Greif's answer, reconstructed out of documents pulled from a storeroom attached to a Cairo synagogue and shipped to Cambridge at the end of the nineteenth century by a scholar named Solomon Schechter — a room nobody was supposed to enter for nine hundred years, because Jewish law forbids destroying anything bearing the name of God, so they just kept everything — is that the traders solved it with a closed reputation network instead. Cheat one merchant in the group and word didn't leak to one port. It reached all of them, and every merchant in the coalition stopped dealing with you at once. A man who cheated in Palermo lost his livelihood in Cairo, on the far side of a sea, months before he'd have expected anyone there to hear about it. Cheating stopped paying not because it was wrong but because the network made sure it never worked twice.

(Historians dispute this, some of them seriously — Edwards and Ogilvie think Greif overstates how much of the enforcement was reputation versus plain old courts, and Greif thinks they're attacking a stronger claim than he made. I'm not going to adjudicate it here. The mechanism is real either way; the argument is about how much weight it carried.)

Boehm, quickly, because he's the theory underneath both stories and I don't want to give him more room than he needs. He spent a career comparing chimpanzee troops, which have dominance hierarchies, to human forager bands, which mostly don't — not because humans lack the drive to dominate each other, but because forager groups actively suppress it. His 1993 paper lays out the toolkit, and it escalates: ridicule first, for anyone getting too big for the group; then noncompliance; then ostracism; and, for the rare case that doesn't take the hint, execution by consensus. Boehm's own name for the result is a reverse dominance hierarchy, and his point, which is the point of this whole letter, is that it is not the natural state of anything. It's maintained. Constantly, at cost, by people who'd individually rather not bother.

So: three unrelated groups of people, separated by continents and centuries, independently build almost the same machine — reputation, exclusion, graduated punishment — to solve almost the same problem, which is that cooperation is fragile and cheating is always locally profitable. That's not decoration. That's convergent engineering. The rules look arbitrary right up until you ask what breaks when you remove them, and the answer is always the same thing: the ability to trust a stranger enough to do business with him. I'll admit, because it's true, that both of my main cases only worked because they excluded people — the coalition punished its own and had no lever over anyone outside it; the whole honor world Wellington belonged to was closed by definition, a thread I'm deliberately not pulling on yet. There's also the Icelandic sagas, which run this exact machine with lawsuits instead of pistols — not here, that's a book someone else can write.

One more, briefer, because it's still going on. German student fraternities still fence the Mensur, blades sharpened, faces unprotected, until someone draws first blood, and the resulting scar is worn — not hidden — for the rest of the man's life. Nobody claims this is about swordsmanship. It's the duel's exact logic with the lethality engineered out and the visibility engineered up: an unfakeable, permanent, third-party-legible proof that you stood still under a blade. I don't love resting weight on this one — the numbers I've seen on how far it actually opened doors are thinner than I'd like, and I'd rather undersell it than get caught overselling a scar. But the shape rhymes too well to leave out.

Which gets me back to you at the bar. Here's the strongest version of what you said, sharpened past where you left it: if these institutions exist purely because they're useful — because they solve a coordination problem for the people running them — then nothing about them is actually binding. A useful rule is a rule you follow while it pays and drop the moment it stops. That's not a description of morality. That's a description of a very good norm. The duel, the coalition, the reverse dominance hierarchy — I've just spent five pages proving all three are exactly that: solutions, invented because the problem recurs, and abandoned the instant the environment changes enough that they stop working, which is precisely what happened to dueling within thirty years.

I used to think building the strongest possible case for the *usefulness* of morality was itself an answer to you. It isn't. It's the opposite. The better I make that case, the sharper your objection gets, because everything I've shown you is a machine that runs on incentives, and machines that run on incentives don't obligate anyone once the incentive is gone. If that's all morality is, you were right at the bar and I've just spent two months proving it in more detail than either of us wanted.

I don't have the next part yet. I think the rest of this book is, more or less, an attempt to find out whether there's something underneath the machinery that isn't just more machinery — and I'm telling you now, before I've written it, that I'm not sure the answer is yes.
