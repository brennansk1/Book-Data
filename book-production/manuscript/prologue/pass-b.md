Dear Eli,

Sorry this is late — I said I'd write back the same week and then three weeks happened instead, which is on brand for me and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. It's almost midnight here, I've got cold coffee next to the laptop that I keep drinking anyway because I'm too lazy to microwave it, and I've been turning something over for a month that I think you'll either find fascinating or think I've lost the plot on. Probably both. That's usually how it goes with us.

So my niece is applying to residency this year. You've met her, the loud one, the one who beat you at cards on Thanksgiving and has never once let you forget it. She's a fourth-year medical student now, which still doesn't feel real to me, and she is in the middle of the single most expensive act of paperwork a human being can perform in this country short of a divorce. She is applying to something like seventy programs. Seventy. Not because she wants to work at seventy hospitals — she wants to work at maybe six, realistically three — but because everyone applies to seventy now, and if she applies to fewer than everyone else, she's the one who ends up with nothing in March. Ten years ago the average was about half that. Nobody sat down and decided to double it. It just crept, one application cycle at a time, the way your hairline does, except she's twenty-six and paying fifteen hundred dollars for the privilege.

Here's the part that actually kept me up. I talked to a dean out west — this wasn't for the niece thing, it was a separate rabbit hole I went down, you know how I get — and she said something I haven't been able to shake. I'm going to get the wording slightly wrong but not by much: it's heartbreaking, she said, because it involves so much money and so much effort, and even though we advise students not to do it, they do it anyway. Sit with that for a second. This is not some cartoon villain running the med school admissions office rubbing his hands together. This is a person whose actual job is to protect these kids, standing there, watching them do a thing she has personally, directly told them not to do, and they do it anyway, and she can't stop them, and honestly neither can they stop themselves.

And I want to be straight with you about something, because you'll call it out if I don't: my first reaction to all this, when Sarah first explained the applying-to-seventy-programs thing to me over Christmas, was not sympathy. It was something closer to irritation. I remember thinking, privately, this generation and their neuroses, just pick a dozen good ones and relax. That was a stupid thing to think and I knew it was stupid roughly four seconds after I thought it, but I want to put it on the record because it's the instinct almost everybody has, including people who should know better, including me. The instinct is: somebody here is being unreasonable. Find them. Blame them. The reason I bring it up is that I spent a solid week trying to find that somebody, in this specific case, and I came up empty, and I don't think that's because I didn't look hard enough.

Because here's the thing — everybody in this system is doing the sane thing. The programs aren't being greedy; they're just trying not to end up with empty seats, which used to be a real risk before all this. The applicants aren't being neurotic; if you're the one kid who applies to twenty-five programs like it's 2011, you might genuinely not match anywhere, and "not matching" for a fourth-year medical student isn't a minor setback, it's closer to a small career death. The dean isn't lying when she tells them not to do it — it's true advice, individually, for any one student in isolation. Everyone is right. Everyone is behaving like a reasonable adult who has looked at their actual situation and made the sensible call given what everyone else is doing. And the result of several thousand reasonable people doing the sensible thing is a system where a fourth-year has to spend money she doesn't have applying to hospitals she'll never set foot in, on the off chance one of them says yes.

They did eventually try to fix part of it — some new thing where you can flag a handful of programs as your real top choices, so programs can tell the difference between someone who means it and someone blanket-applying — and applications did come down a bit this last cycle. I want to be honest with you about that too, because it would be easy to write you a tidier letter that pretends nothing ever gets better. Something did get a little better. But — and this is the part I can't get past — the exact same thing is now happening one level down, with research. Kids are padding their applications with research projects they don't care about instead of, I don't know, learning to actually talk to patients, because research is now the thing that separates you from the pile, and next year it'll be something else, and the year after that something else again. You plug the hole and the water finds a different wall.

I keep thinking about this because it isn't really about medicine, is it. You'd know better than me — isn't this basically what you told me happened with your hiring pipeline two jobs ago, the one where everyone started requiring a coding challenge and then a take-home project and then a second take-home project, and every single manager you talked to thought the whole thing was absurd and every single manager kept doing it? I don't think that's a coincidence and I don't think it's a coincidence you and I keep landing on stories shaped exactly like this one whenever we talk late at night. I don't have a name for the shape yet. I've tried "everyone loses" and it isn't that, some people clearly do fine out of it. I've tried "nobody's fault" and that isn't quite right either, because it makes it sound like an accident, and this isn't an accident, it happens the same way every time, on schedule, like something with a mechanism.

What I keep coming back to, and what I actually wanted to ask you, since you're the only person I know who'll take this seriously instead of telling me to relax — how do you even hold someone responsible for something that nobody chose and everybody's doing? Who do you get angry at? Who do you forgive?

Miss you. Call me this weekend if you're around — I want to hear how the new place is going and I want to argue about this in person, it's better that way.

— T
