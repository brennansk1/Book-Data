September 2022. Rachel Kudrna is a fourth-year at Washington State University's Elson S. Floyd College of Medicine, and it is the month the Electronic Residency Application Service opens for the year's applicants. The portal looks like a tax form: checkboxes next to program names, a running total in the corner, a submit button that does not move until the total is paid.

The average U.S. medical graduate that cycle will submit around seventy of those checkboxes. A decade earlier the average was roughly half that. By the following year the number will climb again, to 72.8 — up 24 percent from just 2017. Seventy applications, at the fee schedule in effect that year, comes to $1,499 before a single interview is booked. Interview travel is separate, and for the more competitive specialties it can run past $10,000 — flights, hotel rooms, a suit that has to look the same in Cleveland as it did in Houston, the fourteenth Tuesday in a row spent explaining, in a different building, why this particular hospital is the one she has always wanted to work at.

Kudrna has been through the surrounding stretch of it already, the studying and the boards and the rotations, and what she says about this part is simpler than any of that. "There's tons of anxiety," she says. "It feels like after working so hard to become a physician, it could all still fall apart."

Down the hall, or somewhere equivalent to a hall in a college of medicine that spans a state, is Leila Harrison, WSU's senior associate dean for admissions and student affairs. Her job includes telling students not to do what Kudrna and her classmates are, in September, doing anyway. Harrison advises against padding the list — a program can only train so many residents, and seventy applications from one student don't create a seventy-first slot to fill. She gives the advice every year. Every year the number goes up anyway.

"It's heartbreaking because this involves so much money and effort," Harrison says. "Even though we advise students not to do it, they do it anyway."

The programs feel it from the other side of the same screen. One program, reviewing the flood of applications that arrives every fall, estimated the cost in lost faculty clinical time at $3,736 per applicant interviewed — a single program's number, not a national one, but a number attached to real clinic hours that a real physician spent reading files instead of seeing patients. The median number of interviews a successful applicant actually completes has crept up too, from ten a decade ago to thirteen now, which means the faculty on the other end are reading more files to fill the same number of chairs.

Nobody in this is doing anything unreasonable. Kudrna is applying to enough programs to protect herself against the chance that thirteen of them say no. Harrison is giving accurate, sound advice to a room full of people who cannot individually afford to take it, because the protection only works if you have more of it than the person next to you. The programs are drowning in files because every other program is also drowning in files, for the same reason, at the same time, every September.

It is the last week of the month. Kudrna has the portal open on her laptop, the cursor sitting in the box for program number forty, then forty-one. Somewhere the total is still climbing.
