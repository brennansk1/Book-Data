James Somerset had been purchased in Virginia, some accounts say Boston, around 1749, by a customs officer named Charles Stewart. Stewart brought him to England in 1769. Almost nothing else about Somerset as a man survives in the record: no letters, no physical description beyond the bare fact of him, nothing about what he thought he was doing when, in October 1771, he ran.

He was free for a little over a month. Recaptured in late November, he was chained aboard a ship called the *Ann and Mary*, under a captain named John Knowles, bound for Jamaica to be sold. Three people intervened: John Marlow, Thomas Walkin, and Elizabeth Cade, godparents from Somerset's baptism into the Church of England, who applied for a writ of habeas corpus on his behalf. That application is close to the edge of what is independently documented about him. After the case, he disappears from the record entirely.

The man who took the case up was Granville Sharp — not a lawyer, an abolitionist layman who had already spent years intervening in similar disputes and had published a tract, three years earlier, arguing that English law gave no support to slavery. Sharp organized Somerset's legal team and paid for it himself. This was not an accident of circumstance. He had been looking for a case with exactly this shape.

Stewart, for his part, seems to have wanted to settle. Mansfield reportedly urged it too, more than once, before the hearing ever reached judgment. But Stewart was backed by other slaveholders and West India merchants who wanted a precedent, not a private accommodation, and the case went forward.

Lord Mansfield delivered his ruling on 22 June 1772, in a courtroom he had tried repeatedly to keep out of the position of ruling at all — he worried aloud, on the record, about the property consequences of a sweeping decision, in a country then holding an estimated fourteen to fifteen thousand enslaved people. The judgment itself was never formally published; it survives only through the law reports that recorded it. What he actually said, as those reports have it, was this: "The state of slavery is of such a nature, that it is incapable of being introduced on any reasons, moral or political, but only by positive law... it's so odious, that nothing can be suffered to support it, but positive law... the black must be discharged."

That is narrow. It says a master cannot use force to put a man on a ship and sell him abroad against his will. It does not say slavery is illegal in England. It did not free the fourteen thousand. Slavery went on, in fact, in the country, for years afterward.

The line everyone remembers is not in that judgment at all. Somewhere in argument, one of Somerset's own counsel invoked a case from two centuries earlier, 1569, the *Cartwright* case, for the claim that England's air was too pure for a slave to breathe in. The record is not settled on whether the counsel who said it was William Davy or John Hargrave. Even that older case may not have said it. A borrowed line, about a borrowed precedent, entered the transcript as advocacy and left it as verdict.

By the time the story had finished traveling from the courtroom out into the city, slavery had ended in England. It had not.
