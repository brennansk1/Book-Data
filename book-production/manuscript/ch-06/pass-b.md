Helen —

I don't know if they'll read this to you or just file it, and I've made my peace with not knowing which. It's already cooling off here, the first real drop of the year, the kind where you notice your own breath again. So I'm going to write it the way I would have written it in 2003, sophomore year, when you were still handing papers back with more ink in the margins than I'd put on the page. You'll forgive me if it runs long. You always said the ones that ran long were the ones I actually cared about, and you were right about that the way you were right about most things, which is to say: mostly, and not in a way that let you off the hook for the rest.

Here's the thing I keep circling. You spent a whole semester trying to get eighteen of us to take the egoist seriously, and I don't think I did, not really, not until about four years ago. I thought the position was just unreasonable, dressed up as philosophy — a person refusing to see what was right in front of him. I want to tell you I was closer to seeing it than I was, so let me not do that. I was wrong, in the ordinary sense, the whole time you were teaching me otherwise.

Here's the strongest version, the one you'd have wanted me to sit with instead of dodge. My pain is bad for me. Yours is bad for you. There is no third position, no view from nowhere, from which the two facts get weighed on the same scale — there's just my scale and your scale, and nothing forces me onto yours. Sidgwick said it plainer than I can: it would be contrary to common sense to deny that the distinction between one individual and another is real and fundamental, and that I am concerned with the quality of my own existence in a sense I am not concerned with anyone else's, and — this is the part that actually stopped me — he didn't see how it could be proved that this distinction wasn't the one that should govern the ultimate end of rational action for an individual. He wasn't posturing. He spent the rest of his life unhappy about it, by every account I've read, and never found the proof.

Parfit went looking too, decades later, in the book you made us read the hard chapters of. He tried something clever: if the utilitarian has to answer why I should sacrifice my own happiness for someone else's greater happiness, then it's only fair to ask the egoist the same question about his own future — why sacrifice a pleasure right now for a bigger one next year, why care about his future self any more than about anyone else's present self. It's a real question. But it doesn't convict the egoist of anything, it just shows that pure self-interest is a strange, half-built position — partial across persons, weirdly impartial across his own timeline — sitting at an unstable spot between two cleaner ones. Unstable isn't the same as wrong. Nagel had tried something similar even earlier, arguing that seeing yourself as one person among others rationally compels the same move as seeing the present as one moment among others, and by his own later account it didn't work. Singer — forty years into trying to get people to widen the circle — reports that failure and then does the thing I actually want you to notice: he doesn't call the man who declines the move irrational. He says plainly that those who don't take the further step aren't in error. Peter Singer, conceding the point to the person he's spent his career arguing against, in his own book, without flinching.

So here's where the last letter and this one actually connect, and I keep losing this thread when I try to say it out loud, so bear with the plumbing. The axiom only tells you what has value — that suffering, wherever it shows up, is bad. It doesn't tell you whose suffering you're required to count. Those are two different jobs and the old draft of this book, the one you'd have hated, tried to make the first job do the second job's work, by turning around and asking the egoist to justify himself. That's backwards. It puts the burden on the wrong person and it doesn't even work, because — see above — there's a real answer waiting on the other side, one your own favorite philosopher never managed to defeat.

The impartiality has to come from somewhere else. It comes from the shape of the game, not the shape of the axiom. Picture agents who don't know where they'll land — who's strong, who's weak, who's inside the circle and who's out — playing the same game with each other indefinitely, over and over, with no fixed end. A rule that only protects the currently strong is a rule that stops protecting you the moment you're not strong anymore, and everyone in the game knows it, which means nobody stable can be built on it. Rules that count everyone's stake the same way are the only ones anybody has a standing reason to keep once the wind changes. That's not a nicer story about human progress. It's closer to hydraulics than to sentiment: the water's been climbing the whole time, and you plug the leak at one wall of the tank only to watch it find the next one, because the pressure was never really about that particular wall.

Which gets me to the part of this argument I actually think is ours — yours, mine, everyone who ever sat in that seminar room — the part that isn't just borrowed Sidgwick and repackaged Parfit. Every place anyone has ever drawn the line — tribe, then nation, then race, then sex, then species, then some fuzzy cognitive threshold nobody can quite locate — has moved. Not because people got kinder. Because none of those lines was ever a natural resting point, and every one of them was exploitable by whoever had something to gain from redrawing it. Somerset walks free on a ruling that frees almost no one else, and forty years later the whole framework it rested on is gone. Bentham, in 1789, asks the one-sentence question that guts a century of settled practice before the century has even started: not can they reason, not can they talk, but can they suffer. A century and a half after that, in a room in Cambridge in July of 2012, a group of neuroscientists signs a declaration saying, essentially, we were wrong about where the line was, again, with Stephen Hawking sitting in the audience listening to them say it. The line keeps moving because it was never a wall to begin with. It was a place the water happened to be pooling.

I'd love to leave it there. You wouldn't let me, so I won't either.

Caste. You know this is the objection I owe you honestly, because you're the one who taught me not to skip the case that doesn't fit. A boundary can hold. The Indian caste system, on the reading historians used to give it, sat more or less unmoved for over two thousand years — four classes, same order, generation after generation. My first instinct was that it must have been eroding quietly the whole time and we just don't have the record. Too neat, and I don't believe it. The better answer, and the one that actually costs the argument something: the boundary got sharper in the colonial period, not softer, hardened by an administration confident it would never be on the wrong side of the line it was drawing. Which is, followed all the way through, exactly the condition under which this argument predicts a boundary can hold — an agent sure it's permanently advantaged has no game-theoretic reason to want impartial rules. I don't love that this is true. It means the claim isn't "everything erodes," full stop. It bites only where somebody, eventually, has reason to doubt they'll stay on top, and I have to say that plainly instead of letting the tidy version stand.

And the economics. Historians still argue whether abolition tracked the collapse of a moral consensus or the collapse of sugar profits — Eric Williams said profits, Seymour Drescher spent a career arguing it stayed profitable and Britain gave it up anyway. Not settled, and I won't pretend it is. What I'll say instead: the equilibrium story explains the direction things moved and why they never moved back — nobody has ever un-abolished a boundary once it fell — but the timing, the why-1833-and-not-1785, ran substantially through money and power, not through anyone discovering game theory. Both can be true. I think both are.

The place none of this reaches cleanly is the place it matters most: infants, the severely disabled, animals, people who don't exist yet. None of them can play the game. They can't threaten, bargain, or shift a coalition, which means the whole engine I just described — nobody wants to risk being on the losing side — has nothing to grip. A newborn in 1985 had major surgery with a paralytic and no anesthesia, because the standing belief was that infant nerves couldn't carry the signal; it took one mother, Jill Lawson, and a doctor's published data, to move that particular wall two years later. The infant did nothing to earn that. This is the part of the argument that has to do its work without reciprocity anywhere in it, and I don't think I've fully done that work yet. I think it's where the whole thing is honestly still under construction, and I'd rather tell you that than pretend the tank has no more holes in it.

I found your comments on my old caste-and-relativism paper in a box in Mom's garage last month, helping her sort through what to keep before the move. You'd written, next to a paragraph I was clearly proud of, "the exception is the argument." I didn't understand that note at twenty. I think I finally do.

— B.
