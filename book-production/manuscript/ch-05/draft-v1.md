Two chapters back I told Dana I owed her an answer for why a merely useful rule should bind her once it stops paying. I still owe it. Part Two starts by asking a smaller, prior question first: whether anything underneath any of this is real enough to build an answer on.

In 1928, Paul Schilder and Erwin Stengel published a case history built from a series of tests run on a patient recovering from sensory aphasia. She does not withdraw her arm from a pinprick. She does not turn her body away from whoever is holding the pin. In the whole course of the examination she makes no attempt to avoid the examiner.

What she does, the case notes read, is this.

Schilder and Stengel wrote it down plainly, in the flat present tense clinicians use for things they're watching happen in front of them: "Pricked on the right palm, the patient smiles joyfully, winces a little, and then says, 'Oh, pain, that hurts.'" She laughs. She reaches the hand further toward the investigator and turns it to expose all sides. The same performance follows at her face. It follows again at her stomach. Her expression throughout, the authors note, is one of complacency. She is not confused about what has happened to her, she names it correctly, every time, as pain. She is not inattentive. She leans in.

Pain asymbolia, the condition she has, is now understood to follow damage to the posterior insula, the fold of cortex where the sensation of injury is thought to connect to the felt sense that it matters. Nobody in 1928 had a scan that could show that. They had her. They had a pin, and a page of notes.

Ten patients pass through Schilder and Stengel's series that year and the next with some version of the same split. Five recover it as they recover from the aphasia, the withdrawal comes back the way the words come back. Two, later, say they can remember the pin going in. They say they did not feel it.

The paper itself was written in German. The words above are a translator's English, made decades afterward, for a journal citing the original case.

On the thirtieth of September, 1811, in Paris, Frances Burney, the English novelist, then fifty-nine, underwent a mastectomy for a probable cancer of the breast. Seven medical men were in the room. Antoine Dubois, at the time the Empress's own accoucheur, led the operation. Dominique Jean Larrey, Napoleon's chief military surgeon and one of the most celebrated surgeons in Europe, assisted him. A Dr. Moreau, a Dr. Aumont, a Dr. Ribe, and two surgical pupils filled out the room. There was no anaesthetic. Ether was thirty-five years away.

She did not write about it until nearly six months later, in a letter to her sister Esther dated the twenty-second of March, 1812. It was composed, by her own account, in a kind of trance, and never reread afterward. The letter runs to twelve pages in her own hand. It is held now by the British Library, which has photographed and posted every page of it. The library's own description calls it remarkable as a patient's record of an operation from before anaesthesia existed, when surgery itself was still in its infancy.

She told Esther what the knife felt like going in: "when the dreadful steel was plunged into the breast — cutting through veins — arteries — flesh — nerves — I needed no injunctions not to restrain my cries. I began a scream that lasted unintermittingly during the whole time of the incision."

No pin, this time. A knife, and seven men holding her still.

Jonah, you called Tuesday from the parking lot outside the endodontist's. Your jaw was still numb, and the first thing you told me was that the drill had caught something it wasn't supposed to catch, a nerve the chart said should already be dead. For about four seconds you felt it directly, the vibration and the heat and something under both that wasn't either one. Then you said the thing I want to write to you about. You didn't just feel a sensation. It wasn't the strength of it that struck you. It was that the sensation was *bad*, like that was simply what it was.

I said, and I want this on the record because I got it wrong out loud and you caught me, "that's your nervous system labeling it, though. The signal's neutral. You're adding the verdict." You didn't let it go. You asked whether the drill going into a dead tooth carries the same label, functionally, as the drill going into your gum. I said no, of course not. And then I heard myself. I've spent four months arguing against the exact position I'd just handed you, because it's the easy sentence, the one that sounds sophisticated at a dinner party: sensation, then commentary, added after, like a caption under a photograph. I don't think that anymore. You talked me out of it, mid-sentence, before I'd finished being wrong.

It's the fast-first, check-later problem I've told you about at length: I reach the tidy version of an idea before I've done the work the idea requires, and usually I get away with it, because most people don't push. <!-- KEEP -->

You pushed, from a numb-jawed parking lot, without knowing you were doing philosophy, and it took you eleven words. I'm not telling you this because it makes a good story about a phone call. I'm telling you because the sentence I said to you, the one about the neutral signal and the added verdict, isn't a fringe position I invented to be wrong for dramatic effect. It's close to the default answer in a lot of the philosophy I've actually read, stated by people considerably smarter than I am. I believed a version of it for years before you asked the question that made it come apart in real time. I don't get to write the rest of this chapter as though I arrived at the answer through patient scholarship. I arrived at it because my brother asked me something obvious and I didn't have anything to say back. The scholarship came after, to check whether you were right or just quicker than me. You were right. It took me most of a season to catch up to eleven words spoken with your face still half asleep from the anesthetic.

Here's the case doing the actual work. That woman in the 1928 clinical literature, no name, just a case number, the convention of the era, had a stroke and came out able to feel pain without minding it. Pricked in the palm, she'd smile, wince a little, say, "Oh, pain, that hurts," then hold the hand out further, turning it so the doctor could reach more of her arm. Not confused. Not numb. She named the sensation correctly every time. She just couldn't feel that anything was wrong with having it. The condition is called pain asymbolia, and it's the closest thing philosophy has to a natural experiment on the question you asked from the parking lot. Can you have the sensation with the badness taken out surgically, cleanly, the way a nerve gets clipped? Empirically, it looks like yes.

What that fact *proves* is where I owe you honesty rather than momentum, because the people who study it don't agree with each other. Nikola Grahek, whose book on this is the standard reference, reads it as two components normally soldered together. Sensory and affective, he calls them, and a stroke can pry them apart cleanly, the way you'd sever two wires that happen to run through the same conduit for years without either one noticing the other. That reading helps me. It means the badness was a real, separate ingredient all along, not a story stitched on afterward. A philosopher named Colin Klein disagrees, and not quietly. His reading is that asymbolics haven't lost pain's badness specifically. They've lost something broader: a general capacity to care about their own bodies, since the same patients also don't flinch at a swung fist, a shouted threat, a truck horn behind them on a hospital road. Two papers from the last two years are still fighting over which of them is right. I'm confident the two things can come apart in this condition. I'm far less confident about what Grahek and Klein's fight over *why* actually settles, and I don't think either of them would claim it's settled either. What the case shows for certain is that the two things can come apart. What the separation *means* stays argued.

Set it beside Fanny Burney, 1811, a mastectomy in her own drawing room with seven doctors present and nothing at all for the pain. No dispute about her case. The sensation at full voltage, badness fused all the way through, nothing left to peel apart. Put her letter next to the woman offering her arm to the doctor and you have the whole argument in two documents. The two things travel together in one case. They come apart in the other. That gap is solid enough, so far, to build the rest of this on.

There's a further case I could reach for here, and I'm not going to. There's a whole literature on children born with no pain signal at all, congenital insensitivity, and it would make a tidy third exhibit at a dinner party. It doesn't do work the asymbolia case doesn't already do more precisely, so I'm leaving it out.

You laughed at this on the phone and said the real version of the objection: fine, pain is weird, but everything about being a person is weird, so what does one more weird thing buy you? J. L. Mackie made this formal in 1977. "If there were objective values," he wrote, "then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe. Correspondingly, if we were aware of them, it would have to be by some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else." My answer is close to yours. It stops being a complaint about *this* property once you notice consciousness is already the queer thing. A private, felt fact, sitting inside a universe otherwise made of particles hitting other particles, with no account anywhere of why any of it comes with something it's like to be you, wincing in a parking lot with your jaw still numb. Grant experience exists at all, and one more charged bit of it is a smaller ask than Mackie needs.

His case was built a priori, from the armchair, about what a universe is allowed to contain, and the a priori has a bad record specifically here. It told philosophers for a couple of centuries that consciousness itself couldn't be a fact about brain matter, on roughly the same grounds Mackie now uses against value. Consciousness turned out to be a fact about brain matter anyway. The armchair lost that argument once already. I don't see why it gets a second run at the same table with better odds.

There's an older cousin of the worry, from G. E. Moore in 1903. Even granting badness just *is* the felt quality, can't I meaningfully ask whether that quality is actually bad? If the question stays open, the two can't be identical. That's a real objection against a *definition*, and I'm not offering one. I'm making the kind of claim chemists made about water and H2O: not that the words mean the same thing, but that the two turned out, on investigation, to be the same stuff in the glass. You can still ask whether H2O is really water and feel a gap open up, right until you notice the gap is in your concepts, not the world. Noticing that settled the question before you got to ask it properly.

The harder one is the one you'd already handed me without knowing it, later in the same call, when you asked whether this is just your brain doing what brains do and I'm calling the result truth. That's the shape of an argument Sharon Street built in 2006, and unlike Mackie she aims a version directly at pain, where realists usually think they're safest. Evolution shaped which sensations we find aversive. If there's also an independent fact about which sensations are *really* bad, she argues, it would be a strange coincidence for evolution to have built us to track it, since natural selection has no access to moral truth and no reason to care about it. She defines pain, for the sake of the argument, as a sensation a creature "unreflectively takes... to count in favor of" avoiding it. Its badness, on that definition, "hinges crucially on our unreflective evaluative attitudes." Her own line, worth quoting exactly because it's the sharpest version of the case against everything I've told you so far: "it is a mistake to say that pain is bad independently of our evaluative attitudes."

She even runs a thought experiment to force the point: a miracle drug that leaves the raw sensation untouched but makes the patient enjoy having it. If badness really is independent of attitude, she says, that drugged patient's pain should stay just as bad as yours was in the chair. It doesn't feel that way to anyone who considers the case. Which, on her reading, means the attitude was doing the work all along, and the sensation itself was never the load-bearing thing.

Here's the answer that closes the loop. Street's dilemma needs two things: an evolved attitude, and an independent fact it tracks or fails to track. But there aren't two things here. The felt awfulness isn't an attitude standing next to the sensation, grading it. It's what the sensation is. Evolution didn't build a detector aimed at an outside target. It built a state whose felt character does the motivating directly, the way it built color vision, and explaining color vision doesn't explain away the fact that red looks like something. You don't infer a burn is bad from noticing your aversion to it, the way you'd infer a fact from evidence. You're acquainted with the badness directly, in the burn, the instant your hand's off the stove. Street's coincidence never gets a chance to arise. Nothing here is tracking anything.

Her own miracle drug, I think, proves less than she needs it to. Change the felt character of a sensation completely enough and of course a person stops minding it, the same way an asymbolic stops minding a pin. That's the constitutive story working exactly as advertised. It isn't the attitudinal one sneaking back in through a thought experiment, however cleverly the experiment is built.

The honest complication is that there might not be a felt character there to be acquainted with at all. Keith Frankish's view, illusionism he calls it, is that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion, that what's actually happening is complicated brain machinery doing a job, and that self-monitoring dresses the job up as something ineffable and further. In his own words, introspection "misrepresents experiences as having phenomenal properties" they don't actually have. If that's true, the metaphysics under this letter gets thinner. I lose the ability to say badness is a brute fact about the universe, and I'd be handing you a weaker floor than the one I've been building. I don't know whether illusionism is true, and this letter doesn't have to settle it. The machinery that makes you jerk your hand back, avoid the drill, do anything at all to stop the sensation, is still there either way. A later letter owes you the full account of how much that costs the argument. What I need from this one survives even the bleakest reading.

One more concession, and it's real. Philosophers like Chris Heathwood and Fred Feldman would say I've got the order backwards. It's not that the sensation is bad and you dislike it because it's bad. The disliking is what makes it bad, no further ingredient required. Asymbolia fits their story as well as mine. What those patients lost, on this reading, wasn't a piece of the sensation but the capacity to dislike anything about their own bodies. I can't rule this out.

It doesn't cost me the argument, though. Here's the actual demonstration, not just the assertion of one. Nothing downstream of this chapter cares which reading turns out true, none of the rules, none of the overrides, none of the arithmetic for weighing one person's stake against another's. Even on the attitudinal reading, the dislike itself is a conscious state with a genuinely negative character, and that's all the architecture ever needed a grip on. Guy Kahane lands close to my side from a different angle, arguing pain's badness comes from its phenomenal quality even once you concede that being disliked is only contingent. He wasn't trying to help me. He just ended up standing where I needed someone to stand.

Two limits, plainly, because I'd rather you hear them from me than catch me leaving them out.

First: everything above gets you that pain is bad *for the person having it*, you, in the chair, that afternoon. It doesn't get you that your having it obligates some stranger who'll never meet you, wincing right now over a different tooth in a different city. That gap is real. Closing it is a different kind of argument, closer to game theory than to anything in this letter, and it's the next letter's job, not this one's. I'm not going to pretend the bridge is already built just because I can see roughly where it needs to land.

Second, and I already told you this once above but it earns repeating on its own terms: the attitudinal theorists might have this exactly right. Nothing here is proven. It's the strongest case I have, held at exactly the confidence it's earned, no more.

Which brings me back to the parking lot. You didn't need any of this to know the drill hitting that nerve was bad. You knew it in the four seconds it happened, before you had a word ready for it, the way that woman in 1928 apparently didn't, and the way Fanny Burney, screaming through an incision with no anaesthetic anywhere in the world to give her, absolutely did. What took me four months and a phone call I lost badly was working out why you were right without having to argue for it.

That's the actual floor. Not a proof. A floor, the kind you notice by trying to dig under it and hitting something that doesn't move. Every rule this book is about to build gets built on top of that afternoon in the parking lot, on top of a Frenchwoman's letter to her sister, on top of a patient offering more of her arm to a doctor who kept pricking it and kept getting the same answer back. I've spent a whole chapter checking whether the ground would hold.

It holds.
