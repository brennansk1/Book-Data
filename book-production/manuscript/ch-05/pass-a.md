In 1928, Paul Schilder and Erwin Stengel published a case history built from a series of tests run on a patient recovering from sensory aphasia. She does not withdraw her arm from a pinprick. She does not turn her body away from whoever is holding the pin. She makes, in the whole course of the examination, no attempt to avoid the examiner.

What she does, the case notes read, is this:

"Pricked on the right palm, the patient smiles joyfully, winces a little, and then says, 'Oh, pain, that hurts.' She laughs, and reaches the hand further toward the investigator and turns it to expose all sides."

The same performance follows at her face. It follows again at her stomach. Her expression throughout, the authors note, is one of complacency. She is not confused about what has happened to her — she names it correctly, every time, as pain. She is not inattentive. She leans in.

Pain asymbolia, the condition she has, is now understood to follow damage to the posterior insula, the fold of cortex where the sensation of injury is thought to connect to the felt sense that it matters. Nobody in 1928 had a scan that could show that. They had her, and a pin, and a page of notes written in the flat present tense clinicians use for things they are watching happen in front of them.

Ten patients pass through Schilder and Stengel's series that year and the next with some version of the same split. Five of them recover it as they recover from the aphasia — the withdrawal comes back the way the words come back. Two of them, later, say they can remember the pin going in. They say they did not feel it.

The paper itself was written in German. The words above are a translator's English, made decades afterward, for a journal article citing the original case.

On the thirtieth of September, 1811, in Paris, Frances Burney — the English novelist, then fifty-nine — underwent a mastectomy for a probable cancer of the breast. Seven medical men were in the room: Antoine Dubois, at the time the Empress's own accoucheur, who led the operation; Dominique Jean Larrey, Napoleon's chief military surgeon and one of the most celebrated surgeons in Europe; a Dr. Moreau, a Dr. Aumont, a Dr. Ribe; and two surgical pupils, one belonging to each of the senior men. There was no anaesthetic. Ether was thirty-five years away.

She did not write about it until six months later, in a letter to her sister Esther dated the twenty-second of March, 1812 — composed, by her own account, in a kind of trance, and never reread afterward. The letter runs to twelve pages in her own hand. It is held now by the British Library, which has photographed and posted every page of it, and describes it as "remarkable as a patient's record of an operation in the era before anaesthesia, and when surgery was still in its infancy."

She wrote to Esther: "when the dreadful steel was plunged into the breast... I began a scream that lasted unintermittingly during the whole time of the incision."
