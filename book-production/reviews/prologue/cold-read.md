# Cold Read — Prologue draft-v1.md

Read once, straight through, as a reader. Findings ordered by severity.

## 1. Raw production markup left visible in the running text (severe — breaks the read completely)

Quotes: `<!-- PLACEHOLDER -->` (before "I didn't go looking for this because of my niece..."), `Seventy. <!-- KEEP -->`, `<!-- ANCHOR -->` / `<!-- /ANCHOR -->` wrapping the Christmas-irritation paragraph, and a second inline `<!-- KEEP -->` mid-sentence ("was closer to irritation. <!-- KEEP -->" and again after "...she pays fifteen hundred dollars...one of them says yes. <!-- KEEP -->").

Diagnosis: this is not a stylistic seam, it's literal leftover editing tags sitting in the reading copy. No reader gets past the first one without the illusion of a person talking to them collapsing. This has to be fixed before anything else here matters — it makes every other finding somewhat moot until it's resolved.

## 2. The one anecdote that goes generic (attention drift — the exact sentence I slid off)

"A friend of mine watched almost the identical thing happen in his hiring pipeline two jobs ago, at a mid-size software company, though I doubt the industry matters much."

Diagnosis: this is where my attention left the page. Everything around it is loaded with names, dollar figures, and dates (Kudrna, Harrison, Sarah, $1,500, $3,700, thirty research activities, Anmol Shrestha). This sentence has none of that — no name, no company, no year, no number — and then hedges its own relevance ("though I doubt the industry matters much"). It reads like a placeholder example dropped in to generalize the thesis rather than a real memory. It's the one moment in the piece that feels costless rather than earned.

## 3. A concept treated as unnamed when it isn't (a smart reader will side-eye this)

"I don't have a good name for the shape yet. 'Everyone loses' isn't right... 'Nobody's fault' isn't right either... I tried 'the market's fault' for about a day..."

Diagnosis: this dynamic — individually rational choices compounding into a collectively bad, self-perpetuating equilibrium nobody can unilaterally exit — already has several well-known names (arms race, race to the bottom, tragedy of the commons, red queen dynamics, and in some circles "Moloch"). A reasonably well-read lay reader will supply one of these within a sentence or two and start wondering why the narrator is performing a search that ends in "I don't have a good name for it yet" rather than engaging with the existing vocabulary and explaining why none of it quite fits. As written it reads as slightly staged naivety rather than genuine difficulty.

## 4. A structural signpost that briefly breaks register

"Hold that instinct a second. I'm going to come back to it."

Diagnosis: minor, but this is the one place the narrator tells the reader what he's about to do rather than just doing it — closer to the framework-document move ("we will return to this") than to a person thinking aloud. Small, but audible against the rest of the piece, which otherwise doesn't announce its own moves.

## 5. A mood-setting sentence that telegraphs the vulnerability before it arrives

"It's late while I write this part, coffee gone cold next to the keyboard, the kind of hour where a person's guard comes down a little. That seems like the right condition to admit the next part in."

Diagnosis: the admission that follows (the irritation at Sarah, "I'm not proud of the order") is genuinely good and reads as earned. But this sentence announces "a vulnerable moment is coming" immediately before delivering one, which makes the vulnerability feel slightly staged-for-effect rather than simply arrived at. It's a small tell — the confession itself doesn't need the stage direction.

## 6. The ending — lands, with one loose thread

"How do you hold anyone responsible for a result that nobody chose and everybody produced together? Who, exactly, do you get angry at? / Who do you forgive?"

This lands. Two escalating short questions, the second one recontextualizing anger as something closer to grief, ending on the shortest, strongest line in the piece. It does not deflate.

One small tension worth flagging, not fixing: the essay's whole argument is that nobody in the chain did anything wrong — "nobody in this is doing anything unreasonable." "Forgive" implies a wrong to pardon. If truly nobody is at fault, "who do you forgive" is a slightly odd verb to land on rather than something like "who do you grieve" or "who do you blame" (the latter of which the piece already spent a paragraph refusing). It may be intentional friction — needling the reader's instinct to still want someone to forgive even after being told no one's guilty — but it's worth a second look, since it sits right on the last line.

## Overall

The prose itself — sentence by sentence, once you look past the markup — is genuinely strong: specific dollar figures, named real people (Kudrna, Harrison, Shrestha), an honest self-implicating admission in the Christmas paragraph, good short-paragraph rhythm, a clean non-deflating ending. The voice mostly holds together as one person. The two things standing between this and a finished cold read are mechanical (the visible tags) and one anecdote (the software-hiring "friend") that doesn't match the specificity of everything around it.
