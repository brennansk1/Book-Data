# Listen Log — Prologue draft-v3.md

Simulated read-aloud pass, per STYLE_BIBLE.md §7 and AUDIO_SPEC.md §4/§9. Going through as sound, not as text on a page. Findings ordered by severity.

---

## 1. Reference collision: "Washington" means two different things and is never redisambiguated (severe — a listener will misunderstand the actual argument)

Three separate hits:

- Line 1: "Rachel Kudrna is a fourth-year at **Washington State University's** Elson S. Floyd College of Medicine…"
- Line 33: "The one thing I found sits upstream of all three, in **Washington** rather than in any of their offices, and it doesn't let anyone in this story off the hook."
- Line 48: "…it's set by a federal cap Congress froze in the Balanced Budget Act of 1997… **Washington's** first thaw didn't come until 2021: a thousand new slots, phased in at two hundred a year."

A reader has capitalization and a full page of context to lean on. A listener has only what's been said in the last thirty seconds. The essay opens by anchoring "Washington" to Washington *State* — a place tied to Kudrna's medical school — and then, roughly a page later, starts using the bare word "Washington" to mean the federal government in D.C., with nothing in the sentence itself (no "the federal government," no "D.C.," no "Congress" in the same clause at line 33) to force the second reading. By line 48 the payoff sentence — "Washington's first thaw didn't come until 2021" — depends entirely on the listener having correctly reassigned the word two uses earlier. If they didn't, the actual mechanism of the piece (a federal cap, not a state one) garbles. This is the single highest-value fix in the draft: reword line 33 and/or line 48 to say "the federal government" or "D.C." explicitly at least once before leaning on the bare "Washington."

## 2. Breath rule: one sentence cannot be said aloud in one breath at all (severe)

> "And several thousand people being right, all at once, in the same direction, is how you end up with a system where a woman who can't really afford it pays sixteen hundred dollars to apply to hospitals she will never see the inside of, on the chance that one of them says yes."

Fifty-three words, one main clause, no semicolon, no period until the very end. This is the longest sentence in the draft by a wide margin and it lands at the emotional climax of the ANCHOR paragraph — exactly the wrong place for the narrator to run out of air. It needs a hard break, probably after "…is how you end up with a system —" or after "…a real cost of that agreement is —" Recommend splitting into two sentences at "in the same direction" or "is how you end up with a system."

## 3. Audible tic: the tricolon/parallel-triad device repeats until it's audible as a device (severe — this is the exact failure AUDIO_SPEC §4 names)

Three separate instances of the identical rhetorical move, close enough together that a listener will start hearing the machinery rather than the meaning:

- Line 19: "The greedy program. The neurotic student. The dean who should just tell them no already."
- Line 33: "Not Sarah. Not Harrison. Not the programs."
- Line 35: "The programs aren't being greedy… The applicants aren't being neurotic… The deans aren't lying…" — three consecutive sentences opening with the identical shape, "The [group] aren't/isn't being [trait]," stretched across a full paragraph.

Plus a milder fourth: line 44's "One coding challenge became two take-home projects, then a case-study presentation…" is a three-item escalation in a single sentence — the classic tricolon the Style Bible caps explicitly.

Individually each is a fine sentence. Together, this is precisely what AUDIO_SPEC §4 warns about by name: "the tricolon is everywhere… it becomes an audible tic within twenty minutes, because the model will render the same cadence every time." A listener who has already heard two three-beat fragment-triads by the time they reach the "aren't being" paragraph will hear the pattern arrive a third time before they hear the argument. Recommend cutting the fragment triad at line 19 or line 33 (they're doing almost the same rhetorical job — "who's to blame" — twice) and breaking the parallel shape in at least one of the three "aren't being" sentences.

## 4. Breath rule: a cluster of secondary long sentences, none fatal alone but several in close range (medium)

Each of these is 28–35 words with only comma breaks, no hard stop — readable but tight, and there are enough of them that the cumulative effect on pacing is worth a pass:

- Line 5: "One program, reviewing the flood of applications that arrives every autumn, put the cost in lost faculty clinical time at close to thirty-seven hundred dollars per applicant interviewed." (28 words, and the subject/verb — "program… put" — is split by a nine-word inserted clause, which makes it harder to hold in one breath than the word count alone suggests.)
- Line 17: "Harrison is giving sound advice to a room full of people who cannot individually afford to take it, because the protection only works if you have more of it than the person next to you." (35 words, a single comma.)
- Line 19: "If you're like most people reading this, part of you is already doing what I did when I first heard about it: scanning for the one person in the story who's actually being unreasonable." (34 words.)
- Line 35: "The one thing I found sits upstream of all three, in Washington rather than in any of their offices, and it doesn't let anyone in this story off the hook." (see also Finding 1.)
- Line 40: "The application service belongs to AAMC, which collects a fee on every checkbox a student ticks, and it added a way for students to flag a handful of programs as their real top choices." (34 words.)
- Line 42: "By one accounting, neurosurgery applicants report nearly thirty research activities each, on average, and in a recent national survey, research sits among the factors program directors most often use to decide who gets an interview." (35 words.)

None of these are unreadable the way Finding 2 is, but six sentences in this range in a 1,930-word piece is a real pattern. Worth a pass to add one more hard stop to at least the three longest (lines 17, 40, 42).

## 5. Weak beat: a paragraph closes on a deflected aside (medium)

> "Plumbers don't do this to each other. Neither, as far as I've noticed, do electricians, and I have a half-built theory about licensing boards that I'm not going to drag in here."

The paragraph's actual content — the software-hiring example, the observation that this shape recurs across fields with nothing else in common — is strong. It ends on the narrator declining to finish a thought. "That I'm not going to drag in here" is the last clause a listener hears from this paragraph, and it's a throwaway, not a landing. Move the licensing-board aside earlier or cut it; end the paragraph on the plumbers/electricians image itself, which is doing real work.

## 6. Repeated cadence: the confidence-modulation closer is used twice, almost identically (medium)

- Line 40: "I think that's real progress, for what it's worth. I'm a lot less sure it holds."
- Line 50: "I'm confident about that part. I'm much less sure what anyone is supposed to do about it."

Style Bible rule 9 wants audible confidence modulation, and both instances individually do exactly what's asked. But the *shape* — short confident clause, then "I'm [degree] less sure [X]" — appears twice within about 250 words of each other, near the end of the piece. On a first read it's invisible. Read aloud, the second occurrence will land as a callback to a device rather than a fresh admission. Vary the construction of one of the two — e.g., replace line 50's version with something that doesn't scan as "confident about X, less sure about Y" restated.

## 7. Interrupted subject-verb constructions split the sentence at the wrong point, twice (low-medium)

- Line 9: "Part of her job is telling students not to do what Kudrna and her classmates are, in September, doing anyway." — the verb phrase "are… doing anyway" is broken across a three-word parenthetical, which forces an odd catch-breath exactly where the ear expects the verb to land.
- Line 33: "My first reaction, when Sarah explained the seventy-programs arithmetic to me over Christmas, was not sympathy." — nine words wedged between subject ("reaction") and verb ("was"), the longest such gap in the piece.

Neither breaks the breath rule outright (both are short overall), but both ask the listener to hold an incomplete clause open across an inserted aside — a harder task for the ear than for the eye. Consider moving the parenthetical to the front or the end of each sentence.

## 8. Homographs — checked against the AUDIO_SPEC §9 list (low, mostly clean)

Full list checked (deliberate, moderate, separate, object, present, live, read, lead, refuse, minute, content, contract, wound, use, invalid, resume, conduct, entrance, desert, bow, close, tear, sow, house, abuse). Only two live hits in this draft, both low-risk:

- Line 46: "…just a lot of **separate**, reasonable people responding to each other." Adjective use (SEP-rit), immediately following "a lot of" and modifying "people" — grammar forces the correct reading. Low risk, but flag it for the respelling table since AUDIO_SPEC §9 calls this word out by name as a constant offender elsewhere in the manuscript.
- Line 42: "…research sits among the factors program directors most often **use** to decide who gets an interview." Verb use (yooz), forced by "often use to," unambiguous in context. Low risk.

Not on the official list but a classic TTS homograph worth a note anyway: line 33, "I'm putting it on the **record** anyway" — fixed idiom ("on the record"), context makes the noun reading (REK-erd) essentially automatic. Low risk, mentioning only because it's the kind of word that occasionally trips a CSM-style model outside its most common collocation.

## 9. Quote boundaries — clean (low)

Both sourced quotations follow the attribution-before rule correctly:

- Line 7: "Kudrna puts it simply: 'There's tons of anxiety…'"
- Line 13: "Here is how Harrison herself describes watching it happen: 'It's heartbreaking…'"

One soft note: line 46 runs three scare-quoted labels in a row — "everyone loses," "nobody's fault," "the market's fault." Quotation marks are inaudible in speech; without them a listener can't tell these are the narrator's own candidate-labels being tried on and discarded rather than someone else's stated position. Context (no attribution, first person "I tried") should carry it, but it's worth a listen-check once rendered — if it reads as ambiguous, consider "what I'd call 'everyone loses'" or similar to make the framing explicit in the sentence itself, not just the punctuation.

## 10. Numbers — clean

Every figure that isn't a calendar year is already spelled out in word form (seventy, sixteen hundred dollars, thirty-seven hundred dollars, ten thousand dollars, twenty-six-year-olds, a thousand new slots, two hundred a year). The four numerals that remain — 2022, 1997, 2021, 2011 — are all calendar years, which the spec explicitly exempts from spoken-form conversion at the print-draft stage. No genuine ambiguity found.

## 11. Minor: unexplained acronym (low)

Line 40 introduces "AAMC" with no expansion — the application service (ERAS) was named at line 1, but the parent organization that owns it and charges the fee is new information dropped in as a bare acronym. Not a homograph or antecedent issue in the technical sense, but the same "listener can't glance back" logic applies: a listener has no way to look up what AAMC stands for mid-sentence. Consider "AAMC, the group that runs the whole application system" or similar on first use.

## Verdict

**FIX, not pass.** The prose is close — strong concrete detail, real named people, the confidence-modulation rule is being followed in spirit, no summary sections, quote attribution is clean, numbers are already in spoken form. But three items are load-bearing enough to block sign-off as-is: the Washington/Washington-State ambiguity actually breaks the argument for a listener (#1), the 53-word sentence is un-sayable in one breath (#2), and the tricolon/"aren't being" triad repeats often enough in 1,930 words to become the exact audible tic AUDIO_SPEC §4 warns about by name (#3). Fix those three plus the weak-beat closer (#5) and this clears Gate 5; the rest are polish-pass items, not blockers.
