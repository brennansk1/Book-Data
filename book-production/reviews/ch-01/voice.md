# Voice Curator Review — Chapter 1, draft-v2.md

Scored against `VOICE.md` §10 judgment metrics. Cross-referenced against `voice/seed/models/` (interim corpus), `voice/seed/anti/`, and `voice/IDIOLECT.md` (binding — this chapter and both frozen units must read as the same writer). `manuscript/frozen/prologue.md` and `manuscript/frozen/ch-10.md` used for cross-chapter comparison, not scored themselves.

**Overall verdict: PASS on every countable §10 metric, but with a mechanical refusal violation and a structural template-risk problem that should be fixed before Gate 3.** The chapter clears every quota — beats, costly signals, obsession, plain/high-band diction, sensory count, one-sentence paragraphs — comfortably. The real risk isn't a missing ingredient; it's that this chapter reaches for the book's two most identifiable house moves (the negation-and-correct construction, and the Prologue's specific beat *order*) harder and more literally than either frozen unit did, which is exactly the kind of thing a detection panel notices across chapters even when no single chapter fails on its own.

## 2. Costly signals — PASS, strong (found 4–5 of 6 categories, need ≥3)

- **Specific numbers/proper nouns requiring lookup:** "The quota Crosbie set for 1992, 187,969 tonnes, was higher than the entire industry's catch the year before, 129,033 tonnes" (line 19) is the strongest instance — a number that only exists because someone went and got both figures and did the subtraction. Also: Alverson's quote ("it took several years to find it, and by then, the stocks were on the downturn," line 19), Sinclair's quote (line 33), the Amazon HQ2 dollar figures (line 35–37).
- **Disagreement with someone the author is otherwise building the chapter's argument on:** the Hardin material (line 47) — "Hardin's actual target was human population growth, a much bigger and uglier argument he was building toward... a policy conclusion I don't share and won't launder by association." This is a real instance of the category (disagreeing with a thinker the chapter needs and uses), done economically.
- **A concession that materially weakens the chapter's own argument** (line 43): "I'm less sure how to weigh the model error against the political choice not to act once the error was found... I don't think the literature is fully settled either. It probably isn't one or the other cleanly, and I don't have a tidier answer than that." Real hedging, not decorative.
- **First-person admission that does not flatter the author** (line 28, ANCHOR): "I wanted it to be the fishermen... a story with an identifiable villain is so much easier to write, and easier to feel something about, than a story where the honest answer turns out to be a math error nobody caught for two years."

**On the instruction to judge real cost, not just presence:** the ANCHOR admission is the one worth being skeptical of. It clears the letter of "admission that does not flatter the author," but compare its stakes to the frozen units' anchors — the Prologue's is a live, unflattering emotional reaction to a family member ("My first reaction... was not sympathy. It was closer to irritation"); Chapter 10's is an admission of real cowardice in the writer's own life (a job, a friendship, both let slide for over a year). Chapter 1's admission is about *craft process* — the writer confesses to preferring a better story while drafting this specific chapter. That's a real and specific admission, not a generic one, and the pre-defense trim (per `reviews/ch-01/decisions.md`) already removed the worst self-congratulatory framing the cold-read flagged ("I'm telling you this not because it's a charming detail... but because it's a reflex almost everyone has"). What's left is honest but lower-stakes than either frozen anchor — it costs the writer very little to admit "I wanted a cleaner villain." Score it a pass on the letter of the quota; flag it as the softest of the book's three anchors so far, worth the Showrunner's eye if the pattern repeats.

## 3. Deliberate obsession — PASS, clean (need 1)

The Hardin/William Forster Lloyd/Ostrom digression (lines 45–53, roughly 9 of 48 paragraphs) is the candidate, and it is self-flagged almost verbatim against the spec's own language: "I'm going to spend a little longer on Hardin than the argument strictly needs, because the man himself is a mess in a way that rarely makes it into the summary" (line 47). The Lloyd/1833 detail itself — "a hundred and thirty-five years between the first telling and the famous one, and the pasture hadn't changed at all" (line 47) — is exactly the kind of thing that earns "the author finds it interesting" rather than "the argument needs it": the chapter's case doesn't require knowing who Hardin borrowed the herdsman example from. Clean pass, correctly signposted, doesn't overstay past the Ostrom material it's building toward.

## 4. Deliberate omission, named — PASS, with a landing risk (need 1)

Line 47: "Hardin's actual target was human population growth, a much bigger and uglier argument he was building toward, one this chapter isn't going to wade into, because it deserves its own honest treatment. The herdsmen were the warm-up act for a policy conclusion I don't share and won't launder by association."

This technically satisfies §1.3 — the omission is named, and the population argument is left alone. But compare it to the spec's own model ("There's a whole literature on how Ostrom's principles fail in fisheries. I'm not going to get into it") and to Chapter 10's cleanest version of the same move ("There's a whole further argument I could make here with Milgram's obedience numbers. I'm leaving it out — Challenger already makes the point"). Both of those are terse and don't editorialize about the omitted material. Chapter 1's version spends three clauses characterizing and rejecting Hardin's unstated argument ("bigger and uglier," "a policy conclusion I don't share," "won't launder by association") before moving on. That's closer to *adjudicating* the omitted material than setting it aside — it tells the reader what to think about an argument the chapter has just declined to present, which is a mild version of stating a position at a confidence the chapter hasn't earned (the book never examines Hardin's population argument, so "uglier" and "won't launder" are conclusions asserted rather than shown). It still reads as an omission rather than a dodge on a first pass — but it's the more elaborated, judgment-laden end of the device, not the clean single-clause model. Recommend trimming to something closer to: "one this chapter isn't going to wade into — it deserves its own treatment" and cutting the editorializing clause.

## 10. Idiolect conformance (voice/IDIOLECT.md — binding)

**Sentence shape 1 — deflating short paragraph after a build: PRESENT, matches, used twice.** "It wasn't." (line 25) lands immediately after the villain setup ("I want to tell you it was the crowd on that wharf... the villain the story hands you before you've even finished the first paragraph," line 23) — a near-exact structural echo of the Prologue's "Seventy." after the niece build-up. "Okay." (line 69) does the same deflating work as a case-to-case breath. Two clean instances is more than either frozen unit used in one chapter — worth noting under template-risk below.

**Sentence shape 2 — short flat declarative, then a longer complicating sentence: PRESENT, matches.** "It wasn't a conspiracy. Nobody sat in a room and decided to bury a warning. It was more like a series of people, each reasonably busy and reasonably confident in the system they worked inside, each deciding this particular red flag could wait for the next budget cycle, until there were no cycles left." (line 41) is a clean instance of the shape.

**Image family — water/hydraulics: PRESENT and well-handled, not on-the-nose.** This is the judgment call the brief flagged, since the chapter's subject matter is already literally water. The good news: the chapter mostly avoids piling literal water imagery onto an already-aquatic case, and instead redirects the *figurative* water/pressure family onto non-water targets, which is the sophisticated move — "the gauge reading full while the level underneath it kept dropping, right up until it didn't" (line 37) applies the family to a broken measurement instrument, not to the fish themselves; "the way water finds the lowest point in a room whether or not anybody built it a drain" (line 93) applies it to how blame diffuses among a crowd, directly echoing Chapter 10's "you plug one hole and the water finds a different wall" with a different vehicle rather than a repeated line. Neither instance describes the ocean or the fishery directly — the chapter earns its one shot at "water is also, literally, this chapter's subject" by refusing to spend it on the obvious target. Verdict: the imagery is working, not overdone.

**Joke shape — understatement: PRESENT.** "I've never met one that I know of" (line 28, re: fishermen), "including plenty of people who write books about ethics for a living. Including me" (line 30), and the closing "which is roughly my average with you" (line 97) are all dry, self-aimed, in the right register — arguably the strongest showing of this marker of the three chapters reviewed so far.

**Two overused words ("unreasonable," "climbing") — PRESENT, but "unreasonable" is over-deployed and "climbing" has drifted from its established sense.** "Unreasonable" appears three times in a single paragraph (line 83: "weren't being unreasonable... weren't being unreasonable... wasn't being unreasonable"), functioning as the chapter's thesis restated in the sheet's own vocabulary — good conformance in principle, but see the rationed-construction finding below, which is the same passage. "Climbing" appears twice (line 19, "couldn't climb back out"; line 83, "climbing the same ladder") but neither usage is the sheet's established sense — an escalating total, per the Prologue's "the total is still climbing" — it's repurposed for a research rabbit hole and a social-ladder metaphor. Present, recognizable as the same word, but not doing the same figurative job it does in the Prologue; a careful cross-chapter reader would notice the word recurs without quite recurring in the same way.

**Named weakness (impatience first, verification second) — PRESENT, well embodied rather than stated.** The ANCHOR enacts the named weakness rather than naming it: the writer reaches for the villain fast ("some part of me had already cast the roles before I'd read a page of the actual research," line 28), then does the slower work and reports it ("So I spent most of a week doing the slower, less satisfying work of actually checking," line 30). This is the cleanest match to the sheet's language of the three chapters reviewed — it shows the weakness in action rather than restating it as a line, which is the better craft choice.

**Refusals — ONE VIOLATION.** No exclamation marks in prose (the six `!` hits are all inside `<!-- KEEP -->` / `<!-- ANCHOR -->` HTML comment syntax). Does not open on a quotation — opens on a dated scene. But: "Find whoever's fault it obviously is. Stop looking once you've found them." (line 30) uses "obviously" — a direct, mechanical hit against IDIOLECT.md's explicit "never writes 'obviously'" refusal. It's used to characterize the *bad* reflex the passage is criticizing, which softens the intent, but the refusal is stated as an absolute stylistic tic, not one with an irony carve-out, and this is exactly the kind of thing a mechanical check (or a hostile reader who's noticed the pattern) catches regardless of authorial intent. **Fix before v3:** reword to drop the word — e.g., "Find whoever's fault it looks like. Stop looking once you've found them."

**Rationed construction ("X isn't/aren't being [vice]; [structural reason]") — borderline pass on the single cluster, but the construction recurs well beyond it.** The line-83 cluster ("The inshore fishermen weren't being unreasonable. The scientists weren't being unreasonable. Crosbie wasn't being unreasonable. Neither were the mayors...") is exactly three explicit instances plus an elided fourth ("Neither were the mayors") in a single passage that genuinely *is* the argument — this is the sheet's carved-out exception ("up to three in a single passage where it IS the argument"), and it's defensible as written. The problem is that the same negation-and-correct shape recurs as the chapter's default connective move well before that cluster, in violation of "zero stray instances elsewhere": "They're not the criminals in this story" (line 33); "It wasn't a conspiracy. Nobody sat in a room and decided to bury a warning" (line 41); "He was a Newfoundlander, not some villain shipped in from Ottawa to do the dirty work" (line 61); "Nobody in either story is stupid and nobody in it is a monster" (line 83, immediately following the sanctioned cluster, functioning as a fifth instance in the same breath). Including the deflating "It wasn't." (line 25) itself, which is the same gesture in its most compressed form, this construction appears in some form at least six times across the chapter, spread across roughly two-thirds of its length rather than contained to the one licensed cluster. This is the single largest idiolect risk in the draft — larger than the "obviously" slip, because it's pervasive rather than local, and it's the same failure mode flagged as the top risk in the Chapter 10 voice review (there at a higher multiple of the budget). Two chapters in a row over-running this specific budget is worth a standing note, not just a per-chapter fix.

## 11. Template-risk — do the three chapters deploy their beats in the same order?

**Yes, and this is the top voice risk in the chapter, ahead of the negation-construction overage.** Chapter 1's beat order in its first third tracks the Prologue's almost exactly:

*Prologue:* opening case → thesis tease ("Nobody in this is doing anything unreasonable," a one-line paragraph) → personal frame-in (niece) → deflating one-word paragraph ("Seventy.") → ANCHOR: first-person admission of a bad first instinct (irritation) → the sanctioned negation cluster (programs/applicants/deans) → causal reveal (the 1997 federal cap) → close on an open question to the reader.

*Chapter 1:* opening case → thesis tease ("I want to tell you it was the crowd on that wharf... It wasn't," a one-line paragraph) → ANCHOR: first-person admission of a bad first instinct (wanting a villain) → causal reveal (fishermen cleared, offshore fleet, broken model) → [second case, Amazon] → the sanctioned negation cluster (fishermen/scientists/Crosbie/mayors) → close on an open question to the reader, with a "tried and rejected these three labels" aside immediately before the close.

The "tried three labels and rejected each" move is the most concrete, quotable instance of this: the Prologue tries "everyone loses," "nobody's fault," and "the market's fault" and discards each in turn (frozen prologue, "There are textbook names for pieces of this... 'the market's fault,' until I noticed there wasn't really a market here"); Chapter 1 does the identical move with "everyone loses," "bad luck," and "the market" (line 93: "Not 'everyone loses'... Not 'bad luck'... I spent an afternoon trying to pin it on 'the market,' and gave that one up too"). Same three candidate labels, same order, same rejection shape, same position — a beat before the chapter's closing question to the reader. Chapter 10 does not have this device at all; its closing movement uses a different mechanism (the Jackall quote, then a return to Priya). That makes this specifically a Prologue/Chapter-1 echo, not a book-wide pattern — which is worse for template-risk purposes, since it suggests the Chapter 1 drafter (or the merge pass) leaned on the Prologue as a structural template rather than on the idiolect sheet as a set of constraints. A detection panel or attentive reader who read the Prologue and Chapter 1 back to back would very plausibly notice the "candidate labels tried and discarded" move recurring in the same slot in both chapters, even with proper nouns stripped.

Recommend: restructure the label-rejection close in Chapter 1 (different candidate labels, different order, or cut the device entirely and let the closing question stand alone — Chapter 10 doesn't need it) so the move doesn't read as a repeated formula the third time it appears in the book.

## 12. Same-writer verdict vs. both frozen units

**Mostly yes — the sentence-level fingerprint is consistent and confident — with three tells a careful reader would catch, all flagged above.**

What matches cleanly: both signature sentence shapes, the joke shape (strongest showing yet), the named weakness (best-embodied showing yet), the water/pressure image family used with real discipline rather than redundantly, the costly-signal instinct, the bursty paragraph rhythm, no institutional "we," no summary section, no numbered exposition.

What a blind reader tracking both books' patterns would notice: (1) "obviously" appears once, a direct hit against a refusal both frozen units hold cleanly; (2) the negation-and-correct construction runs through roughly two-thirds of the chapter rather than staying in its one licensed cluster — the same failure Chapter 10's review flagged as its top risk, now appearing a second consecutive chapter running; (3) the label-rejection close is a near-verbatim structural repeat of the Prologue's, in the same position, which is the more damaging finding because it's about chapter architecture rather than word choice — exactly what the detection panel is built to catch at the end of Part One.

None of these would make a reader suspect two different authors — if anything, the opposite: the repetition is evidence of one very consistent authorial hand leaning on its own favorite moves. But "consistent" and "not yet distinguishable chapter to chapter" are different properties, and the second one is what a hostile detection panel actually tests for.
