## 1. The altitude move doesn't survive contact with its own repair history (strongest objection)

**The claim, line 55:** "A geographer named Patricia Marchak looked at this exact case and argued it isn't really a Hardin-style commons at all, but a tragedy of state mismanagement... I don't think that's a competing account. I think it's the same structure, one floor up... The trap didn't spare the institution that existed to prevent it. It just caught that institution wearing a different uniform."

This is not a new problem the draft is encountering for the first time — it's the exact seam `cold-read.md` §1 flagged in draft-v1 ("Pick the label you like... a shrug where the chapter needs a distinction"), and `decisions.md`'s cold-review repair line says explicitly: "label-shrug replaced with the altitude resolution." So the production history *already* concedes the v1 version didn't work. The question for Gate 3 is whether v2's fix is a real resolution or a more confident restatement of the same evasion — and it's the second one. v1 said "pick the label you like" (honest about not having adjudicated it). v2 says "I think it's the same structure, one floor up" (asserts identity) without adjudicating it either. That's not a fix, it's a confidence upgrade on an unearned claim — which is a worse failure mode for a book whose stated thesis is calibrated confidence (Standing Instruction #2), not a better one.

Here is the actual math, since the chapter claims "the arithmetic barely changes" without running it:

- **Hardin's structure is an n-player externality.** Each of *n* symmetric herdsmen privately captures the full marginal benefit of one more animal and bears only *1/n* of the shared marginal cost. Ruin follows because no individual herdsman's restraint changes the outcome — everyone else keeps grazing. The chapter has *already correctly identified this instantiation in the case*, at line 35: the post-1977 offshore dragger fleet, "each company running boats it was, from where it sat, entirely reasonable to run." Multiple firms, each capturing private benefit, each bearing a fraction of a shared cost. That's Hardin's shape, textbook.
- **DFO's decision is not that game.** It is a single actor — one department, one minister — facing a repeated temptation to defer a concentrated, attributable, near-term political cost (four hundred communities, an election-year mass layoff) in exchange for deferring a diffuse, probabilistic, future cost (a stock collapse that arrives on nobody's specific watch). There is no *n*; there is no rival department racing DFO for the same stock, which is precisely what the prompt for this review flags and what the chapter never addresses. This is the standard political-economy structure of concentrated-benefits-diffuse-costs regulatory capture (Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action*; Wilson's regulatory politics typology) layered onto ordinary political myopia and electoral time horizons — a fundamentally different causal mechanism from a multi-agent commons, even though both produce "individually reasonable, collectively ruinous" outcomes.

Both are real, both are honest, both are structurally interesting. But they are not "the same structure, one floor up" — they are two different structures that happen to share a family resemblance (dispersed cost, concentrated private benefit to the decision-maker) and produce a similar-sounding verdict. Calling them identical is exactly the move POS-11's own known vulnerability warns against: "the partition may not be clean in practice... a taxonomy that requires assigning a dominant axis to compound cases risks inconsistent application." This chapter *is* that compound case, in miniature, and instead of naming the compounding, it flattens it into one label for the sake of a clean sentence. A reader trained in either game theory or public-choice economics will hear "same structure" and immediately ask "same how — same players, same information structure, same payoff matrix?" and the chapter has no answer, because it never specifies what "the structure" is with enough precision to check the claim. The brief's stated argument — "these situations have *a precise mathematical shape*" (`CHAPTER_BRIEFS.md`) — is the thing being sold here, and this is the paragraph where the chapter is least entitled to sell it.

**Disposition: fix.** Don't cut the Marchak beat — it's the chapter's most sophisticated moment and the case genuinely does operate at two levels. Instead, name the two levels as two different mechanisms that compound rather than as one mechanism recurring: the offshore fleet is the commons (many actors, shared stock); DFO's fifteen years of non-cutting is a *second, different* trap — a political-agency problem, concentrated-cost/diffuse-benefit, layered on top of the first. That's a stronger and more honest version of the chapter's own point (coordination failure operating at multiple levels, which is what `evidence.md` §3c.4 actually supports — "the failure operating at multiple levels... rather than one flat plane") without claiming a precision the text hasn't earned. This also sets up Ch. 2 and later Axis 1 material more honestly than a fudged identity claim would.

## 2. HQ2: the chapter states a contested economic claim as if the profession agreed

**Line 77:** "Economists had been warning about exactly this kind of competition for years before Amazon published its list of criteria. It hadn't slowed anything down, and I don't think it was ever going to."

This sentence does real work in the chapter — it's the pivot from "here's what happened" to "and it was foreseeable, which is the whole point." But it launders a genuinely divided literature into a unanimous one. Economists have not spoken with one voice on interstate/intercity tax and subsidy competition: there is a substantial strand of work (going back to Oates-style fiscal federalism arguments, and continuing through the Tiebout-competition tradition) holding that jurisdictional bidding can be an efficient information-revelation and surplus-transfer mechanism rather than pure waste, alongside the more prominent "race to the bottom" critique (Peter Fisher and Good Jobs First's incentive-tracking work, for instance) that the chapter's framing assumes without stating. The chapter is entitled to think the race-to-the-bottom reading is the better one — plenty of economists do — but Standing Instruction #2 doesn't ask for the *popular* reading, it asks the text to say the literature is divided where it is divided, and this is one of the places it is.

More damaging: the chapter's closing beat on the case — New York's withdrawal, "Half the announced prize, gone" (line 75) — is framed unambiguously as the city dodging a bad deal, with AOC as the opposition's voice. It never mentions that this is itself a live, contested empirical question: multiple economic analyses in the years after the withdrawal argued New York forwent tens of thousands of jobs and a larger tax-revenue stream than the subsidy cost it, i.e., that the "win" the chapter implicitly credits the opposition with may have been a net loss for the city on its own terms. The chapter doesn't need to resolve that dispute — Ch. 1 isn't the place to relitigate HQ2 — but presenting the withdrawal as a clean, uncomplicated escape from a trap, in a chapter whose entire argument is "look how much better this reads once you refuse the easy version," is the same flattening move the chapter spent forty lines refusing to make about cod. `research/ch-01/evidence.md` §5 doesn't flag this either — it never surfaces the tax-competition efficiency debate or the NYC-net-loss analyses at all, which means the Researcher's packet has the same gap. That's worth a note back to the Researcher regardless of what the Drafter does with it here.

**Disposition: concede, in one sentence.** After line 75 or folded into line 77, something in voice acknowledging the case isn't unanimous — e.g., that some economists think competitive bidding isn't pure waste, and that whether New York came out ahead by walking away is itself argued both ways. This doesn't cost the chapter its argument (the *individually-reasonable-collectively-bad* structure survives either reading of who won), and it converts a place a hostile economist would flag as overconfidence into a demonstration of the exact calibration habit the book claims to model.

## 3. The Hardin handling: substantially earned, but the disavowal is doing less work than it looks like it's doing

Lines 45–53 are the chapter's most self-aware stretch, and credit where due: it names that Hardin borrowed the herdsman from Lloyd (1833), it uses the *verified* 1998 walk-back passage rather than the popular misquote `evidence.md` §2c specifically warns against, and it explicitly declines to follow Hardin into the population argument: "Hardin's actual target was human population growth, a much bigger and uglier argument he was building toward... The herdsmen were the warm-up act for a policy conclusion I don't share and won't launder by association."

But notice what that sentence actually does: it disavows Hardin's *conclusion* while continuing to use, at full rhetorical strength, the *vehicle* he built to sell that conclusion. The herdsman-and-pasture image wasn't a neutral illustration Hardin happened to reach for — on the chapter's own account, it was the "warm-up act," constructed to make ruin feel arithmetically inevitable before the population argument arrived. A hostile reader knows this and will ask: if the vehicle was built to launder the conclusion, what does it mean to take the vehicle at face value while refusing the conclusion? "Won't launder by association" is stated as if the laundering risk ends there, but the chapter still spends the load-bearing quote of its entire opening act ("Ruin is the destination toward which all men rush...") on the same construction, unqualified, four lines before the aside about population even arrives (line 45 vs. the eventual walk-back at line 49). The order of operations — quote the vehicle at full strength, *then* flag what the vehicle was built for — is the "convenient" reading of the setting-aside the review brief asks about: it gets the persuasive force of the fable first and the disclaimer second, which is exactly backwards from what "honest" would look like if honesty were the only goal.

This is a real but modest objection — the chapter does more disclosure work here than almost any comparable trade book bothers with, and the 1998 quote is correctly the verified passage, not the popular fake one. I don't think this one survives to "laughed out of the seminar," but a sharp discussant would press on it.

**Disposition: concede, lightly.** No structural change needed. One clause acknowledging that the vehicle and the conclusion aren't as separable as "warm-up act" implies — something short, in voice, immediately after "won't launder by association" — closes the gap without turning this into a Hardin biography.

## 4. The CPUE compression may have merged two different technical findings into one clean story

Lines 37–39 compress two distinct claims from `evidence.md` §3a into a single seamless mechanism:

1. The general methodological point from Myers, Hutchings & Barrowman (1997): catch-per-unit-effort is a bad abundance proxy once fishing technology improves, because a shrinking stock can still be caught at a steady rate, masking decline. The chapter renders this well and accurately — the "gauge reading full while the level underneath it kept dropping" image is a genuinely good de-jargoned rendering of a real mechanism, and `cold-read.md` §5 already flagged and the repair fixed the earlier version's unglossed phrasing.
2. The specific "mathematical fault" D. L. Alverson's 1987 audit found — which, per the evidence packet, involved fish mortality being underestimated and the model over-relying on a cohort it expected for future recruitment, not simply "CPUE goes stale." These are related but not identical findings, from different work (Myers et al. is 1997; Alverson is 1987, and the Alverson quote is itself flagged in `evidence.md` §3a as **REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE, two removes from primary** — not yet verified against Harris 1998, p. 104).

The chapter's prose treats these as one continuous story — CPUE illusion, then Alverson's audit "found a mathematical fault" — with no seam between them, which reads to a general audience as a single clean technical narrative but compresses two separate findings from two separate papers, one of which the Researcher explicitly hasn't verified against its primary source yet. That's a Gate 4 (Verifier) problem as much as a Gate 3 one, but it's exactly the kind of place a fisheries scientist in the room would ask "wait, is that the same error, or are you running two findings together because they rhyme?" — and right now the chapter can't answer that question, because it isn't posing it to itself.

**Disposition: fix, small.** Doesn't require new research or new words, just precision: attribute the CPUE-masking mechanism to the general methodological point and the "mathematical fault" specifically to what Alverson's audit found, rather than letting the second read as elaboration of the first. If the distinction turns out not to matter once Alverson is verified against Harris (1998) p. 104, this becomes a non-issue at Gate 4 — but the chapter shouldn't get ahead of a verification its own Researcher flagged as outstanding.

## 5. "One of the main engines" is the whole book's foundational scope claim, and it's asserted, not supported

Line 21: "I don't think that anymore. I think it's one of the main engines of the worst stuff that happens." This is the chapter's rendering of the brief's stated argument — "a large share of the world's avoidable suffering comes from situations where individually reasonable choices produce collectively terrible outcomes" (`CHAPTER_BRIEFS.md`) — and POS-11 rates the three-axis diagnosis this chapter opens STRONG, with a live vulnerability about compound-case cleanliness (see objection 1). The scope claim itself — that coordination failure of this specific shape is a *main engine*, as opposed to *an* engine among several comparably-sized ones (deliberate cruelty, poverty, disease, war fought for ideological rather than coordination reasons, circle failure per Axis 2 itself) — rests on exactly two cases in this chapter and a first-person conversion narrative ("I used to think that kind of case was rare... I don't think that anymore"). No evidence packet claim, no citation, no comparative weighting against the other candidate main engines is offered anywhere in the read material.

This may be entirely defensible as the book's founding intuition rather than a claim requiring citation at this point in the argument — Ch. 1 is allowed to assert the thesis it will spend the rest of the book supporting. But the *rhetorical register* the claim is delivered in ("I think it's one of the main engines of the worst stuff that happens") reads as considered, weighed judgment rather than as a flagged hypothesis, and nothing in the surrounding text signals "this is the claim the next seventeen chapters exist to earn" rather than "this is already established." A reader with any background in moral psychology or history of atrocity (the same reader POS-11's Axis 2 material will later need) will clock this immediately: most of the canonical worst things humans have done to each other are not coordination failures in this chapter's sense at all — they're coordination *successes* against an excluded group, which is Axis 2's entire reason for existing, not Axis 1's. Stating the scope claim this early, this confidently, without a "this is Axis 1's share of the story, not the whole story" gesture, risks the chapter over-claiming for its own axis before the taxonomy that would discipline the claim has even been introduced.

**Disposition: concede, one clause.** Not a structural problem — just needs a scope-limiting gesture, in voice, signaling this is a hypothesis under construction rather than a settled tally (e.g., something acknowledging he hasn't weighed it against the uglier kind of failure, the kind with an actual villain, which is coming). That also sets up Axis 2 for later chapters instead of implicitly crowding it out.
