# Voice Curator Review — Chapter 10 ("Three Gates"), draft-v2.md

Scored against `VOICE.md` §10 judgment metrics. Cross-referenced against `voice/seed/models/` (interim corpus), `voice/seed/anti/`, and `voice/IDIOLECT.md` (binding — this chapter and the Prologue must read as the same writer). `manuscript/prologue/draft-v3.md` used for cross-chapter comparison only, not scored itself.

**Overall verdict: PASS on the countable §10 metrics, with two idiolect-level problems that need a Line Editor / Showrunner pass before Gate 3.** The named omission (Milgram) does not land clean — it over-explains and functions as a tease. And the chapter's connective tissue leans on the "X isn't/wasn't [thing]; it's actually [structural reason]" negation-and-correct move far past the budget IDIOLECT.md sets for it — this is the single largest voice risk in the draft, bigger than the Milgram issue, because it's pervasive rather than local.

---

## 1. Beat sentences — PASS (found 5–6, need ≥4)

In a technical chapter these matter most, and this one places them well — mostly as scene-grounding rather than as filler:

1. "The memo went into the file." (line 5) — pure consequence-of-nothing; the reader is meant to sit with the fact that a written warning changed nothing.
2. "The switch itself was small enough to fit in a shirt pocket." (line 17) — a physical detail that carries no argument, placed right after the GM ignition-switch numbers to let the reader's attention breathe.
3. "That part is a small, human detail I don't want to lose: auditors working after hours by lamp light, checking a set of books they were not supposed to be checking yet." (line 77) — self-flagged beat sentence; the author names it as one, which is a nice, honest move.
4. "I never found out how Priya's patient did." (line 95) — one-sentence paragraph, pure beat, closes nothing argumentatively.
5. "She didn't say, and I didn't push. Some stories a friend tells you are for the story, not for the ending. You learn that after a while." (line 97) — reflective aside with no load-bearing claim.

"Lund changed his vote." (line 11) is arguably a sixth — it's the plot's hinge, so it's not purely beat by the letter of §1.1, but it does the same *deflating-short-paragraph* work as the Prologue's "Seventy." (see idiolect §12 below).

## 2. Costly signals — PASS, strong (found 5–6 of 6 categories, need ≥3)

- **Specific numbers/dates/proper nouns requiring lookup:** dense throughout — the O-ring flight-history floor of 53°F, the Boisjoly memo language, the GM "switch from hell" internal memo, the $3.8B WorldCM figure, Loewenstein's 1996 hot-cold empathy gap paper, Buxtun's November 1966 and 1968 filings, the National Research Act of 1974. This is the chapter's dominant costly-signal category, appropriately for a technical chapter that needs to earn trust on facts.
- **First-person admission that does not flatter the author** (line 56, ANCHOR): "Twice in the last five years I have used procedural language — *I need more information, I need to think it through properly, I don't want to act rashly* — to describe what was actually a decision I was too scared to make." Genuinely costly — names cowardice, not a forgivable flaw.
- **A named prediction with a date attached** (line 91): "I'll put a real date on a guess, so you can hold me to it: by 2036, at least half of the twenty largest U.S. hospital systems will have a published policy for a reporting channel that bypasses the ordinary chain of command entirely." Checkable, specific, real risk of being wrong.
- **A concession that materially weakens the chapter's own argument** (line 91): "I suspect a version of these gates would have slowed down some of the good rule-breakers of history along with all the Mulloys, and I don't have a clean way to make it slow down only the second group." This concedes the central mechanism has a real cost the chapter can't resolve.
- **Disagreement with someone the author treats with respect** (line 93, Jackall): "I think he's too cynical... most days I'm not fully sure he's wrong and I'm right." Softer than a clean "admired" disagreement but functionally the same move.

Comfortably clears the floor, and this chapter's version of the metric leans harder on the researched-fact category than the Prologue's did — appropriate to a "technical extreme" chapter per PRODUCTION_BIBLE §8.

## 3. Deliberate obsession — PASS, but under-signaled (need 1)

The candidate is the institutional-capture apparatus: Buxtun/Tuskegee, Cooper/WorldCom, and the subsequent role-by-role breakdown (soldier / civil servant / physician / private citizen) for how the gates' "hardest condition" varies by scaffolding. This runs from roughly line 65 through line 87 — close to 40% of the chapter — and is well past what the chapter's stated thesis (three gates, five conditions, illustrated by Priya) strictly requires.

The caveat: unlike the Prologue's research-arms-race tangent, which read as pure authorial interest, this section does real argumentative work (it's answering Priya's "bureaucracy applied to conscience" objection with the false-negative case). That makes it harder to cleanly separate "obsession" from "the chapter's second half." Score it a pass, but it's the same "weakest of the eight metrics to call with confidence" caveat the Prologue review flagged for its own obsession call.

## 4. Deliberate omission, named — MARGINAL FAIL (need 1; verify it lands as omission, not tease)

Line 63: "There's a whole further argument I could make here with Milgram's obedience numbers — the ordinary, decent people who delivered what they believed were dangerous shocks because a man in a lab coat told them to continue. I'm going to leave it out. The clean 65 percent figure everyone quotes is one condition out of roughly two dozen Milgram ran, and archival work done decades later complicated even that. Challenger already makes the point, and I don't want to borrow trouble from a data set with an asterisk on it."

This does not land as the spec's model omission. Compare the Prologue's version, line 44: *"There's a whole separate literature on why some professions never get this bad, and I'm not going to get into it here."* — one clause, no content delivered, immediately dropped. VOICE §1.3's own example is the same shape: *"There's a whole literature on how Ostrom's principles fail in fisheries. I'm not going to get into it."*

Ch-10's version is four sentences long, and the back half doesn't just announce the omission — it delivers content about the omitted material (the 65% figure, "roughly two dozen conditions," the archival controversy). A reader comes away knowing more about Milgram than about most things the chapter *does* include. This reads as a tease dressed as a refusal: it wants credit for restraint while still cashing in the interesting fact. It's well-written and the reasoning ("Challenger already makes the point") is sound, but the execution violates "named in a single clause and left alone." **Fix: cut to something closer to** "There's a whole further argument I could make with Milgram's obedience numbers. I'm leaving it out — Challenger already makes the point." Everything after "leave it out" in the current draft should go, or move to a footnote/cut entirely.

## 12. Idiolect conformance (voice/IDIOLECT.md — binding)

Checked against all six §2 categories, plus the book-specific seventh item (the rationed negation-and-correct construction).

**Sentence shape 1 — deflating short paragraph after a build: PRESENT, matches.** "Lund changed his vote." (line 11) directly reproduces the move ("Seventy." in the Prologue) — a short paragraph landing right after a tense build (the thirty-minute caucus). Slightly less minimal than "Seventy." (four words vs. one) but unmistakably the same tic.

**Sentence shape 2 — short flat declarative, then a longer sentence that complicates: PRESENT, matches.** Multiple instances: "That's outcome bias, and it's exactly backward." → longer complicating sentence follows (line 21). "Here's the admission, and it isn't a comfortable one." → the long ANCHOR sentence follows (line 56). Reads as the same writer.

**Image family — PRESENT, but heavily rebalanced toward engineering.** IDIOLECT.md specifies water/hydraulics as the *first* reach for describing how traps behave, with engineering (fences, firmware, gates) "available." This chapter inverts that ratio hard: "gate(s)," "trigger," "channel," "architecture," "scaffolding," "mechanism," and "threshold" appear roughly 40 times combined, against a single clear water/hydraulic callback — "each condition, taken alone, has a hole the next one exists to plug" (line 43), which nicely echoes the Prologue's "you plug one hole and the water finds a different wall." No third family is introduced, so it stays inside the "don't mix more than two" budget, and the tilt toward engineering is defensible given the chapter is literally titled *Three Gates* and its whole apparatus (trigger/delay/review) is an engineering structure. But it means a reader moving from Prologue to Chapter 10 experiences a real shift in which family is dominant, with only one deliberate bridge-image connecting them. Recommend at least one more explicit water-image touch describing how the *institutional-capture* trap behaves (currently rendered as "It was a mirror," line 69 — an engineering/reflective image, not water) to keep the two chapters' families from reading as simply "different chapters, different metaphors."

**Joke shape — understatement: PRESENT, but faint.** "I don't want to borrow trouble from a data set with an asterisk on it" (line 63) and "most days I'm not fully sure he's wrong and I'm right" (line 93) are dry-understatement in the right register, but this chapter's joke shape is quieter and less structurally load-bearing than the Prologue's ("that's on brand for me," "I think that's real progress, for what it's worth"). Present, not absent — but a Line Editor should confirm at least one clearer instance survives revision, since this is the thinnest of the confirmed markers.

**Two overused words ("unreasonable," "climbing") — ABSENT. This is a real gap.** Neither word appears anywhere in the chapter (confirmed by direct search: zero hits for "unreasonable," zero for "climb"/"climbing"). This matters more than a simple miss because the chapter's own material is a near-perfect match for the Prologue's "nobody here is being unreasonable" pattern — Challenger and the GM switch are both explicitly framed as chains of "individually reasonable-sounding calls" (line 17) and people "staying inside their lane" (line 17) — the identical thematic move, minus the actual word. This is the easiest of the flagged gaps to fix and probably should be: "reasonable" without "unreasonable" reads as the same idea in different clothes, which a careful reader tracking the book's vocabulary would notice.

**Named weakness — DIVERGED.** IDIOLECT.md locks in one specific self-named flaw: "impatience first, verification second... the writer reaches a judgment fast, then does the slower work and reports the correction on the page." The Prologue's ANCHOR names exactly this. Chapter 10's ANCHOR (line 56) names a *different* flaw — using procedural/rigor language to disguise fear-based avoidance ("I'm being rigorous" as a nicer story than "I'm being a coward"). Both are genuine costly signals and both are well-written, but they are not the same named weakness recurring, and IDIOLECT.md is explicit that this should be *one* weakness, carried "in the Coda as a costly signal" — implying a single recognizable thread across the book, not a new self-diagnosis per chapter. There is a thematic echo (the whole chapter argues for building external checks against exactly the kind of fast, self-trusting judgment the "impatience" flaw describes), but the chapter never makes that connection explicit, so the echo will not read to a blind reader as the *same* weakness — it will read as a second, unrelated one. **Flag for the Showrunner:** either rewrite the ch-10 ANCHOR to tie back to impatience/verification explicitly, or make an affirmative decision that the named weakness gets to evolve/accumulate across chapters (in which case IDIOLECT.md should say so).

**Refusals — PRESENT, clean.** Zero exclamation marks in prose (all four `!` matches are inside `<!--` HTML comment syntax, not punctuation). Zero uses of "obviously." Does not open on a quotation — opens on a dated scene ("On the evening of January 27, 1986..."). All three refusals hold exactly as specified.

**Rationed construction ("X isn't/aren't being [vice]; [structural reason]") — FAILS THE BUDGET. This is the top voice risk in the chapter.** IDIOLECT.md calls this "the book's central rhetorical gesture and its most dangerous tic" and sets an explicit budget: *one cluster per chapter (up to three instances in a single passage where it IS the argument), zero stray instances elsewhere.* This chapter runs the negation-and-correct shape as its default connective move throughout, not as one contained cluster. Confirmed instances, scattered across the whole draft rather than concentrated: "Nobody in that chain was covering up a crime. Each person was applying a category..." (line 17); "Not through some rogue official ignoring the rules. Through the review process itself..." (line 71); "It doesn't ask *is this justified*... It asks something cruder, and earlier" (line 25); "That line ran toward the very people implicated... [she] took it to Max Bobbitt..." (line 79, same shape via contrast rather than direct negation); plus smaller instances at lines 23, 27, 31, 35, 41, 61, 69, 87, 97, 99. That is well over a dozen occurrences of the family across the chapter — an order of magnitude past "zero stray instances elsewhere." Some of these are legitimate (the GM-switch passage at line 17 is arguably the chapter's one earned cluster, doing exactly the argumentative work the budget carves out room for), but the rest read as the writer's reflexive default sentence-joint rather than a deliberate, rationed gesture — which is precisely the failure mode the Prologue's own voice review flagged as "the anti-corpus's own stated cap," and this chapter blows well past even that looser bar. **Recommend: keep the GM-switch cluster (line 17) as the one sanctioned instance; convert most of the others to plain declaratives in the Line Edit pass.**

## 13. Cross-chapter verdict — would a blind reader say the same person wrote both?

**Mostly yes, with two tells a careful (not casual) reader would catch.**

What matches cleanly: the sentence-level habits (both signature shapes present and recognizable), the refusal set (no exclamation marks, no "obviously," no cold-open-on-a-quote), the costly-signal instinct (a real, unflattering personal admission placed at the ANCHOR), the beat-sentence discipline, the sensory grounding extending past the opening scene, the loose/bursty paragraph rhythm (short one-liners next to 180+ word paragraphs), and the general register — first-person, direct, willing to say "I don't know," no institutional "we," no summary section, no numbered lists carrying the argument.

What a blind reader tracking vocabulary across both chapters would notice: (1) the total absence of "unreasonable" and "climbing" in a chapter built on the identical "everyone locally reasonable, collectively bad" pattern the Prologue uses those exact words for; (2) two different self-named flaws at the two ANCHOR points, with no bridge between them; (3) the negation-and-correct gesture, used sparingly and pointedly in the Prologue (already flagged there as a risk at 5 instances in 1,754 words), running roughly 3x that density here with no equivalent containment. None of these would make a reader suspect two different authors outright — the sentence-level fingerprint is too consistent for that — but a reader who'd just finished the Prologue and started Chapter 10 back to back would plausibly notice the vocabulary discontinuity and the rhetorical-tic density shift, which is exactly the kind of thing the detection panel (Gate 6) is built to catch later if it isn't fixed now.
