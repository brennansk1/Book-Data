## 1. Mawelle: the chapter tells a capture story when its own numbers tell a population-pressure story first (strongest objection)

**Lines 57–65.** The chapter's turn on Mawelle is explicitly causal: "None of it saved them" pivots straight into the colonial cap (32 nets), "enforcement lapsed," a climb to 71 nets by the mid-1940s, three ignored petitions, and then the hinge — "**Then** in 1964 a man named David Mahattea... went around the local official... got his own Member of Parliament to authorize new licenses anyway." The chapter's own gloss makes the causal claim explicit: "A politician with a favor to sell walked in from outside, and the local rules had no standing against him." The whole passage is built to make Mahattea's political capture the explanation for Mawelle's failure.

Do the numbers in `cases.md` support that? Run the arithmetic the chapter gives you the ingredients for but never does itself:

- Legal cap: 32 nets (1933).
- By 1945: 71 nets — driven, per `cases.md` line 282–283, by **"entrepreneurial pressure (population growth, a new road, an ice factory, a fourfold rise in fish prices 1938–41)"** — not by anyone's political intervention. This is a straightforward population/demand-pressure story: fish got valuable, people got poor enough (or ambitious enough) to want in, and an unenforced cap didn't hold.
- By 1964, pre-Mahattea: 84 nets, per `cases.md` line 291 ("24 new nets were constructed and added to the 84 nets then in operation"). The chapter never states this number — it only implies it by backing out the arithmetic from the final total (108 − 24 = 84) — which means the reader never sees that **the beach was already at more than 2.5× the legal cap and roughly 3–4× carrying capacity nineteen years before Mahattea shows up.** What happened in that 1945–1964 gap is never narrated. If it's the same economic/demographic pressure that drove 32 → 71 (the packet gives no reason to think it's anything else — no second political-capture event is documented for that period), then Mahattea's faction fight accounts for 24 of the roughly 76 nets of total overcapacity — **about a third.** The other two-thirds accumulated before he arrives, under exactly the same "enforcement lapsed" conditions the chapter already narrates as background, not as the villain of the story.

That's the shape of the *simpler* reading the assignment asked me to test: population pressure was already doing most of the damage; the political-capture episode is the dramatic, nameable last act, not the majority cause. The chapter's own instinct not to flatten this — "Nobody in that village was foolish or lazy or bad at cooperation" — is right and worth keeping. But naming Mahattea as *the* answer to "what broke this" when the packet's own numbers show most of the overcapacity predates him is the same flattening move Ch. 1 refused to make about cod, applied here in the opposite direction: it finds a villain because a villain is a better scene than "nobody enforced a number for two decades and prices kept rising."

**A second, smaller loss in the same passage:** `cases.md` records petitions in **1940, 1942, and 1945**, plus **a 1946 petition that succeeded temporarily** before the gains were lost again. The chapter's "petitioned three separate times... and mostly got nowhere; each petition went into an office and nothing came back out of it" (line 61) drops the 1946 partial win entirely. That's not a small compression — "we asked and were ignored every time" and "we asked, won once, and then lost it anyway" are different indictments of the state-above-the-village layer the chapter is building toward (line 67's Principle 8 payoff). The second version is the more honest and, frankly, the more damning one for the chapter's own argument: it shows the outside layer *could* respond and chose not to sustain the response, which is a sharper indictment of unreliable subsidiarity than "nothing ever came back out of the office."

**Disposition: fix.** Don't cut Mawelle — it's the chapter's honesty case and it's the right one (Ostrom's own Table 5.2 makes it the more interesting failure, per `evidence.md` §8, precisely because Mawelle got more right than Bodrum/Izmir before collapsing). But the causal frame needs a sentence acknowledging that most of the overcapacity was already there — driven by fish prices and population, not by anyone's politics — before Mahattea's name enters the story; he's the proximate trigger for the final, violent escalation, not the whole explanation. Restore the 1946 temporary win if word count allows; it's a better, more specific indictment than the current flat "nothing came back out of it."

## 2. "The conditions... are known" claim is inconsistent with the chapter's own hedge, and dodges the selection-bias objection entirely

**Line 21:** "The conditions under which it holds together aren't folklore. Somebody did the fieldwork. Somebody counted the fines." **Line 73:** "I'm confident the boundary-monitoring-sanctions bundle is real and it generalizes. I'm much less sure how far the tiny-fines finding travels past a group small enough that everyone already knows everyone's face."

Here's the seminar-room problem: Ostrom's design principles — all eight, including the three the chapter builds Valencia around — were derived from a corpus of CPR cases that were **already long-enduring** at the time she studied them. That's the selection: you don't get into *Governing the Commons*'s success column unless you already survived long enough to be studied. This is a textbook case-selection-on-the-dependent-variable problem, and it's a real, standing critique of the design-principles literature (the failures in her Chapter 5 are a genuine partial corrective — Mawelle and Bodrum/Izmir show she wasn't only counting survivors — but the *principles themselves*, the eight-item list the chapter is built around, are read off the successes, not derived from a matched comparison of communities that tried the same rules and failed anyway for unrelated reasons). Nowhere in the chapter is this named. Ostrom's own hedge, which `evidence.md` §1 flags as "worth pointing to directly" — **"This list of design principles is still quite speculative. I am not yet willing to argue that these design principles are necessary conditions for achieving institutional robustness"** — sits right there in the research packet and never makes it into the chapter's own confident register at line 73.

More damaging, the chapter's own two-tier hedge doesn't survive its own logic. Line 73 says the *fine-size* finding is scope-limited to small, face-to-face groups, but grants the *boundary-monitoring bundle* full generalization. But the monitoring mechanism the chapter spends its best paragraphs on (lines 35–39: "a farmer waiting his turn... nothing to do but watch") is **exactly as dependent on small-N, repeat, face-to-face interaction as the fine-size finding is** — reciprocal monitoring only works as a free byproduct of the queue when the queue is short enough that everyone recognizes everyone else in it. There's no principled reason given for why "monitoring generalizes" survives past a hundred farmers while "tiny fines generalize" doesn't; a hostile reader will notice the hedge was placed to protect the chapter's most surprising number (the pennies) rather than applied consistently to the mechanism that actually has the tighter scope condition. Ostrom herself, and the CPR literature since (group size and heterogeneity of interests are recurring variables in her own later collaborative work), treats group size as a live variable for the *whole* bundle, not just the sanction schedule.

**Disposition: fix.** Two moves, both small: (1) extend the existing hedge at line 73 to cover monitoring, not just fine-size — there's no defensible line between them; (2) one sentence, in voice, naming that Ostrom's own case set is a set of survivors, and that the chapter can't rule out that some communities tried the identical eight-part bundle and failed anyway for reasons the surviving cases can't show you, because they aren't in the sample. This doesn't cost the chapter its argument — the Mawelle case already partially answers it by showing a case that got most of the bundle right and still failed for an *external* reason — but it should be said as a limitation of the method, not just illustrated once via Mawelle and left for the reader to generalize on their own.

## 3. Valencia's numbers are close to the best case for monitoring, and the chapter's language overshoots what two years of Castellón data can support

**Lines 9, 17.** Line 9 is careful — "The clearest surviving numbers come not from Valencia's own huerta but from its close neighbor, Castellón" — matching `evidence.md` §4's explicit instruction not to silently collapse Castellón's fine-book statistics into Valencia's own record. Good; that discipline holds through the anchor block at line 26 too ("the fine books of Castellón, a huerta town up the coast from Valencia").

But line 17 lets the discipline slip: "She went looking in the archive Glick's fine books had left behind, and in the wider Valencia scholarship built on top of them, and found the same pattern on page after page. Hundreds of infractions a year. Almost all of them minor. Fines too small to matter, and **compliance, across centuries of record-keeping, running close to total.**" The evidence packet supports exactly **two documented years** of Castellón fine-book data (1443: 441 fines; 1486: 499 fines) — not "centuries of record-keeping," and not a computed compliance rate at all. There is no denominator anywhere in `evidence.md` (total turns taken, total gate-openings, total opportunities to cheat) that would let anyone compute "close to total" compliance from a fine count. "Hundreds of infractions a year, almost all minor" is what the packet supports. "Compliance running close to total across centuries" is an inference the chapter states as if it were the same finding, and it isn't — it's an extrapolation from two data points to "centuries," dressed as continuation of the sourced claim two clauses earlier.

There's a second, harder version of this same objection, and it's the one an actual development economist reaches for: **Valencia's huerta is close to the easiest possible monitoring environment a commons could have.** Water theft in a gravity-fed canal is visually unmissable — the farmer downstream *cannot fail to notice* his water didn't arrive — and exclusion is cheap because the physical infrastructure (a gate, a known rotation) does the enforcement work for free. The chapter comes close to admitting this without ever quite saying it: line 35, "monitoring is the line item that eats the budget... Nobody is paid to watch. Watching is just what waiting looks like" — this is presented as the system's cleverness, not flagged anywhere as the reason Valencia might be a *poor* representative case for how hard commons monitoring usually is. Most real-world commons problems (fisheries with mobile, hard-to-observe actors; groundwater with invisible extraction; carbon; congestion) don't have a gate and a queue doing the monitoring for you. The chapter's line 73 hedge addresses group size but never addresses observability — arguably the bigger reason Valencia is a best-case rather than a representative case, and the one a hostile economist would raise first.

**Disposition: fix, both parts.** (1) Line 17's "compliance... running close to total" should either be cut or re-scoped to what's actually documented — "hundreds of infractions, almost all of them minor, out of the two years anyone actually counted" is the honest version. (2) Somewhere near the monitoring section (lines 35–39), one clause naming that a gravity-fed canal with a visible queue is close to the easiest case for reciprocal monitoring to work — Grace, the water-pricing economist, is exactly the interlocutor who would ask this, and currently doesn't.

## 4. The nesting/subsidiarity answer to "doesn't scale" asserts a line that canon itself says is unresolved (POS-18)

**Lines 67, 71.** "That layer is a state, functioning the way you'd actually want a state to function, permissive rather than intrusive... Interstate rivers, aquifers three states are drawing from at once, anything where the users can't see each other, let alone watch each other in line — those are jobs for a state."

This is Ostrom's own Principle 8 material, correctly sourced (`evidence.md` §6), and the chapter is right that nesting is Ostrom's actual answer, not a patch bolted on. But POS-18's own known vulnerability, on file in `canon/POSITIONS.md`, is precisely that **"genuine externality" is the contested judgment call in most real disputes** — the escalation rule "can be invoked by centralizers to justify almost any upward transfer of authority and denied by localists to resist almost any oversight," and CANON "supplies no test for adjudicating contested cases, only the uncontested-in-principle rule." The examples the chapter reaches for (interstate rivers, shared aquifers) are deliberately the *easy* cases — nobody disputes that a three-state aquifer is a genuine cross-jurisdictional externality. That's exactly why they're safe examples and exactly why they don't do the work the sentence's confident cadence implies they're doing. The chapter states the boundary ("those are jobs for a state") as though the line between "genuine escalation" and "invoked-but-spurious" is simply visible once you're looking at the right scale, when the canon file this chapter is answerable to says explicitly that no such discriminating test exists yet in the framework.

This is a lower-stakes objection than 1–3 because the chapter never claims to solve subsidiarity's hard cases here — it's making a narrower point about water specifically, and water-sharing-across-visible-jurisdictions probably is a genuinely easy case. But the rhetorical confidence of "those are jobs for a state," landing right after the "doesn't scale" concession that's otherwise the chapter's most careful moment, will read to a reader who's just seen POS-18's own hedge as more settled than the framework itself claims to be.

**Disposition: concede, one clause.** Not a structural fix — a scope gesture, in voice, acknowledging that the easy cases (visible, multi-state, physically shared resource) are easy precisely because everyone agrees they're genuine; the harder judgment calls the framework hasn't fully solved yet live elsewhere in the book, not here.

## 5. The stag hunt is basically correct game theory, applied to the right mechanism

**Lines 37–39.** "There's a name in game theory for the shape underneath that: the stag hunt... It runs on a different engine than the courthouse and the trenches from the last chapter: TIT FOR TAT ran on incentive, keep defecting and eventually somebody defects back. The stag hunt runs on belief, before either side has done anything at all."

This is sound and correctly distinguished from the prisoner's dilemma. A one-shot PD has a dominant strategy (defect) independent of belief about the other player, which is exactly why iterated play and grudge-holding strategies like TIT FOR TAT are what's needed to sustain cooperation there. A stag hunt (assurance game) has two pure-strategy Nash equilibria — mutual stag, mutual rabbit — and equilibrium selection genuinely depends on what each player believes the other will do, with no history required. The chapter's distinction between "runs on incentive" and "runs on belief" tracks the real difference. Applied specifically to turn-taking compliance at the gate — where showing up on time is worth it only if you expect everyone else to honor the rotation too — the fit is reasonable: this is closer to a coordination problem than to a per-turn temptation to defect regardless of others' behavior.

Two small notes, neither disqualifying. First, the CPR literature this chapter is drawing on has its own established reframing here — Runge (1981) argued explicitly that common-pool resource problems are usually better modeled as assurance games than as prisoner's dilemmas, against Hardin's implicit PD framing — and the chapter's stag-hunt move re-derives that argument from scratch without citing it, which is fine for voice but means the claim isn't checked against `evidence.md` at all; it's the Drafter's own analytical addition, not sourced material. Second, the underlying appropriation problem *before* boundaries and sanctions exist (why not take more water than your share?) is closer to a PD/tragedy structure than an assurance game — individual temptation exists regardless of what others do. The chapter's design-principle bundle (boundaries + sanctions) is what turns that PD-shaped problem into something where trust-based coordination (the stag hunt) becomes sufficient. The chapter doesn't say this explicitly, which slightly obscures that "the stag hunt" describes the *post-institution* equilibrium-selection problem, not the raw commons dilemma Hardin describes.

**Disposition: concede, optional.** This doesn't rise to a seminar laugh — it's a genuinely defensible use of the concept. If there's room, one clause distinguishing "before the rules exist, this looks like a prisoner's dilemma" from "once boundaries and sanctions exist, what's left is a stag hunt" would tighten it further, but I wouldn't spend word count fighting for this one over 1–3.

## 6. Confidence-vs-sources sweep

Checked every attribution above evidence.md's own confidence ratings. Most of this chapter is genuinely careful:

- **Levi attribution (line 49):** "she borrows it from a political scientist named Margaret Levi" — matches `evidence.md` §4 exactly (SOLID, pp. 93–94). No issue.
- **Huerta age claims (line 13):** "at least six centuries, proven. Close to ten is what scholars believe, and they say so carefully" — matches `evidence.md` §5d's hedged treatment precisely, including the 1960 "thousandth year" civic anniversary correctly flagged as unbacked by any 960 CE document. This is the chapter's best-disciplined passage and should be the model for the rest.
- **UNESCO note (line 15):** matches `evidence.md` §5e (SOLID, exact title, 2009, joint Valencia/Murcia listing) with no overclaim.
- **Canal count (line 4, "the rest of the eight"):** states the current figure without flagging Ostrom's 1990 "seven," which `evidence.md` §5c explicitly leaves at the Drafter's discretion ("a footnote... is enough if the Drafter wants the belt-and-suspenders version") — not a violation, just noting it's the one place the packet offered an opt-in caveat the chapter didn't take.
- **The two overclaims that do exist** — "compliance... running close to total" (line 17, objection 3) and the unhedged "generalizes" at line 73 covering monitoring as well as boundaries (objection 2) — are already covered above and are the only places in the chapter where the prose runs measurably ahead of what `evidence.md` actually licenses.

No fabricated quotations, no invented statistics, no citation attached to the wrong case. This chapter's sourcing discipline is real; the two overclaims that survive are both about how far a real, correctly-sourced finding is allowed to travel, not about whether the finding itself is true.
