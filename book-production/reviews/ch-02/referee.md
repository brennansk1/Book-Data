# Referee — Chapter 2, "The Shadow of the Future" (draft-v2)

Read as a hostile game theorist and a hostile military historian, against `spec/PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §2.7,
`canon/POSITIONS.md` (POS-11, POS-12 background), `manuscript/ch-02/draft-v2.md`, and
`research/ch-02/evidence.md`. Instructed to be unfair. Strongest objection first.

---

## 1. The chapter's own emotional case is a textbook Axis-2 mechanism, and it never says so

**The objection.** CANON §5 (POS-11) defines Axis 2 in the framework's own words: "Successful
coordination *against* excluded parties... better incentive alignment among perpetrators
intensifies the harm." The chapter's central worked example — Marisol and the ADA she's opposed
for six years, cooperating because they keep meeting again — is a repeat-player dynamic inside an
adversarial system that has a well-documented ugly reading: the "courtroom workgroup" literature
(prosecutors, defense counsel, and judges who see each other every week settling into comfortable,
efficient routines — plea mills, docket-clearing, "going along to get along") describes exactly
this mechanism producing outcomes that serve the repeat players at the expense of the person who
passes through only once, the defendant. Same shadow of the future, same niceness-and-forgiveness
equilibrium, opposite verdict on whether it's good news. The chapter spends its whole back third
telling Marisol (and the reader) that her comfortable relationship with opposing counsel is the
same beautiful machine as the trench truce. A defense attorney is professionally trained to notice
when a stable arrangement between insiders might be costing someone outside it. She would notice
this in about four seconds. The chapter doesn't let her.

**Why this isn't answered by the book's existing deferrals.** The chapter already defers two
things on purpose and says so: stability-vs-rightness goes to "a later part of this book" (¶55) and
to Valencia at the close; the love/friendship reduction is explicitly Ch. 13's job per the brief.
Both of those have a named future home. Axis-2 material does too, structurally — `CHAPTER_BRIEFS.md`
routes it to Ch. 4 ("that's Axis 2 and Ch. 6. Foreshadow rather than resolve") and Ch. 6 ("Whose
Suffering"). But Ch. 6 is about the moral-circle boundary (who counts), not about repeat-player
capture inside an adversarial institution, and no chapter in the 18-chapter brief set (Ch. 16,
"Institutions That Can Be Wrong," included) is about courtroom-workgroup collusion against
defendants specifically. Unlike the other two deferrals, this one has nowhere to land. Silence here
isn't foreshadowing; it's just silence.

**Fix, not cut.** This doesn't need a detour or a hedge that guts the warmth of the Marisol material
— it needs one sentence, in the narrator's own voice, of the kind the chapter already uses well
elsewhere (¶55's "I'm confident about the first part... I'm far less sure about the second"). Something
that names the fact that the same mechanism keeping Marisol and the ADA civil to each other is the
mechanism that, pointed the other way, produces docket-clearing coziness at a defendant's expense —
and that the difference between the two isn't in the game theory, it's in who's inside the repeated
game and who isn't. That is, in fact, close to being the cleanest possible one-sentence bridge to
Ch. 6's territory (whose suffering counts, who's inside the circle) rather than a digression from it.
Recommend placing it near ¶69, right where the chapter is already doing its most direct
you-and-the-ADA address, rather than breaking into the trench section.

**Verdict: fix.** Not a cut candidate — the Marisol material is the chapter's best writing and
shouldn't be pulled. Not a concede-and-move-on, either: this is a case where the honest sentence
costs the chapter almost nothing and buys real credibility with exactly the reader (a lawyer, a
judge, anyone who has watched a courthouse from the losing side) most likely to have already had
this thought and clocked its absence.

## 2. "Dozens of sectors" — the trench system's prevalence is oversold past what the source, or the sourcing chain, supports

**The objection.** ¶37: "Men trying to kill each other on the Western Front built, independently,
in dozens of sectors, without anyone ordering it, a working system of not killing each other."
Neither `research/ch-02/evidence.md` nor `research/ch-02/cases.md` contains a "dozens of sectors"
figure anywhere — the packet's own strongest prevalence claim is Axelrod's unquantified "widespread
and durable" (evidence.md §5, p. 81). "Dozens" is a specific-sounding quantifier the research
packet never supplied, which is close to the thing Standing Instruction #1 (`PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md`
§7.1) exists to prevent: not a fabricated quotation, but a fabricated-*sounding* statistic dressed
in the same voice as the page-cited material around it.

The deeper problem a military historian adds: Tony Ashworth's own book — the one this whole section
is filtered through — frames live-and-let-live as concentrated on *quiet, static sectors* and
*periods between major offensives*, not as a Western-Front-wide default. It receded sharply during
the Somme and other high-tempo operations, varied enormously by unit culture and command
aggressiveness, and was, on Ashworth's own accounting, a minority experience set against a war whose
modal experience was extraordinary, continuous violence. None of that is in the evidence packet —
the Researcher explicitly flagged (§5) that Ashworth's book itself was never independently pulled
this session — which means the chapter is currently one level more confident about *how much* of
the war this describes than its own sourcing chain can support, on top of already being one level
removed from the primary diaries (Axelrod → Ashworth → diarist).

**On the existing disclosure clause.** ¶37 already discloses the sourcing chain honestly: "I'm
getting it secondhand... a chain worth naming rather than hiding." That's the right move, and it's
well executed. But it discloses *provenance*, not *scope* — it tells the reader where the story came
from, not how much of the war it covers. Those are different disclosures and the chapter has only
made one of them. A reader who trusts the first clause has no reason to suspect the second problem
exists.

**Verdict: fix, two moves.**
1. Cut "dozens of" — replace with something the packet actually supports ("in sector after sector,"
   "wherever units sat still long enough," or simply naming it as widespread without a false-precision
   count).
2. Add the scope caveat alongside the existing provenance caveat — one clause is enough: that this
   was concentrated in the static, quiet stretches of the line and receded during the big pushes,
   which if anything sharpens the chapter's own argument (it's more evidence that the mechanism needs
   *sustained, predictable* repeated contact, and evaporates exactly when that predictability breaks —
   which is the same logic the chapter already uses to explain why the raid killed it). This isn't a
   concession that costs the argument anything; it's a free intensifier the chapter is currently
   leaving on the table by overstating uniformity instead.

Flag for Verifier per evidence.md's own open item #1: Ashworth 1980 needs to be pulled directly
before print regardless of this fix.

## 3. The tournament's evidentiary weight: the Pavlov aside undercuts the very framing it sits inside

**The objection.** The chapter spends two tournaments and a full paragraph building "everyone knew
and still couldn't beat it" into a near-mythic result (fair — it's Axelrod's own finding, verified
SOLID, p. 42, and the chapter is honest that second-round entrants were specifically *trying*
exploitation and still lost, ¶13). Then, one paragraph after introducing Nowak and Sigmund's 1992
noise-robustness fix, it adds: "The same two researchers found a further rule in 1993, one that does
even better in some conditions... I'm not going to walk you through that one." That is the chapter
telling the reader, in its own voice, that a strategy exists which beats TIT FOR TAT — the same
sentence structure ("does better") used nowhere else in the chapter except to describe TIT FOR TAT
itself — and then declining to explain why that doesn't matter. A skeptical reader doesn't need the
mechanics of win-stay-lose-shift; they need one clause on why "something beats it under some
conditions" doesn't quietly retract "nobody, trying, could beat it." Right now the chapter just
asserts the retraction doesn't matter ("that generosity result is what actually matters here")
without showing why the Pavlov result is a different kind of finding rather than a competing one.

It is a different kind of finding — Pavlov still requires the same repeated-game structure and the
same durable expectation of meeting again; it's a dispute about *which* rule is optimal within
iteration, not about whether iteration is what's doing the work. That's a real answer and it's one
sentence long. The chapter has the material to make it and doesn't.

There's a second, larger version of the same worry worth naming even though the brief scopes it out:
Axelrod's tournaments are a specific payoff matrix, 200 rounds, one particular round-robin ecology.
The finding that TIT-FOR-TAT-like strategies dominate is not ecology-independent — later work
(spatial models, larger strategy spaces, and, further out than this chapter needs to go,
Press & Dyson's 2012 result on extortionate "zero-determinant" strategies) shows the winner is a
function of who else is in the room. The chapter never claims eternal, ecology-free superiority in
so many words, and `evidence.md` §6b already flags the noise-fragility point as the honest caveat to
land — this is not a demand for a literature review mid-letter. But the current one-line dismissal of
Pavlov reads as *aware of the problem and waving at it* rather than *answering it*, which is a worse
look than not raising Pavlov at all.

**Verdict: fix, small.** Either cut the Pavlov sentence entirely (it currently costs more credibility
than it buys) or keep it and add the one clause distinguishing "which rule wins" from "whether
repetition is what's doing the work." The second option is better — it's free reinforcement of the
chapter's actual thesis if stated, and a live wound if left as a shrug.

## 4. Does the chapter argue from stability to rightness before its own flag?

**The objection.** ¶55 ("is this just self-interest with a longer memory?") is the chapter's
official, well-executed answer to the stability/rightness conflation, and it's honest: "It explains
the mechanics of a fact. It doesn't tell you the fact is good." Good — that's exactly what
`PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §7.2 requires (never state a contested claim at higher confidence than
warranted) and it's calibrated correctly per `STYLE_BIBLE.md` §9.

But thirty paragraphs earlier, at the Saxon soldier's apology (¶45), the chapter writes: "Something
that starts as bare incentive can still turn into more than that, given enough repetition between
the same two sides." Read in isolation, "more than that" invites the reader to feel that sustained
stable cooperation matures into something better — genuine moral regret — which is one short
inferential step from stability producing rightness, exactly the move the chapter later disowns.
The chapter catches itself two paragraphs later ("Predictability, not decency, was what the
arrangement needed from either side," ¶47) and the whiplash is presumably deliberate — plant the
warm reading, then correct it — but it means the chapter's real answer to "does stability become
rightness" is delivered twice, once by implication at ¶45 and once explicitly at ¶55, and the first
delivery is the one that leans the wrong way before the correction arrives. A reader who stops at
¶45 (entirely possible — it's a strong, quotable beat) walks away with the conflation intact.

**Verdict: concede, minor — tighten rather than cut.** The ¶45→¶47 sequence is defensible as written
and arguably more honest than removing the temptation entirely (it lets the reader feel the pull
before being told why to resist it, which is more persuasive than asserting the caveat cold). But
"more than that" is doing more work than the chapter means it to; a small rephrase — something that
names the moral-regret reading as *real but separate from the argument*, rather than as an
escalation of it — would close the gap without losing the beat. Not urgent enough to block the gate;
worth a Line Editor pass.

## 5. Confidence vs. `evidence.md` — clean bill, with the exceptions already logged above

Checked every load-bearing empirical claim in the chapter against its rated confidence in
`research/ch-02/evidence.md`:

- Both tournaments' structure, entrant counts, disciplines, and Rapoport's win (¶3–17): matches
  evidence.md §1a/1b (SOLID) exactly, including the specific entrant details (Tideman & Chieruzzi's
  41 lines, Stein & Rapoport's 50, the anonymous 77-line entry finishing ahead of only RANDOM) —
  verified against the full Table 2 reproduction in `cases.md`. No overclaim.
- The ecological simulation and HARRINGTON (¶19): matches evidence.md §1c (SOLID) exactly, including
  the fiftieth/thousandth-generation figures.
- The four properties and Axelrod's closing quote (¶25): word-for-word against evidence.md §2
  (SOLID), correctly page-cited.
- "Shadow of the future," the wedding example (¶33): matches evidence.md §3 (SOLID).
- "Costly signal": the chapter never actually uses the phrase or attributes it to Axelrod — correctly
  following evidence.md §4's explicit warning that this is the chapter's own synthesis, not Axelrod's
  vocabulary. Good discipline.
- The football-match omission (¶53): explicitly banned per decisions.md and evidence.md §6a, and the
  chapter names the omission out loud rather than silently avoiding it — this is the right way to
  handle a banned beat and should be treated as a model for other chapters, not just a pass.
- Nowak & Sigmund noise material (¶57): correctly dated (1992, 1993), correctly characterized as a
  "fixed, small, calibrated dose of unearned forgiveness," matching evidence.md §6b. The only issue
  here is the rhetorical one raised in §3 above, not a sourcing problem.
- The trench-system claims (¶37–51): faithful to Axelrod-as-reported in evidence.md §5, with the one
  prevalence overstatement flagged in §2 above. The Saxon apology quote and the "evening gun" material
  are both exact and page-cited.

No claim in the chapter exceeds its evidence.md rating except the "dozens of sectors" phrase (§2
above), which isn't in evidence.md at all — that's the only fabrication-adjacent item found. Everything
else is faithful, correctly hedged, and in several places (the football omission, the noise caveat)
better calibrated than the median chapter is likely to be.
