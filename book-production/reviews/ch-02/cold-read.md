# Cold Read — Chapter 2 (draft-v1)

One read, straight through, no manuscript context beyond this chapter.

## 1. Register failures

**The "not X, Y" frame runs four times and becomes audible by the third.**
- "Not because simple is a virtue in itself; I don't think it generally is. Simple happened to be the shape that let the other player see what it was dealing with fast enough to cooperate back…" (¶29)
- "Not because they wanted to hurt anyone. Because a side that can't demonstrate it's dangerous can't be trusted when it says it's holding back." (¶41)
- "They weren't being decent to each other. They were being predictable." (¶47)
- "That's not a fact I've pulled from the record. It's just what the logic underneath the record predicts…" (¶65)

None of these is bad alone — the first two land well. But by the fourth the shape is recognizable before the sentence finishes, and recognizable rhetoric reads as engineered rather than felt.

**"It's worth sitting for a second on who lost."** (¶29) A close cousin of the stock throat-clear. It's the one place the essayist voice pokes through the letter voice — the sentence announces that attention is being directed, instead of just directing it.

**"Here's the claim, plainly, before I earn it:"** (¶23) Structurally honest, but it names the move ("before I earn it") in a chapter that otherwise trusts the reader to notice the claim arriving late. Minor, and arguably in-voice for this narrator, but it's the one sentence that sounds like it knows it's in an essay.

## 2. Seams

The tournament material and the Marisol frame join cleanly — the direct address at ¶21 ("Marisol, I still haven't been able to put down…") is a hard cut, but it's *supposed* to be a hard cut, it's a letter, and it works.

The real seam is in the back third, where four different materials get braided in about ten paragraphs: the noise/forgiveness game-theory result (¶57), the personal betrayal anecdote (¶59–63), the trench/courthouse merge metaphor (¶65), and the closing return to Marisol (¶67–71). Each transition is individually smooth, but stacked this densely the chapter starts to feel like it's collecting its threads rather than following one. The tell is ¶65 itself: "I think about the trench system and your courthouse as the same machine wearing two different costumes… and I don't think that's me forcing a metaphor where it doesn't belong." Pre-defending a metaphor against the objection is a sign the writer felt the seam too.

The trench section itself (¶37–53) is where the courthouse frame goes missing longest — seventeen paragraphs with no Marisol, no "you," a register shift toward drier historical narration with citation clusters. It comes back working (¶65 explicitly reunites the two), but for a reader mid-chapter it's a long absence from the person the letter is nominally addressed to.

## 3. Attention drift

¶25, the four-properties paragraph: "Retaliation alone traps you in a feud forever, so it was forgiving too. One punishment, then back to cooperating, letting the other player earn its way back rather than paying for one bad move indefinitely (p. 36; a rule called FRIEDMAN never forgave anything and scored worse for it)."

This is where the eye slides. The FRIEDMAN detail is a genuinely new fact — a named counter-case — but it's parked inside a page citation instead of given its own clause. The paragraph has four of these citation-parentheses in a row ((p. 33)…(p. 44)…(p. 36; …)…(pp. 53–54)) immediately before a fifth citation attached to the closing block quote. It's the one place in the chapter that reads like a disguised list with footnotes, and it's exactly the paragraph carrying the chapter's actual mechanism (the four TIT FOR TAT properties) — the content most worth not skimming.

## 4. The argument, and the flourish

After one read, the argument is: *durable cooperation between people who owe each other nothing doesn't require virtue — it requires a credible expectation of meeting again, and that expectation can be built on purpose.*

"You, Marisol, are what the shadow of the future looks like wearing a person's face" — mostly earned. The concept has real scaffolding under it by the time it lands: the wedding example, the accidental-versus-designed distinction, the specific building (the elevator, the vending machine). It's not dropped on the reader cold.

But it costs something on a second look, because "wearing a person's face" gets echoed almost verbatim thirty paragraphs later — "the same machine wearing two different costumes" (¶65). Two personifications built on the same verb, close enough together that the second one reads as a callback the writer planted rather than a thought that occurred twice. That's either deliberate idiolect (a recurring image, used on purpose) or a tell that the line was reached for once and the reach got reused. On one read it's hard to tell which, and that ambiguity is itself worth flagging.

## 5. Trust

One voice, largely. The trench material shifts drier and more citation-dense than the rest, but the "I" keeps intruding on it — "which still irritates me slightly, uselessly, this many decades on" — so it never becomes a separate narrator, just the same one reading more carefully out loud.

The retaliation admission (¶59–63) costs. Six years, a funeral, "I was running FRIEDMAN on a person I'd known since I was nineteen and calling it integrity," and the flat "I don't know whether the six years bought anything or just cost both of us six years, which is a weird thing to still not know." Nothing about that flatters the narrator, the specifics are too particular to be generic, and it's framed as a first-time confession to Marisol specifically ("I've never told it to you before tonight"). It reads as a real cost, not a performed one.

## 6. Pacing — the trench section

Mostly holds. It's carried by two strong concrete beats — the "evening gun" (¶41) and the German soldier's apology (¶45) — spaced far enough apart to keep resetting attention. The named omission ("There's a version of this story with a Christmas football match… I'm leaving it out," ¶53) is a good release valve right where the section could overstay.

It sags once, in the stretch between the two beats: the "New units rotating in got briefed by the ones rotating out" paragraph and "It ended from above" paragraph (¶42–51) run more administrative than scenic — mechanism-explaining without a person or an object in the room. It's not fatal, but it's the one patch where seventeen paragraphs on one battle line starts to feel like seventeen paragraphs.

## 7. Factual side-eyes

- The Dawkins anecdote (¶3) — a typescript landing on his desk, never sent in, foreword decades later — is a very well-shaped story for something this incidental. Worth a source check.
- The whole trench narrative appears to rest on one book (Axelrod's) plus a single secondary citation (Ashworth 1980) relayed through it. For a claim this striking — independently-invented truces across "dozens of sectors" — a cold reader wants to know if that's corroborated anywhere outside the one popularizing account, or if the chapter is repeating Axelrod's own synthesis as settled military history.
- "A ten-year-old computer hobbyist" among the second tournament's sixty-two entrants (¶13) — vivid enough to want confirming rather than take on faith.
- Nowak and Sigmund are dated only to "the early nineties" (¶57), notably vaguer than every other citation in the chapter, which mostly carry page numbers. Given how precisely everything else is sourced, the vagueness here stands out.
