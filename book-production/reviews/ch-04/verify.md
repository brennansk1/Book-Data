## Battersea Fields duel

1. **Date/hour/location — Saturday, 21 March 1829, 8 a.m., Battersea Fields, Surrey.** PASS —
   converged across Wikipedia "Wellington–Winchilsea duel," British History on This Day, King's
   College London's own "Duel Day" feature. 21 March 1829 was in fact a Saturday.
2. **Wellington as sitting Prime Minister.** PASS — undisputed across all sources.
3. **Winchilsea's letter in *The Standard*, 14 March 1829, "insidious design for the infringement
   of our liberties and the introduction of Popery into every department of the State."** PASS —
   exact phrase converges verbatim across Wikipedia, King's College archives, Woolley & Wallis.
4. **Retraction demand and refusal.** PASS — Wellington demanded withdrawal; Winchilsea privately
   conceded he'd overstepped but publicly refused, matching the draft's line 5.
5. **Seconds: Hardinge and Falmouth (Edward Boscawen).** PASS — Sir Henry Hardinge for Wellington,
   Edward Boscawen, 1st Earl of Falmouth, for Winchilsea. Confirmed by name.
6. **Twelve paces.** PASS — converged across all sources.
7. **Wellington fired first and missed.** PASS — undisputed across all sources.
8. **The fire-first ambiguity, as the chapter presents it.** PASS — Wellington's own claim of a
   deliberate miss vs. his reputation as a poor shot is a genuine, unresolved contemporary dispute;
   one source (utterlyinteresting.com, via a physician's account) even reports that some observers
   sympathetic to Winchilsea believed Wellington had aimed to kill and missed through incompetence
   — i.e., the record supports an even *more* contested reading than the chapter states, so the
   chapter is if anything conservative here, not overclaiming.
9. **Winchilsea deloping — raised his pistol and fired into the air, prearranged with his
   second.** PASS — confirmed by Woolley & Wallis and the utterlyinteresting.com physician's
   account (Winchilsea waited for Wellington's shot, then discharged into the air). Note for the
   record: one early secondary snippet encountered this session gave a garbled account implying
   Winchilsea never fired at all; the fuller sources resolve this as fire-into-the-air, matching the
   draft. The chapter's account is correct.
10. **The written apology withdrawing "disgraceful and criminal motives."** PASS-WITH-NOTE —
    located the actual apology text (via search of period sources): "I do not hesitate to declare,
    of my own accord, that, in apology, I regret having unadvisedly published an opinion which the
    Duke states to have charged him with disgraceful and criminal motives in a certain transaction."
    The phrase itself is confirmed genuine. But the actual wording hedges ("which the Duke states to
    have charged him with") rather than flatly owning the retraction — the chapter's gloss "withdrew
    the charge...without qualification" slightly overstates the directness of the apology's own
    language, even though it was accepted by both seconds as fully satisfactory and closed the
    matter. **Fix (optional, minor):** soften "without qualification" to something like "and both
    sides considered the matter closed" if the Drafter wants the phrasing to track the actual text
    more closely; not required, since the *effect* described (satisfied honor, no further contest)
    is accurate.
11. **Winchilsea's 1834 wedding, five years later.** PASS — Wikipedia confirms Wellington attended
    Winchilsea's 1834 wedding to Emily Bagot (Wellington's own great-niece). The chapter already
    flags this in-text as single-source and unconfirmed by the narrator, which is appropriately
    cautious even though the underlying fact checks out.
12. **Dueling illegal in England; no scandal large enough to end anything in 1829.** PASS —
    consistent with the record (Wellington faced no prosecution and his standing was, if anything,
    strengthened).

## The narrator's own conjecture passage (line 23)

13. **"No credit bureau."** PASS — no credit-reporting institution existed in Britain in 1829 in
    any form resembling a modern bureau.
14. **"No enforceable contract between two gentlemen who'd shaken hands on a debt."** PASS —
    gaming/wagering debts were not legally enforceable in English courts in this period (formalized
    later in the Gaming Act 1845, but the underlying non-enforceability of debts of honor at law
    predates it); gentlemen relied on the honor code, not the courts, to collect them.
15. **"No real police interest in what happened between men of a certain class, provided nobody
    important got hurt."** PASS — consistent with the historical record generally and directly
    demonstrated by the duel itself: a sitting Prime Minister committed an illegal act in public and
    faced no police or prosecutorial consequence.
16. **That the passage reads as flagged authorial speculation, not a sourced claim.** PASS — the
    passage explicitly labels itself "a guess of my own," "not because I can back it up," and names
    no source for the credit/collateral inference itself. Confirmed nothing in the passage smuggles
    in an unsourced claim dressed as fact.

## Appiah

17. **The ridicule mechanism as characterized.** PASS — Davis's NYRB review confirms the direct
    Appiah quotation "ridicule at last did more than morality to kill dueling," and independent
    reviews (Philosophical Quarterly) confirm the exclusivity mechanism (dueling stopped signaling
    gentlemanly membership once it spread down the social scale) as Appiah's actual argument. The
    chapter paraphrases rather than quotes Appiah directly, which is the right call given no primary
    text was available to either the Researcher or this Verifier.
18. **That the book runs the same argument through footbinding and the Atlantic slave trade.**
    PASS-WITH-NOTE — confirmed accurate as stated. Note for completeness, not a correction: the book
    actually has a fourth major case, honor killing in contemporary Pakistan, which multiple sources
    (Carnegie Council review) confirm Appiah treats differently in kind — as an open, forward-looking
    test of the theory rather than a completed "moral revolution" like the other three. The chapter's
    claim ("two further moral revolutions") is accurate on its own terms and doesn't require
    mentioning the fourth case, but the Drafter should know it exists in case a later chapter wants
    it.
19. **The "moral revolutions" framing.** PASS — this is Appiah's own book subtitle framing,
    confirmed.
20. **The ~1850s end-date for English dueling.** PASS-WITH-NOTE — individually-dated facts converge
    cleanly: army banned dueling among officers 1844; last fatal English-vs-English duel on English
    soil, Hawkey–Seton, 20 May 1845; last duel of any kind on English soil, Cournet–Barthélemy,
    1852. "By the 1850s the whole apparatus is essentially finished" is a fair characterization of
    this data, not a precise sourced statistic — which is how the chapter treats it (no exact figure
    is claimed).
21. **"Two centuries of sermons."** PASS-WITH-NOTE — this is rhetorical scene-setting, not a
    citable statistic, and the chapter doesn't present it as one. Clerical/moral condemnation of
    dueling in England is genuinely old (English anti-dueling proclamations and church opposition
    trace back to at least the early seventeenth century, arguably to Elizabeth I's 1571
    condemnation), so "two centuries" before 1829 is a defensible approximation. No single source
    was found this session stating "two centuries" as an agreed figure, but none was found
    contradicting it either.
22. **The Davis attribution — cold reader's "possibly the wrong chapter" flag.** PASS, flag
    resolved. Fetched the NYRB review directly: Davis's monocausality critique and his "ridicule…
    did more than morality" quotation are explicitly about the *dueling* material, not the
    footbinding or slave-trade chapters. Davis's own words: "while it is clear that major changes in
    social class were a prerequisite for dissolving the honor code that maintained British
    dueling...I fail to see either the irrelevance of moral argument or the centrality and
    persistence of a new code of honor." The cold reader's suspicion (Davis is a slavery historian,
    so surely he was cited against the slave-trade chapter) does not hold up against the primary
    text of his own review — he devotes substantial attention to dueling specifically. **No fix
    needed; the chapter's attribution is correct as written.** One correction to the Researcher's
    packet, not the chapter: the packet's claim that Davis's complication cites "Cardinal Newman's
    ideal gentleman as 'one who never inflicts pain'" does not appear in the review as fetched —
    Davis's actual complication is about the "vulgarity" of dueling spreading to "self-made men" and
    a resulting loss of exclusivity, closer to a class-composition argument than a
    changing-masculinity one. This doesn't affect the chapter, which never mentions Newman.

## Multicausality (line 31, the four named rivals)

23. **State monopoly on violence.** PASS — a real, recognized explanation (Norbert Elias's
    "civilizing process" literature applies this directly to dueling's decline; multiple sources
    confirm scholars link rising state capacity to dueling's collapse).
24. **Officer-class composition.** PASS — confirmed as a real argument in the literature (shifting,
    increasingly bourgeois composition of the officer corps is cited as a factor in duelling's
    decline, e.g. in East India Company and Continental contexts); fairly stated.
25. **Legal/insurance alternatives to honor remedies.** PASS-WITH-NOTE — the legal half is well
    confirmed (libel actions functioned as an alternative remedy for the rising middle class). The
    insurance half was **not independently confirmed this session** — no source located specifically
    connecting life/liability insurance to dueling's decline in England. This is plausible (it
    appears in some duelling historiography, particularly on the Continent) but should get a source
    from the Verifier or Drafter before the chapter treats it as established; as written the chapter
    only lists it among several rivals it says it "hasn't read...carefully enough to tell you how
    much weight" each carries, which is appropriately hedged and does not overclaim.
26. **A newly aggressive press.** PASS — confirmed; contemporary sources describe the growing print
    media of the period publicizing and reshaping honor disputes.

## Maghribi traders / Greif

27. **The coalition mechanism (closed network, multilateral reputation punishment).** PASS —
    confirmed directly against Greif's own papers and Stanford-hosted materials: agents drawn from
    within the group, an agent's misconduct anywhere in the network resulting in network-wide
    ostracism, courts playing a minor role relative to reputation.
28. **"Cheated in Palermo, finished in Cairo."** PASS — not a quotation (no quotation marks in the
    draft), and it's a fair compression of Greif's actual documented example: a circa-1050 letter
    from an agent in Palermo to a merchant in Fustat/Cairo shows exactly this kind of cross-Mediterranean
    reputational consequence in the correspondence Greif studied.
29. **The Edwards–Ogilvie dispute, as now hedged.** PASS — the hedge matches what's actually known.
    Directly located Edwards and Ogilvie's own claim ("not a single empirical example adduced by
    Greif shows that any 'coalition' actually existed") independent of Greif's summary, which is
    firmer sourcing than the Researcher's packet had. Their position did shift across 2008/2009/2012
    iterations toward acknowledging reputation mechanisms operated "alongside" legal ones. The
    chapter's phrase "at least on his telling" is doing real, necessary work — my own read of Greif's
    hosted debate page did **not** find Edwards/Ogilvie explicitly conceding the specific phrase
    "collective punishment... does appear to have been used" in as clean a form as Greif's rebuttal
    implies; it shows them acknowledging reputation *mattered*, framed as secondary to courts, which
    is a real but subtly different concession. The chapter's "at least on his telling" hedge already
    covers this gap correctly — no fix needed.
