# Ch. 4 ("Morality as Technology") — Referee Report

Reading as a hostile economic historian and a hostile philosopher, on `manuscript/ch-04/draft-v1.md`
against `research/ch-04/evidence.md`, `reviews/ch-04/decisions.md`, `canon/CANON.md` §5, and
`canon/POSITIONS.md`. Ordered strongest objection first. Per Gate 3 (`PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §4),
each finding closes with Fix / Concede / Cut.

---

## 1. The credit-and-collateral apparatus is invented, uncited, and laundered through Appiah's authority

The chapter's central analytic move — that the duel functioned as a *costly signal* underwriting a
*credit economy* ("No credit bureaus... A gentleman's word was what let him borrow money without
collateral... your word was the only collateral you had. The willingness to stand at twelve paces
and take a bullet over it was the collateral behind the collateral... The duel was working... the
way a currency works... A costly signal is what economists would call it now, retroactively") —
**appears nowhere in `research/ch-04/evidence.md`.** The packet's entire Appiah section (§2) is
about honour-world exclusivity and ridicule. There is no source anywhere in the packet for
"no credit bureaus," "borrow money without collateral," "sit on a board, stand for a seat," or the
signaling-theory vocabulary ("costly signal," "collateral behind the collateral"). This isn't a
one-line inference from the case — it's a full page, and it is the chapter's real thesis engine:
the passage is doing more theoretical work than anything explicitly attributed to Appiah, Greif, or
Boehm.

Worse: it is placed immediately after the Wellington case and before Appiah is even named, so a
reader takes it as continuous with the historical record just established, not as the
letter-writer's own unlicensed synthesis. This is exactly the "put a mechanism in his mouth"
failure mode the brief for this review anticipated — except the mechanism isn't put in Appiah's
mouth, it's put in the *reader's ear* with no mouth attached at all.

The frustrating part is that the claim isn't crazy — there is a real historiography of honour,
reputation, and creditworthiness in early modern commercial society (Craig Muldrew, *The Economy
of Obligation*, 1998, is the standard reference; the signaling-theory vocabulary the chapter reaches
for on its own has a real home in Zahavi's handicap principle and Spence's job-market-signaling
model, and Diego Gambetta's *Codes of the Underworld* applies exactly this apparatus to honour
codes). None of that is in the evidence packet, and none of it is cited. `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §7.1
is explicit: "Never invent a citation, a quotation, a statistic, or a date... If the evidence packet
doesn't contain it, write around it or flag it." This isn't a fabricated date or quote, but it is a
fabricated *empirical mechanism* stated with total narrative confidence in a book whose thesis is
epistemic honesty. A seminar would ask "citation?" and there is none to give.

**Fix:** either (a) send back to the Researcher for a real citation — Muldrew for the credit-economy
claim, Zahavi/Spence/Gambetta for the signaling vocabulary — and re-thread the paragraph with
attribution ("historians of early modern credit, notably Muldrew, have argued..."), or (b) if no
citation clears in time, rewrite the passage as explicit authorial inference rather than reported
history: "Here's my own reading of why it worked, for what it's worth — nobody I've cited actually
makes this argument." That second option is cheap, is consistent with the letter's own voice
elsewhere (it already does this move for the Iceland/Mensur material), and would turn a fabrication
into a flagged conjecture, which the project's rules permit.

**Concede:** if the Showrunner wants to keep the passage as color without a citation, it must at
minimum be flagged in text, not silently presented as established fact.

**Cut:** the leanest fix — drop the two "no credit bureaus" paragraphs entirely and let the chapter
rest on Appiah's actual, sourced mechanism (exclusivity → ridicule). This loses a genuinely good
paragraph of prose but removes the chapter's single largest unlicensed claim.

## 2. The functional account retrodicts three cases and is never shown what would falsify it

"Moral institutions are mostly solutions to coordination problems" is asserted and then illustrated
by three cases, all chosen because they fit. The chapter never states what a *disconfirming* case
would look like — a reputation system that collapsed into escalating feud rather than stabilizing
(the blood-feud literature Boehm's own tradition sits against), an honour code that persisted after
the coordination problem it supposedly solved had been removed by courts and credit bureaus (which
would cut directly against the point Objection 1 above tries to make), or a society that solved the
same coordination problem with a mechanism that has nothing to do with honour or morality at all
(insurance, notarial law). Without a named failure mode, a historian's flat objection stands: the
theory predicts nothing and retrodicts everything, and three well-chosen convergent cases are not,
by themselves, evidence against that charge — they're what the charge predicts you'd find.

Worth asking directly of the Case Hunter's process: `decisions.md` notes Iceland was "reserved" for
Ch. 3 or the endnotes. Was it reserved because it complicated the thesis, or purely for chapter
economy? If a candidate case was dropped because it didn't fit as cleanly, that's exactly the
selection effect this objection is pointing at, and it should be answered on the record even if the
answer is "no, it was just scheduling."

**Fix:** one sentence, ideally near the "convergent engineering" paragraph, naming what a
non-convergent case would look like and conceding none is offered here — which costs little, since
the chapter's own honesty-under-pressure voice (the ANCHOR passage) is already built to hold exactly
this kind of admission.

**Concede:** the ANCHOR/coda material substantially defuses the *normative* version of this worry
(it explicitly refuses to let the functional account ground obligation) — but that is a different
objection from the *retrodiction* worry raised here, and conceding one does not answer the other.

**Cut:** not applicable — this needs an added sentence, not a subtraction.

## 3. The ridicule concession is retracted in the sentence that grants it, and the real multicausal literature is absent

The Davis paragraph is a genuine, multi-sentence hedge — more than the "one clause" this review was
primed to expect — so credit where due. But read its last two sentences together: "I think Davis is
probably right that it's more tangled than one mechanism. I don't think the tangle changes what
killed it, which is a change in who else was doing it..." That is a concession followed immediately
by its own cancellation: having granted multicausality, the chapter reasserts monocausality in the
same breath, performing epistemic humility while doing none of the epistemic work the humility
implies. And even Davis's complication (rising middle-class respectability) is the only rival
explanation the chapter names. The standard multicausal account of the duel's decline — the state's
consolidation of a monopoly on legitimate violence (Elias), the changing class composition of the
officer corps, the rise of legal and insurance alternatives to honour remedies, and the press's role
in making duels a public spectacle rather than a private matter of class — appears nowhere, not even
as a named alternative to be waved past. The chapter accuses itself, in its own closing pages, of
being a "machine for making [morality] look optional" — it should hold its own causal history to the
same suspicion it holds its own metaethics to.

**Fix:** rewrite the paragraph's last sentence to stop short of the reassertion — something like "I
don't know how much of the tangle survives if you pull on it harder than I have here" — and add one
clause naming that the wider historiography (state violence-monopoly, legal alternatives, class
composition of the officer corps) exists and isn't being engaged. This is consistent with, not a
departure from, the letter's own honesty register.

**Concede:** the paragraph as written is not nothing — it does name a real critic and a real
complication. The failure is specifically in the walk-back sentence, not the whole passage.

**Cut:** not applicable.

## 4. The Maghribi "agreement" is Greif's own summary of his critics, presented as if neutral

`research/ch-04/evidence.md` §4 flags this explicitly and by name: "this packet accessed the dispute
primarily through Greif's own hosted summary of it... not through Edwards and Ogilvie's published
work directly," and instructs that "before the chapter states the dispute's shape as fact, the
Verifier should pull Edwards and Ogilvie's own published version." The chapter's line — "What both
sides actually agree on, once you read past the argument... some collective punishment happened,
and courts mattered too" — states exactly the fact the packet says isn't yet verified, and does so
with full narrative confidence, no hedge on the sourcing asymmetry itself (only a hedge on which side
is "right," which is a different and easier hedge to make). An interested party's account of what his
critics concede is not neutral ground merely because he seems to be summarizing fairly; that
"seems" is precisely what hasn't been checked. Gate 4 (Continuity/Verifier) is supposed to catch this
before freeze, but Gate 3 review happens first, and the text as it stands overclaims relative to what
the packet itself says it's earned.

**Fix:** one clause — "at least according to Greif's own account of the dispute, which is the only
version I've read" — costs almost nothing and is exactly the move the chapter already makes
elsewhere (the Mensur statistic, the single-sourced wedding detail, the apology-redraft). Doing it
here too is consistency, not extra caution.

**Concede:** acceptable to leave as-is only if the Verifier confirms Greif's characterization against
Edwards and Ogilvie's actual papers before the chapter freezes, per the Verifier obligation
`decisions.md` already logs — but the text shouldn't currently read as if that check has already
happened.

**Cut:** not applicable.

## 5. Boehm is the one place the chapter drops its own hedging discipline

"He surveyed the ethnographic record across dozens of societies: San groups... Hadza... Australian
Aboriginal bands among others, and found the pattern held broadly across all of them" is stated flat,
with no hedge at all. `evidence.md` §5 itself carries an unresolved methodological flag — how much of
the ethnographic record reflects forager egalitarianism *before* sustained contact with colonial and
state authority, versus societies already reshaped by it — and explicitly says to flag this "if
Boehm's material is used at length rather than in the graduated-sanctions framing." The chapter does
use it at some length (a full paragraph plus the "reverse dominance hierarchy" term-of-art and the
"political achievement" framing), which is exactly the trigger condition the packet names. Everywhere
else in this chapter — the wedding detail, the apology redraft, the Mensur statistic, even the earl's
ordinal number — gets a hedge or an omission. Boehm, the most consequential and least
independently-verified claim in the chapter, gets none. That's an inconsistency in the chapter's own
epistemic practice, not just a missing citation.

**Fix:** one clause, e.g., "across societies that anthropologists caution were, in some of these
studies, already reshaped by contact with colonial and state authority by the time they were
recorded." Cheap, and brings Boehm into line with how the rest of the chapter treats uncertain
claims.

**Concede:** minor in isolation; the objection is really about consistency of practice across the
chapter, and the fix is correspondingly small.

**Cut:** not applicable.

## 6. The refused hinge risks reading as a performance of humility rather than an earned pause

The brief mandates this move ("Do not resolve it — sharpen it"), and the chapter executes it with
real craft: the ANCHOR passage's self-reread, the confession that "every case I'd assembled to make
morality look solid turned out... to be a machine for making it look optional," the closing "I don't
know yet. I'm not sure anyone does." A hostile reader can still ask: does refusing to answer cost the
author anything, or does it let him collect credit for honesty while deferring all the actual
argumentative work downstream? The chapter spends more words performing its own uncertainty than most
chapters spend defending a position, and a skeptic could read that ratio as itself a rhetorical
move — sophistication in place of an argument, dressed as its opposite.

This is mostly a brief-mandated feature rather than a drafting failure, so it isn't grounds for a
rewrite of Ch. 4 itself. But it creates a real downstream obligation: the hinge only earns its place
if Ch. 5 (or wherever "useful vs. binding" gets re-engaged) actually cashes it out, rather than
letting the promissory note roll over again. If phenomenal value realism (POS-01, MODERATE
confidence per `POSITIONS.md`) doesn't directly re-address the distinction Dana draws — usefulness
versus obligation — the hinge was theater.

**Fix:** none needed in Ch. 4 text. Flag as a continuity/brief obligation: confirm Ch. 5's brief and
draft actually re-open the "useful vs. binding" distinction by name, not just gesture at grounding
value in general.

**Concede:** brief-mandated structure; accept as written.

**Cut:** not applicable.

## Literature the chapter should have cited and didn't

- Craig Muldrew, *The Economy of Obligation* (1998) — the actual historiography behind the
  credit/reputation claim in Objection 1.
- Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle and Michael Spence's signaling model — the actual theoretical
  home of "costly signal," a term the chapter uses as if it were self-evidently available.
- Diego Gambetta, *Codes of the Underworld* (2009) — applies costly-signaling analysis to honour
  codes directly; the nearest thing to an existing version of this chapter's own argument.
- V. G. Kiernan, *The Duel in European History* (1988), or a comparable dedicated historical
  monograph on dueling — the chapter's entire duel-decline account runs through one popular
  philosophy book (Appiah) and its reviewers; no historian who specializes in dueling itself is
  consulted anywhere in the packet.
- Norbert Elias on the state's monopolization of legitimate violence — the standard alternative
  causal account of honour-violence decline, absent even as a name-check (Objection 3).

## The one line a seminar would laugh at

"The collateral behind the collateral" — a vivid, quotable, entirely unsourced piece of economic
history, stated with the same narrative confidence as the verified date of the duel itself. In a
book whose thesis is that legible, verifiable signals matter, this is the sentence most likely to be
read back to the author in a hostile Q&A.
