# Prologue — Candidate Opening Cases

Brief: "The Thing Nobody Chose." A small, real, recent, non-political case where everyone
involved behaves reasonably, everyone hates the result, and it happens anyway. Must not be
a philosophical thought experiment, climate change, or nuclear war. Must not preview the
book's structure. Ends on the question.

Note on overlap: Ch. 1 ("Everyone Hates This") is already committed to Newfoundland cod
(1992) as its opening case. Anything fishery-shaped in this file is scored down for
thematic redundancy even where the facts check out — the Prologue needs a *different*
shape of trap than Ch. 1 uses, so that Ch. 1 doesn't feel like a rerun.

All facts below were checked against the cited reporting/literature this session, not
recalled from memory. Confidence and risk notes are per-case. See `evidence.md` for the
full sourcing on the top two.

---

## Ranked list

1. **Residency application inflation** — strongest fit, best documentation, cleanest structural shape.
2. **Third-party delivery-app commissions** — strong, vivid, recent; slightly weaker on the "nobody can unilaterally stop it" collective shape because the best-documented named case is someone who *did* stop it.
3. **Junior-employee overwork / face-time culture (banking, law)** — real and well-documented at the aggregate level, but weak on named, on-the-record individuals; the one fully named case is a decade-old death, which is a tonal and currency mismatch.
4. **College admissions test-prep arms race** — the aggregate economics are solid and well-reported; I could not find a named, on-the-record student or family in the "everyone hates this, does it anyway" middle of the distribution (the named figures I found are all sellers — a tutor, a consultant — not buyers, which changes the story from "trapped" to "profiting").
5. **California salmon fishery closures (2023–2025)** — real, named, recent, but the dominant causal story in the reporting is water allocation and drought, which pulls it into contested California water politics. Also redundant with Ch. 1's fishery case at the level of "reader sees two fish stories back to back."

## 1. Residency application inflation — RANK 1

**The people:** J. Bryan Carmody, MD — pediatric nephrologist, associate program director at
Eastern Virginia Medical School, and lead author of the peer-reviewed paper that names the
phenomenon. He also writes as "The Sheriff of Sodium," a widely read commentator on medical
education who has been pushing this critique publicly for roughly five years. Also: Rachel
Kudrna, a fourth-year student at Washington State University's Elson S. Floyd College of
Medicine, quoted on the record in AAMC's own news outlet; Leila Harrison, PhD, WSU's senior
associate dean for admissions and student affairs, quoted in the same piece describing
advising students against over-applying and watching them do it anyway. Also: Anmol
Shrestha, a second-year medical student at California University of Science and Medicine,
who wrote a first-person account of the *research* arms race (a sub-species of the same
problem) for STAT News under his own name.

**Place / dates:** National (U.S. residency Match, administered by AAMC's Electronic
Residency Application Service and NRMP). Core data span 2007–2021 (Carmody et al., published
March 2021); AAMC News piece dated September 13, 2022; STAT News piece dated April 29, 2024;
the partial self-correction (see below) is dated to the 2024–2025 and 2025–2026 cycles.

**What happened:** Over roughly a decade and a half, the average number of residency
applications submitted per U.S. medical graduate climbed steadily — from a 2020 baseline of
70 per U.S. grad (139 for international grads), which the peer-reviewed literature describes
as "approximately twice as many...as they did a decade ago." By 2021, U.S. grads were
submitting 72.8 applications on average, up 24% from just 2017. No individual applicant
benefits from this — a program can only train so many residents, and everyone applying to
more programs doesn't change the number of spots — but any individual applicant who applies
to fewer programs than their peers lowers their own odds of matching anywhere. So everyone
keeps adding programs to the list. Programs, in turn, drown: one program estimated the cost
of lost faculty time per interviewee at $3,736. Applicants pay too — $1,499 in ERAS fees
alone at the 2020 mean of 70 applications, plus travel that "can exceed $10,000" for
competitive specialties. A parallel version of the same dynamic plays out in research
output: neurosurgery applicants now average 29 listed research activities, more than most
PhD candidates publish by the end of a doctorate, and "the average unmatched applicant today
reports more research items than the average matched applicant did 10 years ago" (Shrestha,
STAT).

**The cost:** Financial (thousands of dollars per applicant, thousands more per program),
and — this is the part that fits the brief precisely — nobody thinks the outcome is better.
Carmody's own line, describing decline as a symptom rather than a cure: application volume
is "like fever, or metabolic acidosis, or sinus tachycardia. It's just an outward
manifestation of internal problems." Leila Harrison, on students who apply to dozens of
programs against her explicit advice: "It's heartbreaking because this involves so much
money and effort. Even though we advise students not to do it, they do it anyway."

**The one vivid detail that makes it land:** A program director whose job is literally to
advise students not to do the thing, watching them do it anyway, for reasons that are
individually sound. That's the whole book's thesis in one sentence, from a source who isn't
trying to make a philosophical point.

**Why it fits the everyone-hates-this shape:** This is about as clean a case of the
structure as exists in real, current, documented life. Applicants know the system is
absurd. Program directors know it. The professional associations that run it know it — AAMC
built an entire new feature (signaling) specifically to fix it. Nobody is a villain. The
outcome — a burned-out applicant pool, an exhausted faculty reviewing thousands of
applications nobody reads closely, worse holistic review — serves no one's interests, and
every individual choice inside it is the locally rational one.

**Factual risks:**
- **Currency / resolution risk, the big one.** The system partially *did* fix itself.
  Preliminary 2024–2025 cycle data show a roughly 7.3% decline in applications per
  applicant overall (up to 30–45% in some specialties, driven by the new "signaling"
  feature and interview caps). This is good news journalistically but a problem for the
  prologue's ending, which wants to land on an open question, not a solved one. **The
  chapter must not claim this is current-day unresolved without qualification** — it should
  either frame the case in the past tense of the buildup (which ran essentially unchecked
  for over a decade before any fix arrived) or note the fix explicitly and explain why it
  doesn't retire the phenomenon (the research arms race, which the fix does nothing for,
  is still intensifying as of the 2024 STAT piece). Flagged in detail in `evidence.md`.
- The Carmody et al. figure ("approximately twice as many...as they did a decade ago") was
  extracted via automated fetch from the PMC full text, not read directly by a human eye
  against the PDF. High confidence it's a faithful rendering (Cureus is open-access and the
  passage reads like verbatim article prose, not a summary), but the Verifier should
  re-pull the primary PDF before this goes final.
- Cureus is a lower-prestige, pay-to-publish-adjacent open-access venue relative to NEJM or
  JAMA (it does use peer review, and this specific paper is widely cited in the medical
  education literature, including by AAMC itself — but it's worth knowing the venue isn't
  top-tier if a referee wants to make something of it).
- "50,413 applicants registered for the 2024 match" was surfaced via a web search summary
  from an AMA advice article, not independently fetched and confirmed against NRMP's own
  release. Treat as REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE until re-checked.

## 2. Third-party delivery-app commissions — RANK 2

**The people:** Javier Trujillo, owner of five restaurants in Omaha, Nebraska — Javi's
Tacos, Time to Rise and Shine, Frosty Mug, Helados Locos, and El Milagro — profiled by name
in Entrepreneur (April 28, 2026). Aggregate/industry side: National Restaurant Association
data on operator profitability; DoorDash and Uber Eats's own published commission tiers.

**Place / dates:** Omaha, Nebraska; the reporting is dated April 2026, describing the prior
year's (2025) commission costs. The broader commission-fee dynamic (DoorDash tiers of 15%,
25%, 30%; Uber Eats comparable) is current as of 2024–2026 sourcing.

**What happened:** Trujillo's five restaurants paid $188,000 in delivery-platform
commissions in a single year. He described losing money specifically on orders where a
driver never showed to pick up food that was already made: "Maybe there was not enough
Dashers in the area and they took the order, we get the order, we made the food, but nobody
comes to pick it up. That's losses on us." Independent restaurants nationally run on
razor-thin net margins — commonly cited at 3–5% — against delivery commissions of 15–30% per
order, meaning a single third-party order can erase the entire in-house profit on the plate
and go negative. Trujillo eventually dropped the apps. Most operators don't: reporting on
restaurant economics describes owners as unable to "afford, or at least don't think they can
afford," to walk away from a channel that drives visibility and order volume, even while it
compresses their margins toward zero.

**The cost:** For Trujillo specifically, $188,000/year across five locations. Industry-wide,
NRA data cited in reporting puts 42% of operators as currently unprofitable, in an industry
where third-party delivery is now a structural, not optional, cost center.

**The one vivid detail that makes it land:** The made food nobody picks up. The restaurant
did everything right — took the order, cooked it, had it ready — and lost money anyway,
because of a mechanism it doesn't control and can't opt out of without giving up the order
volume the apps also bring in.

**Why it fits the everyone-hates-this shape:** Every actor is locally rational. Restaurants
join the apps because customers now expect to find them there and a competitor's presence on
DoorDash draws the customer who would otherwise have called direct. Customers use the apps
because they're the path of least resistance. The apps charge what the market will bear
because that's what a platform with dominant market share (DoorDash alone is reported at
roughly two-thirds of observed U.S. meal-delivery sales) does. Nobody in the chain is
behaving unreasonably, and the equilibrium — thin-margin restaurants bleeding a third of
each order's revenue to a platform, in a business with **no** cartel-like ability for
restaurants to collectively negotiate — makes everyone but the platform worse off.

**Factual risks:**
- **The strongest documented individual case is an escape, not a trap.** Trujillo *did*
  leave the platforms. That's a better story about agency than about the "everyone hates it
  and it happens anyway" inevitability the brief wants — the Drafter would need to frame him
  as the exception that proves how hard the trap is for everyone who *doesn't* have his
  specific position (five locations, presumably enough foot traffic/brand recognition to
  survive without app visibility), not as evidence the trap is easily escaped.
  **This needs to be handled carefully or it undercuts the chapter's point.**
  I could not find comparably well-documented reporting on a restaurant that shut down
  primarily attributable to delivery commissions (several general pieces on rising
  restaurant closures cite delivery fees as one of several pressures, not a sole cause).
- A second quote I found ("Ya Siam," a Thai restaurant, "highest sales month ever but made
  less money") is sourced only to a self-published Medium post (dated September 2025), not
  vetted journalism. **Do not use this quote or restaurant name without independent
  verification** — I'm flagging it here only so the Drafter or Verifier doesn't rediscover
  it and assume it's clean. It is not in `evidence.md` as usable.
- The specific commission percentages (15/25/30% tiers) are current as of the vendor sites
  themselves (DoorDash's own merchant pricing page) rather than independent audit — vendors
  have some incentive to understate effective cost, and reporting notes effective costs can
  run higher (30–40%) once ancillary fees are included. Confidence and sourcing detail in
  `evidence.md`.

## 3. Junior-employee overwork / "everyone stays late" culture — RANK 3

**The people:** Best-documented aggregate case is the 2021 Goldman Sachs first-year analyst
survey — 13 junior analysts who compiled and circulated an internal presentation reporting
95–100 hour work weeks, ~5 hours of sleep a night, and self-rated mental/physical health
declines of over six points on a ten-point scale since starting. Quotes are on the record
but the individuals are not named (standard practice for internal whistleblower-adjacent
documents; Goldman's own leadership responded publicly with a "protected Saturdays" policy).
A single fully named, on-the-record individual case exists but is dated and heavy: Moritz
Erhardt, a 21-year-old German student on a summer internship at Bank of America Merrill
Lynch in London, who died in August 2013 after reportedly working through multiple
all-nighters in the final days of a seven-week internship; an inquest attributed the death to
natural causes related to a pre-existing epilepsy condition but the case triggered
industry-wide scrutiny of intern hours and Bank of America changed its internship policy
afterward.

**Place / dates:** Goldman Sachs survey — global, reported March 2021 (Forbes, CNBC, CNN).
Erhardt case — London, August 2013.

**What happened / the shape:** Nobody wants to be the first to leave the office, because
leaving first reads as low commitment in a culture that rewards visible hours over output;
so everyone stays, including the people who privately think the norm is stupid and harmful.
This is a clean stag-hunt/coordination-failure shape and is extremely well attested at the
level of survey data, management literature, and first-person online testimony (Ask a
Manager reader letters, described but not independently verifiable). But **I could not find
a single, small, recent, named, on-the-record individual case** that isn't either (a)
anonymous aggregate survey data or (b) the 2013 Erhardt death, which is thirteen years old
(fails "recent") and disproportionately dramatic relative to the brief's request for a case
where "everyone involved is behaving reasonably" and the badness is structural rather than
a tragedy with a single proximate cause (an inquest, notably, did not even establish a direct
causal link between the hours and the death).

**The cost:** Self-reported health decline in the Goldman case; in the Erhardt case, a death,
though causally contested.

**The one vivid detail:** "My body physically hurts all the time and mentally I'm in a really
dark place" (one anonymous Goldman analyst) is visceral but anonymous, which weakens it for a
book that wants named, specific people. Erhardt's case has a genuinely devastating detail —
he collapsed in his flat after what colleagues described as three consecutive nights working
until 6 a.m. — but the causal story is legally and medically contested, which is a real
factual risk for a book whose whole thesis is intellectual honesty about what the evidence
supports.

**Why it fits the everyone-hates-this shape:** Structurally, this might be the *purest*
version of the pattern in the whole list — a coordination trap with no external villain, no
malicious actor, and full common knowledge that the norm is dysfunctional. It's also the
easiest for essentially every reader to recognize from their own working life, which is a
real strength for a prologue trying to hook a general audience immediately.

**Factual risks:**
- The core weakness is sourcing to *named* individuals as the brief specifically requires.
  This is the case where I'd recommend the Showrunner decide whether "vivid and universally
  recognizable, but anonymous-sourced" beats "less universal, but fully named and cited" —
  because on the current search results, this case can't have both.
- If Erhardt is used, it needs careful handling: the inquest's finding of "natural causes"
  and the epilepsy factor must be stated, not elided, or the case becomes a misleading
  implication of a causal claim the record doesn't support. That tension itself may be too
  much text for a 2,000-word prologue to carry responsibly.

## 4. College admissions test-prep arms race — RANK 4

**The people:** On the seller side, well documented and named: Adam Nguyen, founder of Ivy
Link (a former Columbia admissions reader/interviewer), profiled in Fortune (December 17,
2024) charging $100,000–$750,000 for long-horizon admissions consulting to
high-net-worth families, with clients reportedly starting as early as fifth grade. SAT/ACT
tutors charging up to $500/hour are documented in Bloomberg (March 19, 2024) reporting on
the SAT's return to required status at several elite universities. On the buyer side —
the actual "everyone hates this, does it anyway" families — I did not find a named,
on-the-record profile within this session's search budget; the reporting I found describes
the phenomenon and cites sellers, not a specific named family caught inside the arms race
against their own preferences.

**Place / dates:** National (U.S. elite college admissions). Reporting dated 2024.

**What happened:** As more applicants buy test prep, tutoring, and consulting, the
competitive baseline rises for everyone, so families who would rather not spend $500/hour on
a tutor feel they can't unilaterally opt out without disadvantaging their kid relative to
peers who do spend it. This is a textbook positional arms race — same shape as the residency
case — but the reporting available skews toward describing the sellers and the price points
rather than a specific buyer who says, on the record, "I hate this and I'm doing it anyway."

**The cost:** Individual families: tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars. Structurally:
compounding inequality, since the escalation is a tax that only wealthier families can fully
pay, which shifts this case toward a fairness/inequality story rather than a pure
everyone's-in-the-same-trap story — a subtly different shape than the brief wants, since part
of the point of the brief's cases is that the trap catches people *symmetrically*, not that
it's an arms race only the well-resourced can run.

**The one vivid detail:** Nguyen's own framing — steering clients toward "aristocratic
sports" (equestrian, fencing, sailing, squash) specifically because "you need to identify a
sport that, frankly, most people can't afford to play" — is vivid, but it characterizes the
arms race as a mechanism for preserving advantage rather than a trap nobody wanted, which
cuts against the prologue's argument that this happens even among people acting in good
faith.

**Why it fits (or doesn't) the everyone-hates-this shape:** It fits the "arms race" pattern
structurally, but the best-documented human specifics skew toward people extracting value
from the arms race (consultants, tutors) rather than people trapped by it, which is a
meaningfully different story than Ch. 1's cynic-proof point (Newfoundland fishermen who saw
the collapse coming and couldn't stop it even though nobody was winning). This case currently
has *winners*, clearly identified and named. That is a liability against the brief.

**Factual risks:**
- No named buyer-side individual found. Would need further research before Gate 1 if the
  Showrunner wants this case; I'd recommend against it as a top pick for that reason alone.
- The consulting-industry figures ($100K–$750K) come from a single Fortune profile of a
  single firm; treat as illustrative of the top of the market, not representative of a
  typical affected family.

## 5. California salmon fishery closures, 2023–2025 — RANK 5

**The people:** Jared Davis, captain of the charter boat Salty Lady; Aaron Newman, captain
of the Maria Isabel, who switched to crabbing when salmon season was cancelled — both named
in coverage of the closures (CalMatters and related outlets).

**Place / dates:** California and parts of southern Oregon coast. Commercial and most
recreational Chinook salmon fishing closed in 2023, closed again in 2024 (second consecutive
year), and reporting describes a third consecutive closure into 2025. The 2024 Sacramento
River fall-run Chinook count (99,274 returning fish) was reported as substantially lower than
2023's already-low count.

**What happened:** Multiple consecutive years of total or near-total closure of California's
commercial and recreational salmon fishery, driven by collapsed Chinook returns to the
Sacramento and Klamath rivers, devastating for fishing families and charter operators up and
down the coast, with the state seeking federal disaster relief funds.

**The cost:** Multi-year loss of income for an entire commercial and charter fishing sector;
state-level disaster declarations.

**The one vivid detail:** A charter captain who now takes his boat out for crab instead of
salmon, because there's no salmon left to fish for.

**Why it's ranked last:**
- **Redundant with Ch. 1.** Ch. 1's brief is already locked to Newfoundland cod (1992) as
  its opening case. Putting a second fishery collapse in the Prologue, four to five thousand
  words before Ch. 1's cod case, means the reader encounters the same species of disaster
  twice in short order — undercutting the impact of both.
- **Political entanglement risk, which the brief explicitly rules out.** The dominant causal
  narrative in the reporting is water allocation — how much Sacramento and Klamath river
  water goes to Central Valley agriculture versus fish flows — which is one of the most
  contested and partisan resource-policy fights in California politics (it runs through
  Governor Newsom, the state's agricultural lobby, and federal water contracts). This is a
  meaningfully different causal shape than Newfoundland cod (driven by fishing effort
  itself, a cleaner coordination-failure story) and risks reading as a proxy for a live
  political fight over water rights, which the brief says to avoid ("too politically
  pre-loaded, and the reader has defenses up" — same logic the brief applies to climate
  change).
- It is also not obviously a case of individually rational actors producing the bad outcome
  through their own choices in the way cod or residency applications are — the fishermen
  didn't cause this by each independently deciding to overfish; the causal chain runs through
  upstream water management decisions made by other parties entirely. That's a different,
  and for this brief, weaker, fit to "everyone involved is behaving reasonably" *and*
  contributing to the outcome through their own reasonable choices.

**Factual risks:** None significant on the facts as reported (closures and figures are
well-attested across multiple outlets), but the shape-fit and political-overlap problems
above are why this ranks last regardless of factual soundness.

## Argument for #1

Residency application inflation is the only one of the five candidates that clears every
constraint in the brief simultaneously: it's small in scope (a professional subculture, not
a planetary system), real and independently verifiable through peer-reviewed literature and
an institution's own published data, recent (the core statistics run through 2021–2022, with
a live, still-unfolding 2024–2026 postscript), and non-political in the sense the brief
means — nobody's partisan priors are engaged by residency applications the way they would be
by, say, water rights or minimum wage.

It's also the cleanest structural match to the book's actual thesis. Every actor — the
applicant, the program director, the dean advising against over-applying — is behaving
exactly as a reasonable person in their position should behave, given what everyone else is
doing. The outcome serves no one. And critically, it has the single detail that makes the
book's whole argument legible in one image: a program director whose literal job includes
telling students not to do the thing they are about to watch those same students do anyway,
for reasons she'd give the same advice against and still can't argue them out of. That is
the prologue's argument, dramatized, before the book has made a single abstract claim.

The one thing the Drafter must handle with care — and the reason this isn't a slam dunk —
is the partial 2024–2025 self-correction via program signaling. The honest version of this
case is not "this problem has no solution and never will," it's "this ran essentially
unchecked, with everyone watching it happen and hating it, for the better part of two
decades, before anyone found a fix — and the fix (which is itself a case of the very
`mechanism design` idea this book will spend its first four chapters on) doesn't touch the
adjacent research-inflation arms race, which was still getting worse as of the most recent
reporting I could find." That's a more honest ending than pretending it's unsolved, and it
still lands exactly where the brief wants: on the question, not the answer.
