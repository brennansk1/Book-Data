## CASE 1 — Residency application inflation

### Primary source

**Carmody JB, Rosman IS, Carlson JC. "Application Fever: Reviewing the Causes, Costs, and
Cures for Residency Application Inflation." Cureus. 2021 Mar 10;13(3):e13804.**
Open access. PMCID: PMC8034763. Authors: J. Bryan Carmody (Pediatrics, Eastern Virginia
Medical School, Norfolk); Ilana S. Rosman (Dermatology, Washington University School of
Medicine, St. Louis); John C. Carlson (Pediatrics, Tulane University School of Medicine, New
Orleans).

Confidence on the citation itself: **SOLID** — title, authors, journal, volume/issue,
article number, and PMCID were all returned consistently. Confidence on the exact wording of
quoted passages below: **SOLID with a caveat** — text was extracted via automated tool from
the PMC HTML rendering, not read directly. It reads as verbatim article prose (not a
paraphrase register), so I have high confidence it's faithful, but the Verifier should
re-confirm word-for-word against the PDF before it's set in the manuscript, since even one
dropped or added word matters in a book about epistemic honesty.

**Claim 1 — the headline statistic.**
> "In 2020, the average U.S. medical graduate applied to 70 residency programs, while the
> average international medical graduate (IMG) applied to 139. Both groups of applicants
> apply to approximately twice as many residency programs as they did a decade ago."

Use: this is the single load-bearing statistic for the case — "roughly doubled in a decade"
is directly supported, not an inference. **Confidence: SOLID**, pending the word-for-word
re-check noted above. The 2007–2020 mean-applications-per-applicant series is presented as
Figure 1 in the source article (captioned per the extraction as "Mean number of applications
submitted per applicant, Electronic Residency Application Service, 2007-2020") — the Verifier
should pull the actual figure for a possible chart/paraphrase in the chapter, though per
STYLE_BIBLE the prose should carry this, not a chart.

**Claim 2 — cost to applicants.**
> "A student applying to 70 programs — the current mean for U.S. students — pays $1,499 in
> ERAS fees alone."
> "Attending in-person interviews can exceed $10,000 for competitive specialties."

**Confidence: SOLID**, same caveat as above. Note for the Drafter: the $1,499 figure is ERAS
fees only, at 2020-era ERAS pricing, and does not include the interview travel cost, which is
separate and larger. **Note also**: ERAS fee structure has since changed (see "currency"
section below) — this exact dollar figure is dated to the 2020 mean and should be presented
as such, not as current pricing.

**Claim 3 — cost to programs.**
> "One program estimated the cost of lost faculty clinical productivity at $3,736 per
> interviewee."

**Confidence: SOLID** (same sourcing caveat). This is a single program's estimate, not a
national average — the "one program" language should be preserved if quoted, not smoothed
into an implied national figure.

**Claim 4 — interview counts rising too.**
> "The median number of interviews completed by a successful applicant has increased from 10
> to 13 over the past decade."

**Confidence: SOLID** (same caveat).

**What the article does NOT contain:** No direct, named quotations from applicants or
program directors — it's an analytic/policy piece, not a narrative one. The human-voice
material below comes from separate sources.

### The currency problem — must be handled explicitly

This is the single most important thing in this packet for the Drafter and Showrunner to see
before Gate 1.

**The core application-count arms race has partially self-corrected.** Multiple sources
(Medscape, AAMC's own account, and general search-aggregated preliminary NRMP/AAMC data)
describe a **7.3% decline in average applications per applicant in the 2024–2025 cycle**,
with individual specialties seeing 30–45% declines (dermatology reportedly fell from 72
applications per applicant in 2023 to 40 in 2025; otolaryngology fell 42.5%). The mechanism
credited is "Program Signaling" — a feature AAMC added to ERAS letting applicants flag a
limited number of programs as genuine top choices, which lets programs weight those
applications more heavily and, per the reporting, reduces the incentive to blanket-apply.

**Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE at the level of exact percentages** — these numbers
came from search-engine-aggregated summaries of preliminary AAMC/NRMP releases and a
Medscape article, not from a directly fetched and read primary AAMC data table. The
directional claim (applications per applicant declining since signaling was introduced) is
corroborated across multiple independent mentions and I'd rate it **SOLID as a direction**,
but the specific percentages (7.3%, 42%, 42.5%) need a direct primary-source pull before
they're printed as numbers rather than as "the AAMC reports a meaningful decline."

**What this means for the chapter:** the Drafter cannot honestly write this as "and it's
still happening exactly like this today, unresolved." The honest version is: this ran
essentially unaddressed for roughly fifteen years, with everyone inside it — applicants,
deans, program directors — describing it in exactly the terms the book will later use
(individually rational, collectively destructive, known and hated by every party), before
institutions found a partial fix. That fifteen-year run, not the current state, is where the
prologue's case lives. If the chapter wants a thread that's still live and unresolved as of
the most recent reporting, that's the *research* arms race (Shrestha, above), which the
signaling fix does not touch.

This is exactly the kind of finding this role is supposed to flag per the standing
instructions ("never state a contested empirical claim at high confidence... where the
literature is genuinely divided, the text says so") — not contested exactly, but
time-sensitive in a way that could make the chapter look sloppy or dated if handled carelessly.

## CASE 2 — Third-party delivery-app commissions

### Primary named case

**Entrepreneur. "$188K in Delivery Fees Forced This Restaurant Owner to Ditch DoorDash and
Uber Eats: 'That's Losses on Us.'" entrepreneur.com, published April 28, 2026.**

**Confidence: SOLID** on the core facts (named individual, named restaurants, a specific
dollar figure, direct quotes) — Entrepreneur is a staffed editorial outlet and this reads as
a standard reported business-news piece, not a syndicated aggregation.

- **Named individual:** Javier Trujillo.
- **Location:** Omaha, Nebraska.
- **Restaurants (five, all named):** Javi's Tacos, Time to Rise and Shine, Frosty Mug,
  Helados Locos, El Milagro.
- **Headline figure:** "$188,000 last year" in delivery-platform fees across the five
  locations, in Trujillo's own words:
  > "Any service that we use that does delivery and then pushes our menu to their customers,
  > we have paid $188,000 last year."
- **The vivid detail, exact quote:**
  > "Maybe there was not enough Dashers in the area and they took the order, we get the
  > order, we made the food, but nobody comes to pick it up. That's losses on us."
- **What actually happened:** Trujillo stopped using DoorDash, Uber Eats, and the other
  delivery platforms — **the restaurants did not close.** This is important: the piece is a
  story of a successful exit, not a story of someone trapped who couldn't get out. **The
  Drafter needs to be careful not to imply otherwise.** See risk note below.

### Structural / aggregate data (industry-wide, not Trujillo-specific)

- **Commission rates:** DoorDash's own published merchant pricing lists three tiers —
  "Basic" 15%, "Plus" 25%, "Premier" 30% — sourced directly to DoorDash's merchant pricing
  page. Uber Eats reported as comparable, 15–30% depending on plan and factors like distance
  and order size. **Confidence: SOLID** for DoorDash's own stated tiers (primary source, the
  vendor's own pricing page); **REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE** for the claim that effective
  costs run higher (30–40%) once ancillary fees are included — this came from an industry
  blog (Rezku) rather than an independent audit, and vendors clearly have an interest in the
  headline number looking lower than the effective one, so some gap is plausible but the
  specific 30–40% range is not independently confirmed.
- **Restaurant margins:** "The average independent restaurant runs on a 3–5% net profit
  margin" — this figure recurs across multiple industry-trade sources (Restaurant Business
  Online, OPA, Rezku) independently, which raises confidence, but all ultimately trace to
  general industry-margin conventions rather than one traceable primary dataset I could pin
  down this session. **Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE-CLASS** (consistent across
  several trade outlets, none of which cite an original underlying study I could verify
  directly) — safe to use as "commonly cited," not safe to cite as if from a single
  authoritative study.
- **Market share:** "As of March 2024, DoorDash captured approximately 67% of observed US
  meal-delivery sales, with Uber Eats second at 23%" — sourced in aggregate search results
  to Bloomberg Second Measure, a reputable transaction-data analytics firm that publishes
  this kind of tracking regularly. **Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE** as extracted
  this session (the original Second Measure datapoint page was in the search results but not
  independently fetched and read) — should be re-pulled directly, since Second Measure
  updates this figure regularly and the exact percentage will drift by quarter.
- **Operator profitability:** "42% of operators say they are not profitable" and industry
  profitability "has declined since 2019" — attributed to the National Restaurant
  Association in search-aggregated summaries. **Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE** —
  I was blocked (403) from fetching NRA's own report pages directly this session and could
  not independently confirm the 42% figure against a primary NRA document. **Flag for the
  Verifier: re-pull the NRA "State of the Restaurant Industry" report directly before this
  number is used.**

### The framing risk — must be handled explicitly

The brief wants a case where "it happens anyway" — where no one can unilaterally escape. The
best-documented named individual in this case **did** escape, successfully, and the
restaurants kept operating. That's a real tension with the shape the prologue needs.

Two honest ways to handle it, for the Drafter to choose between (not for the Researcher to
resolve):
1. Frame Trujillo as evidence of how bad the equilibrium is even for someone with the
   standing to leave — five locations, an established local customer base — while the
   article and industry data (3–5% margins against 15–30% commissions, 42% of operators
   reportedly unprofitable) carry the case for why most operators can't follow him.
2. Find a different named individual for this specific slot before Gate 1 — someone who
   stayed on the platforms while saying, on the record, that they hate it and can't leave.
   I did not find one who meets the brief's "named, real, verified" bar within this
   session's search budget; the "Ya Siam" quote above was the closest hit and it's not
   usable as sourced.

I'd flag this as a live open item rather than something I can resolve as the Researcher —
it's a judgment call about how to frame a true story, which is Drafter/Showrunner territory,
not a missing fact I can go find.
