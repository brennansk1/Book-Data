Role: Researcher. No manuscript prose below. Every claim is tagged SOLID / SINGLE-SOURCE / CONTESTED. "SOLID" means corroborated by a primary document (government report, court filing, deposition transcript, DOJ agreement) or by multiple independent secondary accounts that agree on the specifics. ## 2. GM IGNITION SWITCH (opening case, backup recommendation)

| Claim | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Ray DeGiorgio, GM's ignition-switch design engineer, approved the switch used in the Saturn Ion (and later Chevy Cobalt and other platforms) despite it not meeting GM's own torque specification. | Anton Valukas's internal investigation report, commissioned by GM's board and released June 2014 (widely reported, e.g. NBC News, ASEE Prism); this is a primary document written for and released by GM itself. | SOLID |

| On April 26, 2006, DeGiorgio signed off on a redesigned switch (increased detent plunger force) without issuing a new GM part number. | GM recalled 2.6 million vehicles beginning in early 2014; CEO Mary Barra stated the Valukas-led internal review found a pattern of "misconduct or incompetence" and specifically stated the review found **no evidence of an organized cover-up**; GM fired 15 employees, including DeGiorgio. | Washington Post, CBS News, and Valukas Report summary coverage, June 2014. | SOLID |
| GM's own compensation program (administered by Kenneth Feinberg, independent of GM) ultimately recognized 124 deaths and hundreds of injuries linked to the ignition-switch defect. | Widely reported figure from the Feinberg compensation fund's final numbers (multiple outlets, 2015–2016). | SOLID |

**Important nuance for the chapter, not to be flattened:** GM's own internally-commissioned Valukas Report explicitly concluded there was **no evidence of a coordinated cover-up** — the finding was individual "misconduct or incompetence" distributed across many people and years, each acting within what looked like a defensible bureaucratic category (safety vs. customer-satisfaction; part-number change vs. running change). This is actually a *better* fit for the chapter's thesis than a conspiracy narrative would be: the brief specifically wants a case where individuals applied "defensible-sounding exceptions," not a case of coordinated concealment. GM is arguably the cleaner example of that specific mechanism; Challenger has more dramatic unity of place and time (one room, one night) but involves fewer distinct "exceptions" being applied by different people — it's closer to one bad decision made by consensus under pressure. The Drafter/Showrunner should weigh dramatic unity (favors Challenger) against structural fit to "everyone applied their own defensible exception" (favors GM, or a chapter that uses both).

## 3. MILGRAM — compliance figures, with the caveats the brief explicitly requires

**The headline figure:** In Milgram's original 1963 baseline condition at Yale (voice-feedback condition, 40 male subjects), **65% (26 of 40) administered the maximum 450-volt shock.** This is SOLID — it is Milgram's own reported figure from the 1963 paper ("Behavior Study of Obedience," *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*) and is the figure universally cited as "the" Milgram result.

**The caveats the chapter must include, per the brief and per current scholarship:**

1. **65% is one condition out of roughly 24 that Milgram ran**, with obedience rates varying enormously across variations (from near-zero to over 90%) depending on proximity to the victim, proximity to the authority figure, and other manipulated variables. Citing "65%" without noting it is one data point among many that moved a great deal under different conditions overstates the finding's generality. CONTESTED in the sense that "the" Milgram number is really a family of numbers.

2. **Gina Perry's archival work** (*Behind the Shock Machine*, 2013, and subsequent peer-reviewed papers, e.g. Perry, Brannigan, Wanner & Stam, "Credibility and Incredulity in Milgram's Obedience Experiments: A Reanalysis of an Unpublished Test," *Journal of Social Psychology* / SAGE, 2020) found, from years in the Yale archives and interviews with surviving subjects, that: (a) many subjects expressed skepticism during the experiment about whether the shocks were real, and (b) in a large share of experimental variations (Perry reports 18 of 23), subjects who **fully believed** the shocks were real showed **lower** mean obedience than subjects who were skeptical — meaning belief in the cover story and obedience were not straightforwardly aligned, which complicates the popular "ordinary people will torture a stranger on command" reading. This is SOLID as a description of Perry's published findings; it is itself part of an ongoing scholarly debate (some Milgram defenders dispute aspects of Perry's interpretation), so the debate itself, not just Perry's conclusion, should be gestured at.

3. Perry's broader claim — that Milgram's own published accounts of his procedures and debriefing contained inconsistencies relative to the archival record and subject interviews — is documented in her work but is itself a contested historiographical claim, not a settled consensus finding. **Rate this CONTESTED**, not SOLID, if the chapter cites it.

**Recommended chapter-safe formulation:** cite the 65% baseline figure as real and well-established, immediately note it is one condition among many that varied widely, and flag — in a single sentence, at moderate confidence — that subsequent archival work (Perry) has complicated the standard "ordinary people will do terrible things" reading without overturning the basic finding that obedience to authority substantially overrides many people's own stated moral commitments under laboratory pressure. Do not present 65% as an uncomplicated, unitary fact, per the brief's own instruction and per CANON §7 (standing instruction 2: never state a contested empirical claim at high confidence).

## 4. KAHNEMAN / HOT-COLD EMPATHY GAP — correction to the brief

**The brief cites "Kahneman on hot-cold empathy gaps." This attribution needs correcting before drafting.**

The hot-cold empathy gap as a named concept and research program belongs to **George Loewenstein** (Carnegie Mellon), not Daniel Kahneman:

- Loewenstein, G. (1996). "Out of Control: Visceral Influences on Behavior." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 65(3), 272–292. — introduces the role of visceral factors (hunger, pain, craving, emotion) in producing gaps between people's predicted and actual behavior. SOLID.
- Loewenstein, G. (2005). "Hot-Cold Empathy Gaps and Medical Decision Making." *Health Psychology*, 24(4, Suppl.), S49–S56. — the paper that names and formalizes the "hot-cold empathy gap" specifically. SOLID.

Kahneman's connection is real but indirect and should not be the citation used for this specific term: Loewenstein's framework draws on the general Kahneman–Tversky behavioral-economics tradition (prospect theory, state-dependent preference), and Kahneman's own later work (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011) discusses related affective-forecasting and planning-fallacy material, but Kahneman did not originate or co-author the hot-cold empathy gap literature specifically. **If the chapter wants a Kahneman citation for the same underlying point** (that people in a "cold" deliberative state cannot accurately predict how they'll reason or feel in a "hot" state, which is precisely the failure mode Ch. 9/10 need — the person who feels certain in advance that they'd never rationalize an exception, and then does), the more defensible move is either (a) cite Loewenstein directly and drop the Kahneman attribution, or (b) cite Kahneman for the adjacent and well-established affective-forecasting/planning-fallacy material and cite Loewenstein separately for hot-cold empathy gaps specifically. **Recommend (a).** This should go back to the Canon Keeper / Showrunner as a brief-correction, not silently fixed.

## 5. JACKALL — Moral Mazes (context source, not case-specific)

Robert Jackall, *Moral Mazes: The World of Corporate Managers* (Oxford University Press, 1988). Ethnographic study of corporate managers. Central line, quoted from a manager Jackall interviewed: **"What is right in the corporation is what the guy above you wants from you."** SOLID — widely quoted and consistent across secondary sources describing the book; page-level citation should be confirmed by the Verifier against the original text before use as a direct quotation (I was not able to confirm the exact page number in this pass).

Jackall's broader argument — that corporate bureaucratic structure detaches managers' operative ethics from any fixed personal conviction and re-grounds it in relationships with whoever currently has power over them — is directly relevant to both Challenger and GM as background theory (it is not itself a "case," it is the interpretive frame the chapter can use to explain *why* individually reasonable people in a hierarchy keep producing this pattern). SOLID as a description of the book's thesis.

| Claim | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|

| In November 1966, Buxtun filed a formal written protest with the PHS Division of Venereal Diseases on ethical grounds; it was rejected on the stated grounds that the study was not yet complete. | Government Accountability Project; corroborated by JTA and Times of Israel obituary coverage. | SOLID (multiple independent obituary/profile sources agree on date and substance; I did not reach the original 1966 memo itself in this pass) |
| Buxtun re-filed a protest in 1968, arguing the men were "quite ignorant of the effects of untreated syphilis"; a PHS panel again reviewed and rejected the objection. | Same sources as above. | SOLID (same caveat — secondary-source corroborated, not primary-document-in-hand) |
| After the internal channel failed him twice, Buxtun's information reached Associated Press reporter Jean Heller, and the story broke in the press in July 1972. | Widely corroborated across all sources on the Tuskegee study's public exposure. | SOLID |
| The study was halted within months of the 1972 exposé; a class-action settlement followed in 1973; the episode directly contributed to the National Research Act (1974) and the creation of the modern Institutional Review Board system and the Belmont Report (1979). | Standard, well-documented history of U.S. research-ethics regulation; consistent across historical and bioethics literature. | SOLID |

**Why this is the strongest available whistleblower case for interlocutor (b), "your gates would have stopped me":** Buxtun's story is not "an individual broke the rules and was vindicated." It is "an individual used the institution's own internal-review process, twice, over six years, and the institution's own gate rejected him both times" — because the institution running the review was the same one invested in the study continuing. This directly exercises CANON §6.3's point that documented review can be captured, and is the strongest available real-world argument for why the framework needs the sealed/delayed-documentation fix and the time-sensitive-capture exemption, rather than relying on internal review alone.

## 7. CYNTHIA COOPER / WORLDCOM (whistleblower counter-case, secondary recommendation)

| Claim | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| In summer 2002, Cynthia Cooper, WorldCom's VP of Internal Audit, and her team discovered approximately $3.8 billion in fraudulent accounting entries — operating expenses improperly capitalized as capital expenditures — largely by working outside normal hours to avoid alerting CFO Scott Sullivan. | Wikipedia (Cynthia Cooper (accountant)) cross-checked against ABC News and University of Lethbridge event coverage of Cooper's own public retelling; consistent across sources. | SOLID |
| Cooper escalated her findings to Max Bobbitt, chairman of WorldCom's audit committee, rather than through her normal reporting line (which ran toward the implicated CFO). | Corroborated across multiple profile sources (techworkerhandbook.org, whistleblowerhouse.org) describing the same sequence. | SOLID |
| The fraud, at $3.8 billion (later revised upward), was at the time the largest corporate accounting fraud uncovered in U.S. history; WorldCom filed for what was then the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history in July 2002. | Standard, widely corroborated financial history (WorldCom scandal, Wikipedia and contemporaneous financial press). | SOLID |
| Cooper was named one of Time's Persons of the Year for 2002, alongside Sherron Watkins (Enron) and Coleen Rowley (FBI). | Widely reported at the time (Time, December 2002) and consistently in later retrospectives. | SOLID |

**Why this pairs well as the second whistleblower case:** unlike Buxtun, Cooper's story shows the internal-escalation gate working as designed — she had a legitimate alternate channel (the audit committee, a body specifically structured to receive exactly this kind of report independent of management) and used it. Running Buxtun and Cooper together gives the chapter both the failure mode (captured internal review) and the success mode (a gate that worked because the institution had, in fact, built a genuine alternate channel) without needing a third case.
