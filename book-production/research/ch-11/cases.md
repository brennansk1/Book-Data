## CANDIDATE 1 — Wesley Autrey, 137th Street–City College subway platform, Manhattan, January 2, 2007

**Why it fits:** This is the cleanest match to the brief's actual requirement — "deliberation was impossible and the response came from training" — because Autrey's own account explicitly denies deliberating, and because his account also names the specific trained pattern that fired (confined-space judgment from years of unionized construction work), which gives the chapter a concrete "what was actually installed" answer rather than a vague appeal to instinct.

**The scene:** At approximately 12:45 p.m., a 20-year-old film student, **Cameron Hollopeter**, had a seizure on the platform and fell onto the subway tracks as a downtown 1 train was approaching the station. Autrey, a 50-year-old U.S. Navy veteran and construction worker (Laborers' union Local 79), was on the platform with his two young daughters. He jumped onto the tracks, found he could not get Hollopeter back up onto the platform in time, and instead pinned him down in the drainage trench between the rails — a channel roughly a foot deep — and lay on top of him as the train passed over both of them. Five cars passed above before the train stopped; grease from the train's undercarriage marked Autrey's cap. Both men survived uninjured.

**The training-vs-deliberation detail the chapter needs:** Autrey has been consistent across interviews that this was not a deliberated choice. To CBS: "I did it out of a split-second reaction." To the New York Times: "I don't feel like I did something spectacular; I just saw someone who needed help. I did what I felt was right." Crucially, he also supplies the mechanism: "Since I do construction work with Local 79, we work in confined spaces a lot. So I looked, and my judgment was pretty right" — i.e., he attributes the speed and accuracy of his read of the trench's depth and the train's clearance to a specific occupational habituation, not to generic courage. This is close to a textbook case of "the response came from training" stated in the actor's own words.

**Over-familiarity risk:** LOW-MODERATE. Autrey was a major 2007 news story (profiled by TIME, received a key to the city, met President Bush) but has faded from cultural memory much further than Sullenberger; most contemporary readers will not have this case pre-loaded, which is an advantage for a chapter that wants the reader's attention on the *mechanism* rather than on recognizing the story.

**Caveat for the Drafter:** the "training" here is civilian and somewhat informal (confined-space work habits transferred to a novel life-or-death context), not a virtue-specific or ethics-specific training regime. That is arguably a feature for this brief — it demonstrates the general claim (trained response patterns fire under time pressure) without smuggling in a profession whose training is itself partly moral (firefighting, medicine), which would make the case do double duty the brief doesn't ask for. But it does mean the chapter cannot lean on Autrey to show that the trained pattern was *virtue* practice specifically, only that trained response-without-deliberation is real and can be prosocial. The interlocutor's rebuttal (character is prior to rules) could seize on this: is confined-space judgment really a moral virtue, or just a skill that happened to save a life? Recommend the chapter be explicit that Autrey illustrates the mechanism, and a different, more clearly virtue-relevant case or the Aristotle/Annas material carries the argument that the same mechanism is how virtues specifically get installed.

## CANDIDATE 2 — Chesley Sullenberger, US Airways Flight 1549, Hudson River, January 15, 2009

**Why it's the canonical choice — and why that's a problem:** This is the most famous "trained response under time pressure" story in recent American memory (subject of the 2016 Clint Eastwood film *Sully*), which is exactly why it is simultaneously the strongest and the riskiest choice for an opening case. A reader who has already metabolized this story via the film may experience the chapter's use of it as decoration rather than argument — the opposite of what an opening case is for.

**What the record actually shows (verified against the NTSB investigation and flight timeline), and why it complicates the "no deliberation" framing:**
- Bird strike occurred at 15:27:11; the aircraft ditched in the Hudson at approximately 15:30 — roughly **208 seconds** total from strike to touchdown (some sourcing gives ~179 seconds from strike to water contact; the commonly cited "208 seconds" figure spans a slightly different start point in the CVR transcript — Verifier should pin the exact NTSB-cited interval before the chapter locks a number).
- 150 passengers and 5 crew (155 total) survived.
- Sullenberger had 19,663 total flight hours, 4,765 of them in the A320, was a former Air Force fighter pilot, and was a certified glider pilot and glider instructor — the glider background is the detail most often cited as the specific trained skill that mattered, since it is unpowered-flight judgment.
- **The complicating finding:** the NTSB's own simulator testing is the strongest evidence *against* a pure "no time to think, pure reflex" framing. When test pilots attempted an immediate turn back to LaGuardia with no decision delay, they succeeded in landing 7 of 13 attempts (and 1 of 2 attempts diverting to Teterboro). But when investigators inserted a realistic ~35-second delay to account for the actual time a real crew would need to recognize the bird strike, diagnose the dual engine failure, and decide on a course of action, the LaGuardia-return scenario **crashed**. In other words: Sullenberger did not have the luxury simulator pilots had of already knowing "this is a return-to-LaGuardia drill" — he had to spend real seconds recognizing what had happened before choosing, and the NTSB's own reconstruction shows that decision process was not instantaneous. He made a judgment call, under extreme compression, to rule out both airports and ditch — that is a mixture of trained-reflex flying (the actual handling of the aircraft, informed by decades of instruction and glider work) and compressed but real deliberation (the choice among three options), not the cleaner "response came from training, no deliberation happened" story the brief's opening-case criterion asks for.
- Sullenberger's own description supports the "compressed judgment, not zero deliberation" reading: he has described "quickly compartmentaliz[ing] my mind," prioritizing "the few things that would help us the most," and having "the discipline to ignore the things I couldn't do" — language of triage under time pressure, not language of "I didn't decide anything."

**Recommendation:** Use Sullenberger, if at all, as a supporting reference or a one-paragraph second example rather than the opening scene — both because of over-familiarity and because the honest record is a better fit for a claim like "training compresses deliberation to survivable speed" than for the brief's stronger claim that "deliberation was impossible." Autrey and Arredondo make the stronger version of the argument the brief actually wants.

## CANDIDATE 3 — Carlos Arredondo, Boston Marathon bombing, Boylston Street, Boston, April 15, 2013

**Why it fits:** A first-responder-adjacent case with an unusually well-documented account of the actor naming his own trained/rehearsed pattern as the source of the response, and a genuinely vivid, specific detail (the cowboy hat, the photograph).

**The scene:** Arredondo, then 52, was at the marathon's finish-line area to see National Guard members running in honor of his son, Marine Lance Corporal Alexander Arredondo, killed in Iraq in 2004. After the second bomb detonated, Arredondo — instantly recognizable in news photographs by his cowboy hat — vaulted the security barrier and ran toward the wounded rather than away. He found **Jeff Bauman**, a spectator whose legs had been catastrophically injured by the blast, and, using strips of ripped T-shirt, applied improvised tourniquets to both legs (one just above the knee, where the leg ended in bare bone) while helping move Bauman to a wheelchair for evacuation — the resulting photograph became one of the defining images of the bombing.

**The training-vs-deliberation detail:** Arredondo attributes his response explicitly to rehearsed, embodied patterns from two prior lives: volunteer/professional work as a rodeo clown (whose entire function is to run toward danger — a charging bull — to protect a fallen rider) and firefighter/rescue training, plus experience helping injured bullfighters in Costa Rica. His own account: he "acted instinctively," and press coverage repeatedly frames the rodeo-clown background as the specific mechanism — "run toward the danger in order to keep others safe" was literally his job description before it was a reflex that saved Bauman's life.

**Over-familiarity risk:** LOW. The photograph is iconic, but Arredondo's name and his account of *why* he moved the way he did are much less widely known than the image itself — most readers will recognize "the man in the cowboy hat" faster than they recognize the mechanism, which is again an advantage: the chapter can supply the mechanism as new information rather than retelling a familiar story.

**Caveat:** Arredondo's trained pattern (rodeo clown → run toward danger) is, like Autrey's, an occupational reflex transferred to a moral emergency rather than a virtue specifically cultivated as a virtue. Same caveat applies as for Autrey. This is worth noting as a pattern across three of the five candidates: real documented "no time to think" heroism cases tend to trace to occupational/athletic training, not to deliberate virtue-cultivation programs — which is itself a finding the chapter may need to address honestly (see evidence.md, humility-as-keystone / honest-reply section).

## CANDIDATE 4 — Kim Potter, shooting of Daunte Wright, Brooklyn Center, Minnesota, April 11, 2021 — REQUIRED DARK-SIDE CASE

**Why it fits:** This is the case the brief explicitly requires: a documented instance of a trained response firing wrongly under pressure, illustrating that installed response patterns fire whether or not the situation warrants — the necessary complication to an otherwise triumphalist chapter.

**The scene:** During a traffic stop, Officer Kim Potter, a 26-year veteran of the Brooklyn Center Police Department, intended to draw and use her Taser on Daunte Wright, who was attempting to re-enter his car. She can be heard on body-camera audio shouting "Taser! Taser! Taser!" immediately before firing — but she had drawn and fired her handgun instead, killing Wright. Immediately afterward she is heard saying "I grabbed the wrong f***ing gun" and collapsing in evident shock at what she had done.

**Why this is the right kind of dark-side case, not just a tragedy:** The prosecution's own framing at trial makes the "firmware fires wrongly" point almost too literally: Potter had 26 years of use-of-force training and specifically 19 years of Taser training, including training explicitly covering "weapon confusion" — she had been trained, for years, to prevent exactly the error she then made. The prosecution called it "precisely the thing she had been warned about for years and she was trained to prevent it." She was convicted of first- and second-degree manslaughter in December 2021.

**The research mechanism, which the chapter can use to explain rather than merely report the error:** This is a documented phenomenon in the use-of-force literature, not a one-off. Force-science and human-factors research on "TASER/weapon confusion" describes it as a **capture error**: under stress, a less-practiced, less-automatic action (drawing a TASER, which officers use far less often) can be non-consciously replaced by a more deeply practiced, more automatic action (drawing the firearm), especially when the officer is simultaneously occupied by other demands — threat assessment, verbal commands, time pressure. One review found this was documented as the 18th such gun/Taser confusion incident among police in roughly the prior 20 years, and broader use-of-force research reports that a majority of officers involved in shootings describe responding "automatically," with "little or no conscious thought." This is the same cognitive machinery the chapter is celebrating in Autrey and Arredondo — trained response outrunning deliberation — misfiring because the more deeply grooved pattern (firearm) overrode the less-practiced one (Taser) under load. It is a precise, literal illustration of "installed responses fire whether or not the situation warrants," not a loose analogy.

**Handling note for the Drafter:** this case involves a killing and a real family's grief; it should be handled with the gravity the chapter's tone elsewhere reserves for real harm, not deployed as a rhetorical gotcha. It is also legally and factually distinct from Autrey/Arredondo/Sullenberger in an important way worth flagging to the Showrunner: Potter's error was a slip in a high-stakes, adversarial, weapon-bearing context, and the ethical stakes of "which virtue's firmware are we talking about" are different in kind (use-of-force judgment under an institutional mandate to use force) from a bystander's rescue reflex. The chapter should be precise about what this case does and doesn't prove: it proves installed responses can misfire under load; it does not by itself prove anything about virtue-specific training as opposed to procedural/motor training.

## CANDIDATE 5 — Elite baseball batters vs. a 95+ mph fastball — non-moral illustration

**Why it fits:** The brief asks for a musician/athlete case as the *non-moral* illustration of trained response outrunning deliberation — useful precisely because it strips away the ethical stakes and lets the chapter make the pure cognitive-mechanism point in isolation before returning to virtue.

**The numbers (won't need named individuals, but can be anchored to any specific pitch if the Drafter wants a scene):** A 95 mph fastball reaches home plate in roughly 400–450 milliseconds. Baseline human reaction time to a simple visual stimulus is roughly 200–250 milliseconds. A batter's swing itself takes on the order of 150–200 milliseconds once initiated. Netting this out, a hitter has a genuinely tiny window — commonly cited as on the order of 100 milliseconds or less — in which to both decide whether/where to swing and begin executing that decision. Sports-cognition research on this task (cited in NPR's 2016 reporting on brain imaging of batters) has found that batters who engage the brain's more deliberative, frontal regions more heavily on a given pitch tend to perform *worse* on it — i.e., conscious deliberation is not merely too slow here, it actively degrades performance relative to the trained, largely automatic pattern-recognition/motor response built by years of repetition.

**Value to the chapter:** This gives the argument a case with zero moral content and therefore zero risk of the reader arguing with the facts instead of the mechanism — the point ("years of repetition install a response that fires faster than, and often better than, deliberate thought can") is uncontroversial here in a way it can't be for Autrey or Potter, which makes it useful as a clean, short, second or third beat rather than as the opening scene itself (it has no narrative "case" — no named person, date, place — so it cannot itself satisfy the Case Hunter's core requirement of "real people, named, in places, at dates, doing things"; it should supplement, not replace, one of the human cases above).

## RED FLAGS SUMMARY

- **Sullenberger over-familiarity is real and the record complicates the "no deliberation" framing more than the popular myth admits.** Recommend demoting from opening case to a supporting reference, or using it explicitly to make the *different* point that training compresses (rather than eliminates) deliberation — which is actually a more defensible and more interesting claim, just not the brief's stated one. Flag for Showrunner: if the brief's own claim ("deliberation was impossible") is only strictly true of Autrey and Arredondo and not of Sullenberger, the Drafter/Showrunner may want to soften the argument's phrasing to "the deliberation that happens is too fast and too compressed to be the kind of explicit reasoning the deliberative model assumes" rather than "deliberation was impossible" — Sullenberger then becomes usable again as the calibrated version of the claim.
- **Three of the five candidates (Autrey, Arredondo, and implicitly the batting case) trace the trained response to occupational/athletic habituation, not deliberate virtue cultivation.** This is worth flagging to the Interlocutor section: if "installed response" cases in the wild are mostly skill-transfer stories rather than virtue-training stories, that is mild ammunition for the virtue ethicist's objection that the chapter's "firmware" model conflates skill with character — the chapter should address this rather than let a careful reader notice it first.
- **Kim Potter is a real death and a real family's loss.** Recommend against any framing that risks reading as glib about the human cost, even in service of the mechanism point.
