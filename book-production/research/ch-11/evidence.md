## 2. DORIS, LACK OF CHARACTER (2002) — THE SITUATIONIST CHALLENGE

| Claim | Source / wording | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Doris's central thesis: character-trait attribution as understood by (his target version of) virtue ethics and folk psychology — stable, "global" dispositions like honesty or compassion that are supposed to be robust and predictive across a wide range of relevantly different situations — is empirically unsupported. Behavior is much more strongly predicted by situational variables than by any such global trait. | Multiple convergent secondary summaries (PhilPapers record, Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews review, ResearchGate comments) plus general philosophical consensus on the book's thesis. | SOLID (well-documented consensus reading of a single well-known book) |
| Doris does not conclude there is *no* such thing as character; his own positive proposal is **"local traits"** — dispositions that are real but narrower and more context-bound than the "global" traits classical virtue ethics assumes (e.g., "honest-with-friends-about-money" rather than "honest"), plus the recommendation that ethical theory pay much more attention to situational design ("situationism" as a practical prescription, not just a critique). | Multiple secondary sources converge on "local traits" as Doris's term and positive view. | SOLID |
| The canonical empirical evidence base Doris draws on is not primarily Milgram/Zimbardo (those are more central to the adjacent literature on obedience and institutional evil, relevant to Ch.10, not Ch.11) but rather situational-manipulation studies of ordinary "small" prosocial behavior — most famously Darley & Batson's 1973 "Good Samaritan" study of Princeton seminary students (asked to walk to give a talk, sometimes on the parable of the Good Samaritan itself, and manipulated for how much of a hurry they were told they were in; those in the "high hurry" condition helped a slumped, groaning confederate only 10% of the time, vs. 63% in "low hurry" — and whether their talk topic was literally the Good Samaritan parable had no effect on helping) and Isen & Levin's "found a dime" helping studies (small situational mood manipulations producing large swings in helping behavior). | Darley & Batson (1973), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* — figures (63%/45%/10% by hurry condition) corroborated directly against the original paper (hosted, Greater Good Berkeley PDF) via search. Doris's specific reliance on these studies is the standard reading in the secondary situationism literature, though I did not independently re-verify Doris cites these exact two studies by page number this pass — Verifier should confirm chapter/page in *Lack of Character* itself if quoting Doris's discussion directly. | SOLID for the underlying studies; SINGLE-SOURCE (standard reading, not page-verified) for "these are the studies Doris leans on" |
| **What has happened to the underlying social psychology since the replication crisis — cuts both ways, must be reported honestly:** (1) Several of the most famous *adjacent* studies in this broader tradition (the Stanford Prison Experiment, and to a lesser degree Milgram's obedience paradigm) have been seriously challenged on methodological and even ethical-conduct grounds by subsequent investigative work (e.g., Ben Blum's reporting on Zimbardo's SPE; scholarship revisiting Milgram's own manipulation of participants and selective reporting) — this is evidence *against* over-trusting dramatic single-study demonstrations of situational power, which is double-edged for Doris because his own argument is partly built on trusting exactly this kind of single dramatic study. (2) At the same time, the core situational-manipulation findings Doris leans on most (small, low-cost helping-behavior studies like Darley & Batson and the "found a dime" literature) are of a different, generally more replicable character than SPE/Milgram — they are simple, cheap-to-run, effect-size-modest manipulations of ordinary behavior, which is closer to the profile of study that *has* tended to survive direct replication attempts in the post-2011 replication-crisis literature, though I did not locate a specific, named direct replication of Darley & Batson (1973) itself in this pass — this is a gap the Verifier should close before the chapter asserts robustness either way. (3) The broader "situationism vs. trait" debate itself has also been challenged on grounds independent of replication — critics (Sabini & Silver 2005, "Lack of Character? Situationism Critiqued," *Ethics* 115) have argued the classic situationist evidence base has been over-read, e.g., the true predictive power of *aggregated* situational information (not single-situation snapshots) can rival trait measures once methodology is done carefully. | Stanford Prison Experiment challenge: corroborated by multiple sources (Ben Blum exposé, "Advisory Board" replication-crisis coverage). Milgram methodological criticism: corroborated (PLOS Biology "Contesting the Nature of Conformity" re-analysis). Sabini & Silver (2005) citation: corroborated as a real, specifically-titled rebuttal in the situationism-in-ethics literature. Direct replication status of Darley & Batson specifically: NOT independently confirmed this pass — flagged as a gap. | MIXED — see per-item confidence above; overall CONTESTED, and honestly so |

## 4. ANNAS, INTELLIGENT VIRTUE (2011) — THE SKILL ANALOGY

| Claim | Wording / detail | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Annas's central move: the intellectual structure of virtue is analogous to the intellectual structure of a practical skill (her recurring examples: playing a musical instrument, a sport like tennis, a craft). Skills and virtues share two key features — "the need to learn" and "the drive to aspire" (i.e., skilled practitioners don't plateau; they keep trying to do better, which she takes to mirror how virtue involves ongoing aspiration to act well, not just adequately). | Multiple convergent secondary sources (Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews review of *Intelligent Virtue*; Journal of Value Inquiry special-issue introduction). | SOLID (standard, well-documented reading of a specific, named book) |
| The learner → expert structure: a novice follows rules/instructions consciously and effortfully; with practice, the skilled/expert performer's actions become fluent, often not consciously deliberated in the moment — but, crucially for Annas, the expert can *still give a reasoned account* of why they acted as they did, after the fact, if asked. The exercise of virtue is "intelligent" in the same sense that expert tennis play is intelligent: it is backed by good reasons and understanding even when it doesn't involve real-time conscious deliberation. | Corroborated across the same sources; the "intelligent in the way expertise in tennis is intelligent... practice makes us fluent in a way that can efface conscious deliberation, even though the practitioner could explain retrospectively" formulation is a secondary paraphrase (Journal of Value Inquiry introduction), not a verified direct quotation from Annas's text — Verifier should pull the primary text (a ResearchGate-hosted PDF of *Intelligent Virtue* surfaced in search) to confirm exact wording before the Drafter quotes Annas directly. | SINGLE-SOURCE (consistent secondary paraphrase; primary-text wording not independently confirmed this pass, though a source PDF was located and can be re-fetched) |
| **Disanalogy Annas herself acknowledges — important honest-report item:** unlike discrete skills (which can be developed somewhat independently of one another — you can be a skilled harpist without being a skilled builder), Annas holds that the virtues are, in her account, unified — you cannot fully possess one without the others, because all of them involve the same underlying practical intelligence (phronesis) applied to different domains. This is a real disanalogy in her own framework, not a criticism she's unaware of. | Corroborated by secondary sources on the skill-analogy literature. | SOLID |
| **External criticism of the analogy, for balance:** other philosophers (e.g., John Hacker-Wright, and contributors to the "Annas on Virtue and Skill" special issue) have argued the disanalogies run deeper than Annas allows — e.g., unlike a sport or craft, moral situations don't offer the frequent, low-stakes, clearly-scored practice repetitions and rapid feedback that build genuine expertise in tennis or carpentry; moral "practice" is comparatively rare, high-stakes, and feedback is often ambiguous or delayed (you may never learn whether the compassionate thing you did actually helped). This is a serious, literature-recognized limitation of the skill analogy that the chapter should not paper over if it leans on Annas. | Corroborated by search results referencing named critical responses (Hacker-Wright; the Journal of Value Inquiry special issue framed as responses to Annas). Specific page-level critique content not independently re-verified this pass. | SINGLE-SOURCE — the existence and general shape of the criticism is solid; exact citations need Verifier pass |
