**RED FLAG — the "commonly misdescribed" issue the brief asked me to check, restated precisely:** The popular framing ("the man who refused to launch nuclear missiles" / "the man who saved the world by not pushing the button") is not accurate and should not appear in the chapter without correction. Petrov had no button to push. What actually happened, and what should appear: he was the officer responsible for evaluating and relaying early-warning data, the documented expectation was to escalate a detected launch immediately, and he instead withheld escalation pending corroboration that never arrived — a decision made, by his own account, on instinct rather than on a data point that resolved the ambiguity. Whether that withholding constitutes "breaking a rule" in a clean sense, versus exercising discretion that arguably belonged to his post, is not fully settled by the sources I found in this pass. **I could not find a primary military document (an Oko operating procedure, a general-staff directive) specifying exactly how much evaluation time or discretion a duty officer had before mandatory escalation.** This is the single biggest evidentiary gap in Pair 1 and the Referee should be told about it directly: the chapter's claim that Petrov "broke a rule" rests on secondary characterizations of a procedure I was not able to verify against a primary text. Recommend either (a) a research follow-up specifically hunting for the Oko/Serpukhov-15 standing orders, or (b) the chapter hedges the framing itself — "he was expected to relay it immediately; instead he waited" — without the stronger claim "he broke protocol," which the primary-source gap doesn't fully support.

**Note on Leeson's self-interest:** unlike Wakefield, Leeson's motive is more straightforwardly self-protective (fear of losing his job, his reputation as a star trader) than a claimed higher purpose — this actually makes him a slightly less "sincere" wrong-breaker than the brief's ideal ("someone who broke a rule for what felt like the same reasons" as the right-breaker). ## THE ABSOLUTIST AT ITS STRONGEST

Per the brief: Kant is the obvious source but the murderer-at-the-door hypothetical is explicitly excluded. Two usable alternatives, neither is the banned case:

1. **Kant's general maxim-universalizability point** (not the lying-to-the-murderer application): *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), the Formula of Universal Law: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." Widely and consistently rendered this way across translations (minor variants: "by which" for "whereby"). CONFIDENCE: STRONG that this is Kant's language and formulation; this is one of the most secure quotations in the history of philosophy and needs minimal independent verification, though the Verifier should still confirm against a standard translation (Mary Gregor's Cambridge edition is the modern scholarly standard) before print, and specify which translation is being quoted.

2. **A contemporary, non-Kantian absolutist voice — G.E.M. Anscombe**, "Modern Moral Philosophy," *Philosophy* 33, no. 124 (1958). The line: "if someone really thinks, in advance, that it is open to question whether such an action as procuring the judicial execution of the innocent should be quite excluded from consideration — I do not want to argue with him; he shows a corrupt mind." CONFIDENCE: MODERATE-STRONG — this is a famous and frequently-cited line from Anscombe's essay, appearing consistently across academic secondary sources (Oxford Academic journal article, philarchive.org), but I was working from citations of it rather than the original 1958 *Philosophy* journal text directly; the Verifier should pull the original article (widely reprinted, including in Anscombe's *Collected Philosophical Papers*, vol. 3) to confirm exact wording and page number before the chapter quotes it. This is a genuinely different scenario from Kant's murderer case (judicial execution of an innocent person, not lying to a murderer at the door) and is not covered by the brief's exclusion — worth flagging to the Canon Keeper/Showrunner as a confirmation that using it doesn't violate the "must not" instruction, since it's adjacent territory.

**Recommendation for the interlocutor's strongest form:** use Anscombe over Kant for the in-chapter voice — it's fresher (Kant's general maxim-test is famous enough that citing it risks reading as reflexive), it makes the absolutist's point in its most uncompromising and most quotable form ("he shows a corrupt mind" is a genuinely hostile, well-put line, which fits the brief's instruction to take the interlocutor seriously), and it sidesteps any proximity to the banned case entirely. Kant's Formula of Universal Law can still appear as the underlying logic the position is built on, cited briefly, without doing the interlocutor's main rhetorical work.
