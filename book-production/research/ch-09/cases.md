## RANKED PAIRS

### PAIR 1 (recommended) — Stanislav Petrov / Nick Leeson
**The wait-or-escalate pair.**

- **Right-breaker: Stanislav Petrov**, Serpukhov-15 bunker, USSR, September 26, 1983. Duty officer for the Oko early-warning satellite system. The system reported a U.S. launch of up to five missiles. **Protocol required him to relay the warning immediately up the chain of command.** He did not. He waited for ground-radar corroboration that never came, on the reasoning that a real first strike would involve many more than five missiles, and on what he later described, in a 1999 *Washington Post* interview, as "a funny feeling in my gut — I didn't want to make a mistake." The satellites had mistaken sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds for missile plumes. No missiles existed.
- **Wrong-breaker: Nick Leeson**, Barings Bank, Singapore, 1992–1995. Derivatives trader who, after an initial small loss in 1992, hid it in an unauthorized "error account" (number 88888) rather than report it up the chain — the same move, structurally: don't escalate yet, I can fix this myself before anyone needs to know. He kept trading to make the loss back. It grew from roughly £2 million (1992) to £23 million (1993) to more than £200 million (late 1994) before the bank collapsed in 1995, 233 years after its founding. Leeson later described the concealment as "this catastrophic secret which was burning up inside me — yet... I simply couldn't open my mouth."
- **Why they feel the same from inside:** both are a trained specialist alone with an ambiguous signal, a standing rule that says *escalate now, don't wait,* and a private conviction that they understand the situation better than the rule assumes and can resolve it quietly without triggering the machinery above them. Both chose delay over disclosure.
- **What actually differed:** Petrov's signal was, in fact, noise — his wait cost nothing because there was nothing to escalate. Leeson's signal was real money, and every day of delay compounded it; his motive was also self-protective in a way Petrov's wasn't (reputation and job, not situational judgment about the underlying facts), which is the tell in hindsight, not in the moment. Documentation quality: excellent on both sides — Petrov via multiple corroborating interviews, declassified Soviet material, and consistent journalistic record (Washington Post, NPR, Britannica); Leeson via his own memoir (*Rogue Trader*), the Bank of England's Board of Banking Supervision inquiry report, and trial record.
- **Flag — the "commonly misdescribed" point the brief asked me to check:** Petrov is very widely described as "the man who refused to launch nuclear missiles." **This is wrong and should not appear in the chapter.** Petrov had no launch authority whatsoever — he was a warning-system duty officer, not a launch officer. What he actually did was decline to relay an unconfirmed alarm up the chain during the evaluation window, which is a narrower and in some tellings more defensible act than the popular version implies (evaluating signal quality before escalating was arguably within the scope of his post, not a clean violation of it — see "red flags" below). The chapter needs the accurate version: he deviated from the documented expectation of immediate escalation, not from an order not to launch.

### PAIR 2 — Werner Forssmann / Andrew Wakefield
**The maverick-doctor pair.**

- **Right-breaker: Werner Forssmann**, Auguste-Viktoria hospital, Berlin, 1929. A 25-year-old surgical resident, convinced cardiac catheterization was safe in a living human based on animal and cadaver work, was explicitly forbidden by his supervisor, Dr. Richard Schneider, from attempting it. Forssmann later wrote of the decision: "I decided to override Schneider's prohibition and go ahead with the experiment on my own heart secretly." He talked a surgical nurse into helping him get sterile instruments, anesthetized his own arm, threaded a catheter roughly 60 cm up his own vein toward his heart, then walked to the X-ray department to confirm placement on fluoroscopy, watching the whole procedure in a mirror. He was pilloried for it — colleagues called it a stunt — and effectively pushed out of cardiology into urology for years. In 1956 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it, the technique having become the foundation of modern interventional cardiology.
- **Wrong-breaker: Andrew Wakefield**, Royal Free Hospital, London, 1996–1998. Gastroenterologist who published a 1998 *Lancet* case series linking the MMR vaccine to autism, built in part on invasive procedures — lumbar punctures, colonoscopies, barium studies — performed on children that a UK General Medical Council panel found were not clinically indicated and were carried out in breach of the hospital's ethics approval. He did not disclose that roughly half the children were referred through a personal-injury lawsuit against vaccine manufacturers that was paying him, or that he held a patent for a rival measles vaccine. The GMC's 2010 ruling: he acted "dishonestly and irresponsibly" and with "callous disregard" for the children, and struck him from the medical register. He has never recanted; in a 1997 memo cited at the hearing he wrote that if the vaccine link were confirmed, the study's children "are the few unfortunate who have been sacrificed to protect the majority" — the language of a man who believed he was the one seeing what the institution wouldn't. MMR uptake in the UK fell sharply after 1998 and has never fully recovered in some communities; measles, once effectively eliminated in Britain, returned as endemic outbreaks.
- **Why they feel the same from inside:** both are a physician convinced the establishment is institutionally blind to something urgent, who goes around the normal approval channel — Forssmann around a direct supervisory prohibition, Wakefield around ethics-committee scope — on the private conviction that the ordinary process would smother a finding that matters more than the process does. Both were initially disbelieved and then, for a period, effectively exiled from their subfield for it.
- **What actually differed:** Forssmann's claim was checkable and, when checked by others independently over the following decades, true. Wakefield's was checkable and, when checked (by epidemiological studies at vastly larger scale, and by journalist Brian Deer's investigation into the underlying case records), false, and the falsity was compounded by an undisclosed financial interest in the answer coming out his way — self-interest, uncorrected. This is the cleanest pair for testing one of the five conditions ch-10 will name (self-interest scrubbed out or not) without naming the conditions themselves. Documentation quality: excellent on both — Forssmann via his own 1929 case report and subsequent Nobel materials; Wakefield via the full public GMC hearing transcript and ruling, and the retraction record.

### PAIR 3 — Hugh Thompson Jr. / Captain Will Rogers III (USS Vincennes)
**The fog-of-war threat-identification pair.**

- **Right-breaker: Hugh Thompson Jr.**, Sơn Mỹ (My Lai), South Vietnam, March 16, 1968. Army helicopter pilot flying reconnaissance over the massacre in progress. Seeing U.S. soldiers of Charlie Company killing unarmed civilians, he landed his aircraft between a group of villagers sheltering in a bunker and the advancing American troops, and — according to his own later testimony and that of crew chief Lawrence Colburn — ordered his door gunners to train their weapons on the American soldiers and open fire if they continued shooting civilians. He then radioed for evacuation of the villagers he'd sheltered, and reported the killings to his chain of command immediately on return, which is what got a (partial, delayed) halt order issued that day. He broke the rule that a soldier does not turn his weapon on his own side, on the judgment that what he was watching was a crime in progress, not a lawful operation.
- **Wrong-breaker: Captain Will Rogers III**, USS Vincennes, Strait of Hormuz, July 3, 1988. In the aftermath of a running skirmish with Iranian gunboats, the Vincennes' crew tracked an aircraft their Aegis combat system's operators — misreading a radar-track handoff error and under acute stress — reported to Rogers as descending and accelerating toward the ship on a hostile profile. It was in fact Iran Air Flight 655, a scheduled civilian Airbus, climbing on a published commercial corridor. Rogers, working from what his own crew told him rather than independently re-verifying the raw track, made the call to fire under the rules of engagement's standard for reasonable belief of hostile intent. Two missiles killed all 290 people aboard. He was never disciplined; the Navy's investigation attributed the failure to "scenario fulfillment" — the crew, primed by the recent skirmish, saw the threat picture they expected to see. He received the Legion of Merit in 1990 for "outstanding service" during his command tour; the citation did not mention Flight 655.
- **Why they feel the same from inside:** both are military personnel with seconds to decide, under a rule that says wait for better information / don't act on an ambiguous picture, who instead acted immediately on their read of the situation because they believed delay itself would be catastrophic — Thompson believed more villagers would die if he didn't intervene now; Rogers believed his ship would be hit if he didn't fire now. Both felt certain in the moment. Both were, from inside, applying "imminent, irreversible harm, no time to appeal upstream" — the language ch-10's own trigger gate will use.
- **What actually differed:** Thompson's read was of something he watched directly, at close range, unambiguous once you were looking at it (people being marched to a ditch and shot). Rogers's read was mediated entirely through instruments and a crew he had reason to trust but who were themselves wrong, on a contact he never independently confirmed. Documentation quality: strong on both. Thompson: Peers Commission inquiry, his own and Colburn's congressional and Army testimony, the 1998 Soldier's Medal citation (30 years late). Rogers: Navy JAG investigation (Fogarty Report), Newsweek/Time contemporaneous reporting, subsequent USNI Proceedings retrospectives citing crew unfamiliarity with the Aegis interface.
- **Caveat for the referee:** this is a weaker fit than Pairs 1 and 2 on the "broke a rule" test specifically. Rogers arguably never violated a rule at all — the rules of engagement gave him discretion to fire on reasonable belief of hostile intent, and by his own and the Navy's account he believed that in good faith. If the chapter's frame is "broke a rule," Rogers is better described as a case of correctly following a rule that was built to fail under exactly those conditions (bad data, high stress, short window) — which is a different, adjacent point, useful but not a clean structural match to Thompson. Flagging this rather than smoothing it over.

### PAIR 4 — Chiune Sugihara / Vasili Arkhipov & Jacob van Zanten (weakest fit — included for completeness)
**Two partial pairs, neither as clean as 1–3.**

- **Chiune Sugihara**, Japanese consul, Kaunas, Lithuania, summer 1940. Explicitly and repeatedly ordered by the Foreign Ministry in Tokyo not to issue transit visas to Jewish refugees without a confirmed onward destination. He issued them anyway — by hand, for eighteen-plus hours a day, reportedly writing them from the train window as he was recalled — ultimately covering an estimated 6,000 people. On his return to Japan he was quietly dismissed from the diplomatic service; the visas themselves survive as physical documentary evidence (several are held by families and museums today), which is about as clean a paper trail as this kind of case gets. This is a genuine "broke an explicit written order and was right" case — arguably the cleanest of anyone on this list on that specific test — but I could not find a documented wrong-breaker whose subjective situation mirrors it closely (an official defying an explicit written prohibition, sincerely, for what turned out to be catastrophic reasons, with comparable documentation). It may be worth keeping Sugihara in reserve and pairing him against whatever wrong-breaker the Showrunner's read of the chapter's needs turns up, rather than forcing a mediocre match now.
- **Vasili Arkhipov**, submarine B-59, Caribbean, October 27, 1962. Often told as "the man who refused to fire a nuclear torpedo," which needs the same correction as Petrov: Arkhipov did not launch anything and did not, strictly, break a rule — B-59's unusual command structure (because Arkhipov, as flotilla chief of staff, was aboard) required *unanimous* agreement among three officers to fire, and Arkhipov was the one dissenting vote a normal submarine wouldn't have had. He is better described as a case where a rule requiring consensus functioned exactly as designed under maximum pressure, not a case of rule-breaking at all. I'd flag this to the Showrunner as **not actually fitting the brief's "broke a rule" framing** on close inspection, despite being the single most famous "one person saved the world" story available. Recommend against using it as an opening case for that reason, though it may still be worth a passing sentence.
- **Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten**, KLM Flight 4805, Tenerife, March 27, 1977. KLM's chief 747 flight instructor and a senior, widely trusted authority (his face was on the airline's in-flight magazine) began his takeoff roll in dense fog without an explicit, unambiguous takeoff clearance from air traffic control — the first officer's read-back ended "we are now at takeoff," the tower's reply ("OK... stand by for takeoff, I will call you") was ambiguous and non-standard, and van Zanten, overriding a junior crew reluctant to challenge him, said "we're going" and went. Pan Am's 747 was still on the runway. 583 people died — still the deadliest accident in aviation history. This is a genuine, well-documented wrong-breaker (the full cockpit voice recorder transcript survives) but its natural right-breaker mirror isn't Sugihara or Arkhipov cleanly; it pairs best, thematically, with Petrov (trust-your-own-read-over-the-confirmed-signal), which would mean retiring it in favor of Leeson for Pair 1 or holding it as an alternate wrong-breaker for that slot.

## RECOMMENDATION

**Pair 1 (Petrov / Leeson) is the strongest candidate for the opening case.** It has the cleanest structural mirror (withhold escalation under uncertainty vs. escalate immediately), the best-documented self-justification on the wrong-breaker side, no contested moral valence (no one need adjudicate a war or a diagnosis to feel the force of it), and it sets up "how would I know?" almost for free — the reader is left with two men doing structurally the same thing, one becoming a footnote in nuclear history as a hero, the other becoming a byword for catastrophe, and no test either applied at the time that the other didn't.

**Pair 2 (Forssmann / Wakefield) is the strongest second case** if the chapter wants two examples rather than one, or as a fallback if the Showrunner or Referee finds Petrov's "commonly misdescribed" problem too much of a landmine for an opening case (it requires the chapter to actively correct the reader's likely prior, which costs word count and risks feeling like throat-clearing). Forssmann/Wakefield has no such correction tax — both stories are accurately told in their popular form — and it does more work for POS-13's self-interest condition.

Pairs 3 and 4 are included per the brief's instruction to produce four candidates, but I would not lead with either. Pair 3's wrong-breaker (Rogers) has a genuine "did he even break a rule" problem. Pair 4 is two mismatched halves, not a pair, and Arkhipov specifically should probably be retired from consideration as a "broke a rule" case entirely — see red flags.
