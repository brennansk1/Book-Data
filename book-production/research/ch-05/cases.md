# Ch. 5 ("The Dentist's Drill") — Candidate Cases

Per `CHAPTER_BRIEFS.md`, the brief specifies the opening case must be "something physical, specific
and non-abstract" and offers two locked-in candidates: a named person's account of an unanaesthetised
procedure, OR a pain-asymbolia case history. It does not rank them — Gate 1 is where the Showrunner
picks. This packet expands to five candidates, per `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §2.4, because the chapter's
whole argument runs on cases where **sensation and badness come apart**, and the brief's two locked
candidates don't exhaust the available material that does that job. Full sourcing and confidence
ratings are in `evidence.md`; this file is the narrative build-out and ranking.

**Standing sourcing note.** This session had no PDFs in `Files/` for this chapter and no physical
copies of Grahek, Sinhababu, or Mackie's books. Sourcing runs: (1) primary journal articles read
directly as PDFs — Sharon Street's 2006 paper in full, and Colin Klein's "What Pain Asymbolia Really
Shows," which itself quotes primary case reports (Schilder & Stengel 1928/1931, Berthier et al. 1988,

cross-checked against Wikipedia's reproduction of the letter's text; (3) secondary summaries (NDPR

## RANKED RECOMMENDATION

1. **(a) Pain asymbolia — RECOMMENDED OPENING CASE, specifically the Schilder–Stengel 1928 patient.**
   The only candidate that does the chapter's actual argumentative work *directly* rather than by
   analogy: a documented patient who has the sensation and is missing exactly the thing the chapter
   claims is constitutive of it. Vivid, clinical, non-gruesome (no blood, no screaming — which is
   itself useful contrast to (b)), and short enough to open cold in under 300 words. The complication
   (Klein's rebuttal, below) is a genuine asset, not a liability: it hands the chapter its own
   interlocutor material for free.
2. **(b) Fanny Burney's 1811 mastectomy letter — STRONGEST BACKUP OPENING CASE, or the chapter's
   second case.** The single most vivid physical scene of any candidate here, exceptionally well
   documented (the British Library holds and has digitised the original), and exactly the kind of
   "audio note" anchor the brief demands — impossible to lose a listener's attention during "the
   dreadful steel was plunged into the breast." Its weakness is argumentative rather than factual: it
   shows suffering is severe, not that badness is constitutive of sensation rather than added to it.
   Strongest used as the chapter's visceral cold open, with asymbolia arriving second as the
   philosophical pivot ("now here is a patient who has exactly this sensation and none of that").
3. **(c) Congenital insensitivity to pain (CIP) — Ashlyn Blocker.** Real, well-known, and useful as
   a contrast case (no sensation at all, versus asymbolia's sensation-without-badness) — but it does
   the wrong dissociation for this chapter's argument. CIP patients are missing the sensation itself,
   which doesn't isolate badness from sensation; it just removes both together. Best used, if at all,
   as a one-sentence foil to sharpen what asymbolia uniquely shows ("this is not the girl who can't
   feel her hand is on the stove — this is someone who can").
4. **(d) Morphine's dissociative effect.** Thematically perfect ("I still feel it, I just don't care")
   and the most audio-friendly of all five candidates — every reader already half-knows this from
   general cultural knowledge, so it lands fast — but this session could not source an exact,
   attributable clinical quotation to a specific named patient or paper (see the flag in `evidence.md`
   §5). Usable as a supporting beat referencing the general, well-established phenomenon, not as a
   cited case with a name and a date until the Verifier locates the primary source (likely Beecher).
5. **(e) Anesthesia awareness — Carol Weihrer.** Solid, named, dated, on the record — but it's the
   wrong dissociation again: Weihrer's case is remarkable because she had *no physical pain* during
   her surgery but full conscious memory of the experience (a different axis: consciousness without
   nociception, rather than nociception without badness). Strong connective material for Ch. 7's
   "what I don't know about consciousness" territory; not the right shape for this chapter's core
   argument. Include only as a one-line acknowledgment that dissociation cases exist in multiple
   directions, if the Drafter wants that texture.

**Overall recommendation for Gate 1:** either open cold on Burney (visceral, unimpeachable, gets the
audio-note problem solved in the first ninety seconds) and pivot at the interlocutor turn to
asymbolia as the case that actually does the philosophical work — or open directly on asymbolia and
use Burney later/shorter as a gut-check anchor. Both orderings satisfy Gate 1. What the chapter
should **not** do is open on CIP or morphine as the primary case; neither isolates the dissociation
the argument needs.

## (a) Pain asymbolia — the Schilder–Stengel patient (1928/1931) and the Berthier series (1988)

**What it is, and why it's the chapter's engine:** pain asymbolia is a rare neurological condition,
caused by damage to the posterior insula (and, per some accounts, area 7b and connecting limbic
pathways), in which patients retain full sensory-discriminative pain perception — they can locate,
describe, and identify a stimulus as painful — while losing essentially all of the affective/
motivational component: the distress, the urge to withdraw, the badness. Grahek's own framing (used
by both Grahek and his critics; see `evidence.md` §2): pain asymbolia may be **"the only clear-cut
case of pain without painfulness."** This is, almost too neatly, the exact dissociation the chapter's
argument needs a real case to anchor: sensation present, badness absent.

**The first documented case, Schilder and Stengel (1928, published 1931) — SOLID, read via direct
primary-source translation quoted in Colin Klein's peer-reviewed paper (`evidence.md` §3):** a female
patient with sensory aphasia. The original report, translated from German:

> "The patient displays a striking behaviour in the presence of pain. She reacts either not at all or
> insufficiently to being pricked, struck with hard objects, and pinched. She never pulls her arm back
> energetically or with strength. She never turns the torso away or withdraws with the body as a
> whole. She never attempts to avoid the investigator." (Schilder & Stengel 1928, p. 147)

And, the single most usable sentence in this entire packet for an audio-first chapter — vivid,
strange, and unmistakably a documented clinical observation rather than a philosopher's thought
experiment:

> "Pricked on the right palm, the patient smiles joyfully, winces a little, and then says, 'Oh, pain,
> that hurts.' She laughs, and reaches the hand further toward the investigator and turns it to expose
> all sides ... The patient's expression is one of complacency. The same reaction is displayed when
> she is pricked in the face and stomach." (Schilder & Stengel 1928, p. 147–48)

Schilder and Stengel are explicit that this is not inattention: she is actively engaged, offering more
of her body for testing. Of the ten patients in their broader case series, five recovered from
asymbolia in step with recovery from the general sensory aphasia; two are noted to have said they
could remember the pain being inflicted but did not feel it.

**A second, sharper primary quote, from Hemphill and Stengel's later 1940 case — SOLID via Klein's
direct translation/citation:** this patient's asymbolia generalized dangerously beyond controlled
testing —

> "The patient was observed proceeding one morning along the main road of the hospital. He made no
> effort to get out of the way of a lorry behind him in spite of the loud warning of the horn. That he
> heard the horn and recognised its character is certain, for he admitted as much with considerable
> heat when he was forbidden, for his own safety, to walk alone on the main road." (Hemphill & Stengel
> 1940, p. 256)

This is a genuinely dramatic, real, documented near-miss — a man who heard a truck horn, understood
exactly what it meant, and did not move, then got irritated at being told he couldn't be trusted to
walk alone. It is vivid, physical, sourced to a named clinical report, and does real argumentative
work (sensation and comprehension both intact; only the "get out of the way" pull is gone).

**The larger, better-characterized series — Berthier, Starkstein & Leiguarda 1988, *Annals of
Neurology* 24(1): 41–49 — REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED (abstract and findings corroborated across
multiple sources; full text not read directly this session):** six patients with unilateral
hemispheric damage (five ischemic strokes, one traumatic hematoma) showed absent or grossly
inadequate withdrawal and emotional response to painful stimuli applied across the entire body, and
to threatening gestures; five of the six also failed to respond to verbal menaces. The paper's own
framing — **"a sensory-limbic disconnection syndrome"** — is the standard citation for the condition's
mechanism: the pathway that detects tissue damage stays intact; its connection to affective evaluation
is severed. One case, cited directly in Klein's paper (Berthier et al. 1988, p. 43): an asymbolic
patient suffered a severe burn at home because he made no attempt to escape the danger.

**Fit to the argument:** as close to perfect as this packet has. This is a real, clinically documented
population in which the sensation is present and identified as pain, and the one thing the chapter
claims is constitutive of badness — the distress, the pull to stop it — is specifically and
selectively missing. It is the empirical case that makes "badness is part of what the sensation is,
not a judgment added to it" concrete rather than assertion.

**The required complication — and it is a gift, not just a risk.** Grahek (2007, *Feeling Pain and
Being in Pain*) reads asymbolia as showing pain is a composite of a sensory-discriminative component
and an emotional-cognitive component that can come apart — his own words: pain "is actually a complex
experience comprising sensory-discriminative, emotional-cognitive and behavioural components. These
components are normally linked together, but they can become disconnected and therefore, much to our
astonishment, they can exist separately" (Grahek 2007, p. 2, quoted in Klein). **Colin Klein's 2015
paper, "What Pain Asymbolia Really Shows," directly disputes this reading** (`evidence.md` §3–4). His
argument: asymbolics aren't indifferent to pain alone — they're indifferent to *any* threat to bodily
integrity, verbal or visual or auditory (loud noises, threatening gestures, the lorry). Klein's
alternative — the "Lost Capacity" model — is that asymbolics haven't lost a component of the pain
sensation; they've lost a general capacity to care about their own bodily integrity, of which pain
insensitivity is one symptom among several. If Klein is right, asymbolia shows something about the
relationship between a subject and her body's signals, not directly about what pain *is*. This is
philosophically live and current: two more papers in *Philosophy of Science* pick the fight back up —
Griffith & Kind, "Pain Asymbolia is Not Pain" (2023/2024), and Duval & Klein, "Pain Asymbolia Is
Probably Still Pain" (2025). **This is not a settled literature; it is an active argument, ongoing as
of this year.** For a book whose own metaethical position is rated MODERATE per `POSITIONS.md`, this
is exactly the honest framing: real, documented, still contested at the interpretive level.

**Factual risks:**
- Grahek's own book (2007, MIT Press) was **not read directly this session** — every Grahek quotation
  above is second-hand, via Klein's paper or the NDPR review. Any direct quotation of Grahek in the
  manuscript needs the Verifier to check the physical book.
- The patients in these case reports are, by the nature of 1928–1988 clinical neurology, unnamed —
  described by initials or case number only, per standard practice of the era. This is not a "named
  person" case in the sense Wellington/Winchilsea was for Ch. 4; it is a documented clinical case
  history. The brief itself anticipates and licenses this ("the case history of a patient with pain
  asymbolia" is one of its own two locked candidates), so this is not a deviation, but the Drafter
  should not invent a name or biographical color the record doesn't support.
- Schilder and Stengel's 1928 paper was in German; the quotations above are Klein's own translation,
  explicitly credited in his paper as such. A different translation could render slightly different
  wording — treat as accurate-in-substance, not word-for-word-official, until the Verifier locates any
  existing standard English translation.

## (b) Fanny Burney's 1811 mastectomy — the letter to her sister Esther

**The scene:** Frances "Fanny" Burney (1752–1840), the English novelist and diarist, then living in
Paris, underwent a mastectomy without anaesthesia on **30 September 1811**, after being diagnosed
with (probable) breast cancer. She wrote up the experience in a letter to her sister, Esther Burney,
dated **22 March 1812** — nearly six months after the operation, and, by her own account, composed
without ever going back to reread it.

**Named people present, REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED across Wikipedia and multiple independent secondary
accounts:** the lead surgeon was **M. (Antoine) Dubois**, who at the time was the Empress's own
accoucheur (obstetrician) — a mark of how senior a surgeon was assembled for her case. Also present:
**Dominique Jean Larrey**, Napoleon's own chief military surgeon, one of the most famous surgeons in
Europe at the time; **Dr. Moreau**, **Dr. Aumont**, **Dr. Ribe**, and two surgical pupils (one of
Larrey's, one of Dubois's) — seven medical men in the room in total.

**The provenance — this is well-documented, not folklore.** The **British Library holds the original
manuscript** and has digitised it in full (a twelve-page autograph letter) as part of its Discovering
Literature 18th-century collection, describing it as "remarkable as a patient's record of an operation
in the era before anaesthesia, and when surgery was still in its infancy." This is about as solid as
provenance gets for a document of this age and kind — a named institutional holder, a described
physical object, a stated acquisition/collection context. **No live search this session surfaced any
scholarly dispute over the letter's authenticity** — the debate in the secondary literature (per the
Warburg Institute blog and a PubMed-indexed academic article, "Frances Burney's Mastectomy Narrative
and Discourses of Breast Cancer in the Long Eighteenth Century") is about how to read and situate the
letter, not whether Burney wrote it.

**The exact, quotable passage — SOLID, cross-checked across two independent extractions (Wikipedia's
own article and a separate secondary source) converging on identical wording:**

> "when the dreadful steel was plunged into the breast — cutting through veins — arteries — flesh —
> nerves — I needed no injunctions not to restrain my cries. I began a scream that lasted
> unintermittingly during the whole time of the incision"

And a second, less commonly quoted passage — the sensation of air entering the wound, described in a
strikingly specific, almost synesthetic image:

> "felt like a mass of minute but sharp & forked poniards, that were tearing the edges of the wound"

**Fit to the argument:** the best available scene for raw visceral force and the audio note's demand
("every abstract move needs a physical anchor within three sentences") — but its argumentative fit to
this specific chapter's thesis is real, not perfect. Burney's letter is powerful evidence that
suffering can be extreme and that pain-badness doesn't require modern conceptual apparatus to be
self-evident to the person undergoing it. It does **not**, on its own, isolate sensation from badness
the way asymbolia does — a reader could in principle accept everything in Burney's letter and still
hold an attitudinal theory (the badness is Burney's horror *at* the sensation, not part of the
sensation itself). This is exactly why the packet ranks it second to asymbolia rather than first: it's
the better cold open, asymbolia is the better argument.

**Factual risks:**
- The letter was written six months after the event, from memory, which the historical record itself
  (and Burney's own framing, per multiple secondary sources) already flags — she describes writing it
  in a kind of trance and never rereading it. This is worth one clause in the chapter, not a hedge on
  every sentence: the vividness of the account is well-attested and the delay doesn't appear to be
  contested by scholars, but it should not be presented as a same-day record.
- The exact quoted passages above were extracted via secondary reproduction (Wikipedia's own
  transcription, cross-checked against one other source), not read directly from the British Library's
  digitised manuscript image this session. **Standard flag: before this appears as a direct quotation
  in the manuscript, the Verifier should check it against the British Library's own digitised text**,
  which is publicly available and should be a straightforward check.
- Some secondary sources (Stylist, Jezebel — not independently confirmed this session beyond their
  search-result summaries) describe additional details (e.g., Burney watching the operation via the
  reflection in the surgeon's polished blade) that could not be verified to primary-source standard
  this session and should not be used without the Verifier confirming them in the letter itself.

## (c) Congenital insensitivity to pain (CIP) — Ashlyn Blocker

**What it is:** a small number of documented, named, medically confirmed cases of people born with a
genetic inability to feel physical pain. Ashlyn Blocker, born 1999 in Patterson, Georgia, was profiled
in *The New York Times Magazine* (per multiple secondary sources, January 2012, journalist Justin
Heckert — **this article was not fetched directly this session; NYT blocks the fetch tool used**, so
this is REPORTED, not SOLID, and needs direct Verifier access). Genetic basis: mutations in the
**SCN9A** gene, which the wider search literature (REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED, multiple independent
sources) connects to a 2006 discovery by Cambridge geneticist **Geoffrey Woods**; **Dr. Roland Staud**
(University of Florida) is reported to have studied Blocker's DNA specifically and found two relevant
mutations. One source states there are only 45–50 reported cases of her specific condition worldwide —
REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE, needs a second citation before use as a stated figure.

**Fit to the argument:** real and vivid, but the wrong shape for this chapter's core dissociation. CIP
removes the sensation entirely — there is no pain-percept to have a badness or lack of badness, which
means CIP can't do the work asymbolia does (showing sensation present, badness absent). Its best use
in this chapter, if used at all, is a single contrastive sentence that sharpens what makes asymbolia
philosophically special: unlike Blocker, an asymbolic patient has the sensation and identifies it
correctly as pain — the only thing missing is the part that's supposed to make it matter.

**Factual risks:** the NYT Magazine profile itself was not read this session (fetch blocked). Every
detail above is at best REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED via secondary summary. **Before any specific
incident, quote, or biographical detail about Ashlyn Blocker is used, the Verifier must obtain and
read the original NYT Magazine piece directly** — this is a live, named, minor-at-time-of-profile
private individual, which raises a real privacy consideration on top of the sourcing gap; see the flag
in `evidence.md` §8.

## (d) Morphine's dissociative effect — "I still feel it, I just don't care"

**What it is:** a widely described, clinically real phenomenon — patients given morphine (and to a
lesser extent other opioids) frequently report that the pain sensation is unchanged in intensity or
location, but that it no longer distresses them. This is often traced in the secondary literature to
**Henry K. Beecher's** wartime and post-war clinical pain research (Beecher was a Harvard
anesthesiologist known for, among other things, his WWII battlefield observations that badly wounded
soldiers often required far less morphine than expected, apparently because combat context altered
the meaning, and therefore the distress, of the same tissue damage). Colin Klein's paper (`evidence.md`
§3) states in passing, as an aside rather than a sourced case: "acutely injured patients given a dose
of morphine will often say that they feel pain, but no longer care about it. They closely resemble
asymbolics in this regard."

**Fit to the argument:** thematically the cleanest one-line illustration of the whole chapter's thesis
— it's a a dissociation nearly everyone has some folk familiarity with, which makes it an efficient,
audio-friendly beat. It would make an excellent connective sentence bridging the asymbolia case to a
broader, more relatable register ("this isn't only a rare neurological syndrome — it's what morphine
does to millions of people every day, on a smaller scale").

**Factual risks — this is the packet's weakest sourcing, and should be flagged plainly rather than
patched over.** This session could **not** locate and confirm a specific, citable primary source (a
named paper, a named patient, an exact quoted clinical phrase) for the "I still have the pain but it
doesn't bother me" formulation specifically. The Beecher connection is a reasonable, commonly-repeated
attribution but was not confirmed against one of Beecher's own papers this session (candidates:
Beecher, "Relationship of Significance of Wound to Pain Experienced," *JAMA*, 1956; or his 1959 book
*Measurement of Subjective Responses*) . **Per `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §7.1 ("never invent a citation"),
this material should not be used as a sourced, quotable case until the Verifier locates and confirms
the primary text.** It is safe to use as a general, unattributed statement of a well-established
clinical phenomenon ("patients on morphine commonly report exactly this"), but not as a cited case
with a name, date, and exact quotation the way asymbolia and Burney can be.

## (e) Anesthesia awareness — Carol Weihrer

**What it is:** documented cases of patients regaining consciousness during surgery under general
anaesthesia — sometimes with pain, sometimes (as in this case) without physical pain but with full,
traumatic conscious memory of the procedure.

**Named person, REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED via Wikipedia, cross-referenced against its own citations
to CNN (2005), BBC News (2004), and CBS News (2003) coverage:** **Carol Weihrer** (21 August 1951 – 27
April 2024), an American activist. After fourteen unsuccessful surgeries for recurrent corneal erosion
syndrome, she underwent eye enucleation (surgical eye removal) in 1998. During that operation she
regained consciousness — she experienced no physical pain, but retained complete, vivid memory of the
procedure afterward, and was later diagnosed with PTSD as a result. She received an out-of-court
settlement and became a prominent activist and media presence on the issue (interviewed by CNN, BBC,
and CBS across the following years) until her death in 2024.

**Fit to the argument:** solid and verifiable, but it runs the *wrong* dissociation for this chapter —
Weihrer's case separates consciousness from nociception (she was aware, but not in physical pain),
which is closer to Ch. 7's "what I don't know about consciousness" territory than to this chapter's
sensation/badness split. Useful only as a brief acknowledgment, if the Drafter wants one, that
dissociation between components of experience runs in more than one direction — not as this chapter's
main case.

**Factual risks:** the Wikipedia summary is REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED against named news outlets but
was not independently verified against the original CNN/BBC/CBS segments this session, and contains
no direct quotation from Weihrer herself. Two other frequently cited named anesthesia-awareness cases
— Sherman Sizemore (West Virginia, 2006) and Donna Penner (Canada, subject of a widely aired
Radiolab/NPR episode) — are well known to the researcher from general background knowledge but were
**not independently verified via any live source this session** (a Wikipedia page for Penner does not
appear to exist, and Sizemore was not searched directly). **Do not use either name in the manuscript
without the Verifier confirming them against a primary or clearly reliable secondary source first** —
this is exactly the kind of unverified-recall risk `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §7.1 exists to prevent.
