## 2. Mackie's queerness argument — Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977)

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED for the quotation's wording and location; the physical book
was not read directly this session.**

Full citation: J.L. Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1977).

**The exact passage, as reproduced across multiple independent secondary sources this session,
converging on identical wording (attributed to p. 38 of the Penguin edition):**

> "If there were objective values, then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very
> strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe. Correspondingly, if we were
> aware of them, it would have to be by some special faculty of moral perception or intuition, utterly
> different from our ordinary ways of knowing everything else."

This is Mackie's own summary statement of the two strands of the "argument from queerness": a
**metaphysical** strand (objective values, if they existed, would be radically unlike anything else in
the ontology of a naturalistic universe) and an **epistemological** strand (knowing them would require
a special faculty unlike ordinary perception or inference). The two strands are explicitly linked in
Mackie's own text — one secondary source paraphrases his connective claim as: we are forced to posit
"weird epistemological equipment" only because the properties in question have already been established
as weird. Mackie's queerness argument is standardly read as targeting **non-natural** moral properties
specifically (irreducibly normative facts of the Moorean sort) rather than naturalistically-respectable
properties as such.

**How CANON wants this handled (§2.4):** "Phenomenal consciousness already violates Mackie's
ontological expectations independently; the argument was a priori and the a priori has a poor record
here." This is a real, standard, defensible move in the literature (consciousness itself is already an
apparent anomaly in a purely physical ontology, so pointing to phenomenal value as *additionally*
queer proves less than Mackie needs) — but it is worth the Drafter knowing explicitly that this is
essentially a **companions-in-guilt** strategy (the technical term in the literature for "you already
have this problem elsewhere"), which is not, by itself, a positive argument that phenomenal badness
exists — it only lowers the cost of accepting it. That is consistent with a MODERATE-confidence
framing and should be presented as such, not as a refutation of Mackie.

**Factual risk:** the p. 38 citation and exact wording are consistent and repeated across sources but
were not checked against the physical Penguin 1977 edition this session. **Before this appears as a
direct quotation with a page number, the Verifier should confirm both against the book.**

## 3. Pain asymbolia — the primary clinical literature, read directly this session

**Confidence: SOLID for the case-report quotations (verified via direct reading of Colin Klein's
peer-reviewed paper, which itself reproduces primary clinical text in translation); REPORTED-AND-
CROSS-CHECKED for Grahek's own book and the Berthier et al. 1988 paper's abstract-level findings,
neither read directly.**

Full case-report citations, and the exact wording, are given in full in `cases.md` §(a) and are not
repeated at full length here — see that file for: Schilder & Stengel (1928, published 1931, p.
147–148, including "Oh, pain, that hurts" and the withdrawal-refusal description); Hemphill & Stengel
(1940, p. 256, the lorry/horn case); and Berthier, Starkstein & Leiguarda (1988, *Annals of Neurology*
24(1): 41–49, including the p. 43 severe-burn-at-home case).

**Grahek's own theoretical claim, REPORTED via Klein's direct quotation of Grahek 2007, p. 2 (Grahek's
book itself not read directly this session):**

> "...although pain appears to be a simple, homogenous experience, is actually a complex experience
> comprising sensory-discriminative, emotional-cognitive and behavioural components. These components
> are normally linked together, but they can become disconnected and therefore, much to our
> astonishment, they can exist separately."

Grahek's own summary elsewhere (per the NDPR review of his book, REPORTED via direct fetch of the
review this session): pain asymbolia is presented as **"the only clear-cut case of pain without
painfulness"** — where "painfulness" is Grahek's term for the affective/unpleasant component
specifically. Grahek argues this dissociation refutes both purely subjectivist theories of pain
(because sensory-discriminative content survives intact) and purely objectivist/representationalist
theories (because the representational content, on his reading, cannot be understood independently of
the emotional-cognitive component). Grahek's book is titled *Feeling Pain and Being in Pain* (2nd ed.,
MIT Press, 2007) and treats asymbolia as the empirical core of a broader philosophical argument about
the nature of pain.

**The required, load-bearing complication — Colin Klein's rebuttal, SOLID (full paper read directly).**
Klein disputes that asymbolia shows what Grahek says it shows. His argument, briefly: if what asymbolics
had lost were specifically the affective component *of pain*, they should otherwise behave like the
congenitally pain-insensitive — unmoved by pain sensations, but still motivated to protect their
bodies once they learn (by other means) that a situation is dangerous. The clinical record does not
support this: asymbolics are reported as indifferent not only to pain but to **threatening gestures**,
**loud noises**, **verbal menaces**, and other bodily threats across sensory modalities (Berthier et
al. 1988 report five of six patients failed to respond to verbal menaces specifically). Klein's
alternative, the "Lost Capacity" model: asymbolics have lost a general capacity to care about their own
bodily integrity — of which indifference to pain's badness is one symptom, not the isolated, targeted
lesion Grahek's "Degraded Input" model implies. Klein explicitly frames this as an open, unresolved
question, not a settled refutation, and the debate has continued past his paper: **Griffith & Kind,
"Pain Asymbolia is Not Pain"** (*Philosophy of Science*, 2023/2024) and **Duval & Klein, "Pain
Asymbolia Is Probably Still Pain"** (*Philosophy of Science*, 2025) are both live, recent entries in
the same argument — this is not settled literature as of this writing.

**What this means for the chapter, stated plainly, per this role's mandate to flag overclaiming:** the
brief's own instruction to use Mackie and "the illusionist" as interlocutors is well served by
asymbolia; the case is real, vivid, and clinically documented. But asymbolia's *interpretation* is
itself a live philosophical dispute, and the honest version of this chapter should probably let that
show — not by litigating Klein v. Grahek in text (there isn't room), but by not overclaiming that
asymbolia "proves" badness is constitutive of the sensation. It is best presented as the single
strongest available piece of evidence consistent with that view, not as a demonstration of it. This
matches POS-01's MODERATE rating exactly and should not be pushed past it.

**Factual risk:** Grahek's book itself needs direct Verifier access before any passage is quoted as
Grahek's own words rather than as reported via Klein or the NDPR review.

## 4. Sharon Street's Darwinian Dilemma — the full 2006 paper, read directly, including her own
dedicated argument about the badness of pain specifically

**Confidence: SOLID. The complete paper was read directly this session as a primary-source PDF.**

Full citation: Sharon Street, "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value," *Philosophical
Studies* 127, no. 1 (2006): 109–166.

**The core dilemma, in Street's own structure (§§1–2, read directly):** realist theories of value hold
that there are evaluative facts or truths that hold independently of all our evaluative attitudes (what
Street, following Shafer-Landau, calls **stance-independence**). Evolutionary forces have massively
shaped the content of human evaluative attitudes. The realist must explain the relation between these
two things. Option one: **no relation** — but then our evaluative judgements are, for all we know,
wildly off-track, which is an unpalatable skeptical result. Option two: **a tracking relation** — we
were selected because natural selection favored ancestors who could grasp independent evaluative
truths — but this, Street argues, is scientifically unacceptable as an explanation (it is exactly as
implausible as claiming we evolved to track truths in astrophysics). Antirealism, she argues, escapes
the dilemma because it can explain the very same evolutionary influence on our values without needing
either horn: the causes are prior to the evaluative facts, not distorting influences on our access to
independent ones.

**The chapter-critical finding: Street has her own dedicated section specifically targeting the
badness of pain, not just evaluative attitudes in general — §9, "Third Objection: The Badness of Pain
as an Alleged Independent Truth About Value" (read directly, pp. 144–152).** This is exactly the
objection a phenomenal-realist response to Street would want to make, and Street has already written
the rebuttal. Her argument, reconstructed from the primary text:

- She grants that pain is "one of the strongest temptations toward realism about value" and that
  realists standardly appeal to it.
- She constructs what she calls the **"Pain Dilemma"**, structurally parallel to the main Darwinian
  Dilemma. She first defines pain (for the sake of argument) as "a sensation such that the creature
  having the sensation unreflectively takes that sensation to count in favor of doing whatever would
  avoid, lessen, or stop it" — i.e., she builds an unreflective **evaluative reaction** into the very
  definition of what pain is, functionally an attitudinal move.
- **Horn 1:** if the realist rejects this attitude-involving definition (i.e., insists pain could in
  principle be a sensation a creature is unreflectively inclined to seek out rather than avoid — her
  example: imagine a "miracle drug" that made a patient positively enjoy an ongoing pain sensation),
  then realism about that sensation's badness becomes, in Street's words, simply "not very plausible" —
  because the case for its badness collapses once the attitude is stipulated away.
- **Horn 2:** if the realist instead builds the negative evaluative reaction into the definition of pain
  (so that pain just is, by definition, a sensation we're constitutionally averse to), then — Street
  argues — realism about its badness is forced to admit that the badness "depends... on our being
  unreflectively inclined to take it to be bad," which she says is "just to admit that its badness
  depends in an important sense on our evaluative attitudes." Her direct conclusion, quoted exactly:
  **"Pain may well be bad, in other words, but if it is so, its badness hinges crucially on our
  unreflective evaluative attitudes toward the sensation which pain is. The realist is thus forced to
  recognize the role of our evaluative attitudes in determining the disvalue of pain. Though initially
  plausible, it is a mistake to say that pain is bad independently of our evaluative attitudes."**
  (Street 2006, p. 151.)
- She closes the section: **"I conclude that appeals to pain are not a promising avenue for the realist
  who wishes to escape the Darwinian Dilemma."** (p. 152.)

**Why this matters for the chapter and for the Canon Keeper, not just the Drafter.** CANON §2.4's
planned response to Street is: "Targets evolved evaluative attitudes. Acquaintance with a phenomenal
property is not a debunkable tracking relation." That response works cleanly against the *general*
Darwinian Dilemma (§§1–8 of Street's paper), which is explicitly about evaluative *judgements* and
*attitudes*. It is less clear that it fully answers §9 specifically, because §9 is Street's own attempt
to close off exactly the "but pain's badness isn't an attitude, it's a phenomenal fact I have direct
acquaintance with" move — she doesn't merely reassert the general Darwinian argument against pain
realism, she gives pain its own dedicated dilemma built around how "pain" itself gets defined. A
phenomenal realist wants to say pain's badness is *given in acquaintance*, prior to and independent of
any "unreflective evaluative reaction" — Street's §9 is constructed, deliberately, to make that
position hard to state in a way that isn't secretly attitude-involving. **This is not a claim that
Street wins the exchange — a phenomenal realist has real resources here** (the most obvious being to
deny Street's framing that "badness" just *means* "counts as a reason to avoid," and insist instead
that felt-badness is a genuine qualitative character no attitude-based paraphrase captures — this is
close to what Sinhababu's phenomenal-introspection move and Kahane's felt-quality/dislike distinction
are trying to do; see §6 and §7 below). But it does mean **the chapter's response to Street needs to
engage with her actual pain-specific argument, not only the general-purpose Darwinian Dilemma** — as
currently phrased, CANON §2.4's one-line response risks appearing, to a hostile referee who has read
Street's whole paper (which the Referee role at Gate 3 is instructed to be), to answer a different,
easier version of her argument than the one she actually made. **Recommend the Canon Keeper and the
Referee see this before Pass A drafting begins**, per `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §7.5 ("when the brief and
canon conflict, stop and ask the Canon Keeper" — this isn't quite a conflict, but it is a gap the
brief's confidence in CANON's stated response doesn't yet cover).

**Factual risk: none on the sourcing** — this section is built entirely from a direct, complete read of
the primary text. The risk is purely dialectical (see above), not evidentiary.

## 6. Moore's open-question argument — Principia Ethica (1903)

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED, via a Wikisource transcription of the primary text (not the
same as reading a verified critical edition) plus independent secondary summary; the physical/critical
text was not read directly this session.**

Full citation: G.E. Moore, *Principia Ethica* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1903), §13.

**The argument's core move, as transcribed:** Moore considers, as an example, the proposed definition
"good" = "that which we desire to desire." He then asks: "Is it good to desire to desire A?" — and
argues this question is exactly as intelligible, as open, as the original question "is A good?" His
conclusion (per the transcription): "any one can easily convince himself by inspection that the
predicate of this proposition — 'good' — is positively different from the notion of 'desiring to
desire.'" The generalized argument: for *any* proposed naturalistic (or supernaturalistic) definition
of "good," it remains a significant, non-trivial question to ask whether the property so defined is
itself good — which, Moore argues, it could not be if "good" simply *meant* that property (compare:
"is a bachelor an unmarried man?" is not a live question, because that's what "bachelor" means). This
is standardly read as an argument that goodness is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property — the
founding move of twentieth-century non-naturalist intuitionism, and the historical origin point Mackie
and later metaethicists are all responding to.

**How CANON wants this handled (§2.4):** "Fails against a posteriori identity claims (water/H₂O); this
is that kind of claim, not conceptual analysis." This is the standard Kripke/Putnam-derived reply used
throughout contemporary naturalist metaethics (Cornell realism and its descendants): "pain is bad" can
be a substantive, a posteriori identity claim like "water is H₂O," which also permits an "open
question" at the conceptual level (a pre-chemistry speaker can coherently ask "but is water really
H₂O?") without that openness showing water isn't H₂O. This is a well-established, respectable move in
the literature and does not need further sourcing beyond noting it's a standard reply, not a novel one
this book is inventing.

**Factual risk:** the Wikisource transcription used this session is almost certainly accurate in
substance (this is one of the most famous passages in twentieth-century ethics and is stable across
editions), but Wikisource is a crowd-transcribed source, not a critical edition. **The Verifier should
confirm exact wording against a standard scholarly edition (e.g., the Cambridge Texts in the History
of Philosophy edition) before this appears as a direct quotation with a page number.**

## 7. Illusionism — Frankish's own position

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED — the exact quotation below is drawn from Frankish's own
publisher/author page (his own website), which counts as close to primary as a summary source gets,
but the essay itself was not read in full this session.**

Full citation: Keith Frankish, "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness," originally *Journal of
Consciousness Studies* 23, nos. 11–12 (2016), a special double issue; reprinted as an edited book,
*Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness* (Exeter: Imprint Academic, 2017).

**Frankish's own stated definition, quotable and precise, from his own author page:**

> "Illusionism is the view that phenomenal consciousness (in the philosophers' sense) is an
> introspective illusion — that introspection misrepresents experiences as having phenomenal
> properties."

Frankish's position, per multiple secondary summaries this session, is not that pain or other
experiences don't occur — he affirms ordinary conscious episodes of feeling pain, seeing, hearing, and
so on happen. His claim is that these are, in reality, "complex, multifaceted functional states of the
brain," and that introspection systematically misrepresents them as having a further, ineffable
qualitative character ("phenomenal properties" in the philosophers' technical sense) which they don't
actually have.

**Fit to CANON's Tier 2 (§2.3):** this maps cleanly onto CANON's own framing — "Tier 2: illusionism
true. Representationalism: the relevant property is representational content that is intrinsically
avoidance-generating. Weaker metaphysics, identical normative output." Frankish's own position is
compatible with this reading (the functional/representational state is real and does the causal work;
only its felt, ineffable "what-it's-likeness" is, on his view, illusory) — the packet found nothing this
session that contradicts CANON's characterization, though the essay itself should be read directly
before any extended paraphrase of Frankish's argument (rather than his bare definitional statement)
appears in the manuscript.

**Factual risk:** low for the definitional quotation itself (drawn from Frankish's own author page,
which he presumably controls and keeps accurate); moderate for any claim about the *argument* for
illusionism beyond the bare definition, since the essay itself was not read this session.

## 8. The attitudinal alternative — Heathwood and Feldman, and its bearing on POS-01's stated
vulnerability

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED via the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's "Pleasure"
entry (fetched directly this session) and independent secondary summaries; neither Heathwood's nor
Feldman's own papers were read directly.**

Full citations: Fred Feldman, *Pleasure and the Good Life: Concerning the Nature, Varieties, and
Plausibility of Hedonism* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004); Chris Heathwood, "The Reduction of
Sensory Pleasure to Desire," *Philosophical Studies* 133, no. 1 (2007): 23–44.

**The core distinction, per the SEP's own framing:** attitudinal theories hold that what makes a
sensation pleasant (or unpleasant) is not some intrinsic felt quality of the sensation itself, but a
*propositional attitude* the subject takes toward it — roughly, taking attitudinal pleasure (or
displeasure) in the fact that one is having the sensation. **Feldman's version:** pleasure is "a single
propositional attitude" comparable to belief, "not supposed ever to essentially involve as such any
feeling at all" — sensory pleasures and pains become pleasant or unpleasant only contingently, insofar
as the subject takes attitudinal pleasure or displeasure in the fact of their occurrence. **Heathwood's
version differs on a technical point** (per the SEP, Heathwood denies pleasure's relevant attitude must
be strictly propositional in Feldman's sense) but shares the core commitment: badness (or goodness) is
constituted by a desire-involving attitude toward the sensation, not by the sensation's felt character
as such. Heathwood's own formulation, per the SEP: pleasure as roughly "believed satisfaction of
current desire."

**This is precisely POS-01's own stated known vulnerability, restated in `POSITIONS.md`:** "Heathwood
and Feldman's attitudinal theories hold that badness lies in the subject's dislike of a sensation, not
in the sensation's felt character itself. Pain asymbolia — patients who report the sensation without
its badness — is compatible with either reading: the attitudinal theorist can say the missing element
is the dislike, not a constitutive property." This packet's independent research this session confirms
that characterization is accurate and, if anything, strengthens it: **Colin Klein's "Lost Capacity"
model of asymbolia (§3 above) is itself readable as a sophisticated, empirically-grounded version of
exactly the attitudinal reading** — on Klein's account, what asymbolics have lost is a general capacity
to *care about* (i.e., to take the relevant negative attitude toward) threats to their bodily integrity,
not a sensory-level phenomenal property. This is good news for the honesty of the chapter (it means the
"concede it's live" instruction in CANON §2.4 has real, current, citable philosophical teeth behind it,
not just an abstract possibility) and should sharpen, not soften, how seriously the chapter takes this
objection.

**The demonstration CANON says is missing.** Per `POSITIONS.md` POS-01's own "what would change my
mind" entry, a worked demonstration that the framework's downstream architecture survives unchanged
under the attitudinal reading does not yet exist anywhere in canon, and per `POSITIONS.md`'s own
CANON-KEEPER QUESTION #2, it is unclear whether this demonstration is meant to happen for the first
time inside Ch. 5's draft or whether the Canon Keeper wants to pre-approve its content first. **This
packet flags, as a research finding rather than a drafting decision, that this demonstration is now
more urgent, not less** — it needs to show specifically that the rules/override/aggregation
architecture doesn't secretly presuppose the phenomenal reading in a way that breaks under Heathwood/
Feldman, and Klein's paper (§3 above) means a referee reading this literature could raise the objection
in a well-informed, citable form, not a hypothetical one.

**Factual risk:** neither Feldman's book nor Heathwood's paper was read directly this session — the
characterization above is SEP-mediated. **Before the chapter states either position's content as fact
(rather than via the SEP's own careful hedge-language), the Verifier should check both papers
directly**, particularly given how much weight this objection carries for a MODERATE-rated position.

## 9. Sinhababu's phenomenal introspection argument — Humean Nature (2017)

**Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE (a philosophy blog's reconstruction) for the argument's
precise structure; the book itself was not accessed this session.**

Full citation: Neil Sinhababu, *Humean Nature: How Desire Explains Action, Thought, and Feeling*
(Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2017). Related paper: Sinhababu, "The Epistemic Argument for
Hedonism," in a 2024 Springer volume (title of chapter not independently confirmed this session beyond
the search index entry).

**The argument, as reconstructed via secondary summary (not verified against Sinhababu's own text):**
(1) phenomenal introspection is a reliable way of forming the belief that pleasure is good (and,
extended, that pain is bad); (2) given the extent of persistent, fundamental first-order moral
disagreement, no other process for generating moral belief can be shown independently reliable; (3)
therefore pleasure's goodness (and pain's badness) is very probably the one thing we can know via moral
epistemology, because it's the one moral belief generated by a demonstrably reliable process. This maps
directly onto CANON §2.1's citation of Sinhababu for "the phenomenal introspection argument" and is the
closest thing in the existing philosophical literature to the book's own core methodological claim
("moral epistemology is therefore partly a matter of introspective acquaintance rather than inference
from evolved evaluative attitudes").

**A criticism worth the chapter knowing about even if it doesn't use it, REPORTED via the same
secondary source:** the argument has been criticized (by philosophy blogger Lance S. Bush, whose
critique this session accessed directly, though he is not a peer-reviewed academic source) on several
grounds — that hedonism is a metaphysical/normative thesis, not itself a phenomenological given, so
introspection can at most reveal *that* pleasure feels good-to-have, not settle the further
philosophical question of whether "good" there means stance-independently good; and that pleasant
experiences may simply lack any distinctively *moral* phenomenology at all, as opposed to a merely
positive one. This is a substantive, structurally similar worry to Moore's open-question argument
(§6 above) transposed onto Sinhababu's specific move, and the Drafter may find it useful to notice the
family resemblance even though Bush's specific piece is not citable as an authoritative source.

**Factual risk: this is the packet's second-weakest sourcing tier after the morphine claim.**
Sinhababu's book is explicitly named in both the brief and CANON as core supporting literature for this
chapter's central claim, and **it has not been read this session at any level beyond a secondary
blog's reconstruction.** This is a genuine gap given how load-bearing this source is. **Strongly
recommend the physical book (or at minimum the relevant chapter/paper) be obtained before drafting
begins** — this is flagged as the single highest-priority "needs the physical book" item in this
packet, ahead of Grahek, Mackie, and Moore, precisely because it's the source CANON leans on hardest
for the chapter's own positive argument, as opposed to handling an objection.

### B. Kahane, "Pain, Dislike and Experience" — RESOLVED at the abstract level

**Confidence: SOLID for the abstract, verified verbatim against the publisher's own page (Cambridge
Core); REPORTED (not independently verified) for the body of the article, which remains paywalled and
unread this session.**

Full citation: Guy Kahane, "Pain, Dislike, and Experience," *Utilitas* 21(3) (September 2009): 327–336.
DOI: 10.1017/S0953820809990070.

**The full abstract, exact wording, confirmed directly against Cambridge Core (the publisher):**

> "It is widely held that it is only contingent that the sensation of pain is disliked, and that when
> pain is not disliked, it is not intrinsically bad. This conjunction of claims has often been taken to
> support a subjectivist view of pain's badness on which pain is bad simply because it is the object of
> a negative attitude and not because of what it feels like. In this article, I argue that accepting
> this conjunction of claims does not commit us to this subjectivist view. They are compatible with an
> objectivist view of pain's badness, and with thinking that this badness is due to its phenomenal
> quality. Indeed, I argue that once the full range of options is in view, the most plausible account of
> pain is incompatible with subjectivism about value."

**This corrects the prior packet's framing (§10 above).** §10 speculated, sight unseen, that Kahane
occupies "a middle path between pure sensory-content views and pure attitudinal/dislike views." The
verified abstract shows this was not quite right: Kahane is not splitting the difference — he is making
a direct, objectivist argument that pain's badness is due to its **phenomenal quality**, and that this
survives conceding the (widely-held) premise that pain-without-dislike is not bad. His distinctive move
is showing that conceding the *contingency* between pain and dislike does **not** force subjectivism,
because the badness can still be constituted by the phenomenal character rather than by the dislike
itself. This is, if anything, **more directly supportive of CANON §2.1's constitutive claim** than the
prior packet's "middle path" framing suggested — Kahane and CANON are making structurally the same
move (badness = phenomenal character, not a further attitude), from different starting premises. This
is worth the Canon Keeper's attention: Kahane may be citable as an ally on the constitutive claim itself,
not only as a source on the definitional landscape.

**What is still NOT verified:** the body of the article — specifically, exactly how Kahane handles the
contingency premise mechanically (what work "phenomenal quality" is doing to block the subjectivist
inference) is not read directly. The abstract is a reliable guide to his conclusion and overall strategy
but not to the argument's step-by-step mechanics.

**Guidance for the Drafter:** the abstract passage above may be quoted directly and attributed with full
confidence (SOLID). Do not attribute specific argumentative steps beyond what the abstract states — use
"Kahane argues that pain's contingent dislike does not commit us to subjectivism about its badness"
(paraphrase) rather than inventing his supporting mechanism.

### C. The morphine-dissociation claim — two real, distinguishable primary sources now identified

**Confidence: BIBLIOGRAPHICALLY SOLID for both citations below (verified independently via PubMed, PMC,
and APA PsycNet/DOI records); CONTENT UNVERIFIED — neither paper's exact statistics or quoted patient
language was extractable this session, because both are pre-OCR-era scans/paywalled records that this
session's fetch tooling could not read past the abstract/metadata layer.**

This session found and distinguished **two different real phenomena that the brief's single "morphine
dissociation" framing was conflating**, each with its own correct primary source:

**(1) Beecher's WWII wounded-soldiers finding** — the "wounded soldiers requesting less analgesia than
expected" claim:

> Henry K. Beecher, "Pain in Men Wounded in Battle," *Annals of Surgery* 123(1) (January 1946): 96–105.
> PMC1803463 (open access, freely available — but this session's tooling could only retrieve the
> scanned page-image PDF, not machine-readable text, so no statistic below is independently confirmed
> this session).
>
> A near-simultaneous parallel publication exists: Beecher, "Pain in Men Wounded in Battle," *Bulletin
> of the U.S. Army Medical Department* 5 (April 1946): 445–454. PMID 21017778.

This is a real, correctly-dated, correctly-titled paper — it is not folklore. The commonly repeated
statistic in secondary pain-literature summaries (that a much smaller fraction of severely wounded
soldiers wanted morphine than civilians with comparable surgical wounds) is very likely an accurate
gloss of this paper's actual finding, but **this session could not confirm the exact percentage or any
exact sentence from Beecher's own text** — the primary document exists and is locatable (PMC1803463)
but requires a human or OCR-capable tool to read past the scan.

**(2) The distinct "sensation persists, suffering doesn't" claim from lobotomy/opiate patients** — this
is a *different* claim from Beecher's (which is about wounded soldiers requesting less analgesia, not
about patients directly reporting "it still hurts but doesn't bother me"). The standard citable primary
source for this second claim is:

> T.X. Barber, "Toward a Theory of Pain: Relief of Chronic Pain by Prefrontal Leucotomy, Opiates,
> Placebos, and Hypnosis," *Psychological Bulletin* 56(6) (November 1959): 430–460. DOI:
> 10.1037/h0046676.

This is a review paper that specifically catalogs reports of exactly this dissociation (sensation
without suffering) across **four different interventions** — prefrontal leucotomy (lobotomy), opiates,
placebo, and hypnosis — which is almost certainly why it is the literature's standard citation point for
the "I still feel the pain but it doesn't bother me" formulation, independent of which specific
intervention produced it in any given quoted case. **This session could not confirm the exact quoted
patient language** (Barber's paper is APA-paywalled; the abstract itself did not load past a JS
placeholder in this session's tooling).

**Dennett's essay specifically ("Why You Can't Make a Computer That Feels Pain") could not be located
this session** — the expected Tufts hosting URLs either 404'd or redirected to the Tufts homepage,
