## 1. Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968)

**Citation:** Hardin, Garrett. "The Tragedy of the Commons." *Science*, 162(3859), 1968,
pp. 1243–1248.

**Confidence: SOLID.** Verified by direct fetch of primary-text hosting (dhushara.com
transcription of the original *Science* article), cross-checked against the well-known
consensus wording found across multiple independent citation sources (Wikiquote, academic
mirrors).

### Exact key sentences

**The herdsman's calculation:**
> "As a rational being, each herdsman seeks to maximize his gain. Explicitly or implicitly,
> more or less consciously, he asks, 'What is the utility *to me* of adding one more animal
> to my herd?' ... The positive component is a function of the increment of one animal.
> Since the herdsman receives all the proceeds from the sale of the additional animal, the
> positive utility is nearly + 1. ... The negative component is a function of the additional
> overgrazing created by one more animal. Since, however, the effects of overgrazing are
> shared by all the herdsmen, the negative utility for any particular decision-making
> herdsman is only a fraction of − 1."

**The core tragedy statement — the chapter's likeliest pull-quote:**
> "Each man is locked into a system that compels him to increase his herd without limit — in
> a world that is limited. Ruin is the destination toward which all men rush, each pursuing
> his own best interest in a society that believes in the freedom of the commons. Freedom in
> a commons brings ruin to all."

**Use for the chapter:** this is the cleanest one-paragraph statement in the literature of
exactly the structural claim the brief wants — individual rationality (+1 for me, a fraction
of −1 for me, borne by all) producing collective ruin. It pre-empts payoff-matrix exposition;
the herdsman passage *is* the payoff matrix, in prose, which fits the brief's "no payoff
matrices" instruction if quoted rather than re-derived.

## 2. The standard correction — Ostrom, and McCay & Acheson

### 2a. Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990)

**Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE-CLASS** as extracted this session (multiple
independent secondary summaries agree closely, but I did not fetch and read Ostrom's primary
text directly this session — flag for Verifier to pull the book itself, or at minimum her
1990 Cambridge University Press text's introduction, before print).

**The core correction, as consistently characterized across sources:** Hardin's argument
conflates *open-access* resources (no one owns or controls access) with *common-property*
resources (a defined community owns and jointly manages access under its own rules). Ostrom's
empirical fieldwork — Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village forests, Spanish and
Filipino irrigation communities — documents communities that have self-governed shared
resources for centuries *without* Hardin's assumed ruin, by developing monitoring, graduated
sanctions, and locally-tailored access rules. This is the "unmanaged commons is not the
historical norm" correction the brief specifies.

**Note for the Drafter:** Ch. 3 of this book ("Eight Rules from a Spanish Water Court") is
explicitly built on exactly this Ostrom-adjacent material (Spanish irrigation courts). Ch. 1
should gesture at the correction — enough to answer the cynic and avoid presenting Hardin's
1968 model as the whole truth — without pre-empting Ch. 3's fuller treatment. This is a
continuity note, not a factual one; flagging for the Drafter and Continuity.

### 2b. McCay & Acheson, *The Question of the Commons* (1987)

**Citation:** McCay, Bonnie J., and James M. Acheson, eds. *The Question of the Commons: The
Culture and Ecology of Communal Resources*. University of Arizona Press, 1987.

**Confidence: SOLID** on the book's existence, contents, and argument — corroborated across
multiple independent descriptions (publisher copy, academic reviews) that agree closely; the
book's own framing (per its jacket/abstract as reported) explicitly takes Hardin's 1968 claim
as its starting point to test cross-culturally.

**The correction, as reported:** eighteen essays, the first systematic cross-cultural test of
Hardin's model, with twelve of seventeen empirical case studies focused specifically on
fisheries — directly relevant to the Ch. 1 opening case. Finding: "while tragedies of the
commons do occur under some circumstances, local institutions have proven resilient and
responsive to the problems of communal resource use" — i.e., Hardin's ruin is a possible
outcome of an unmanaged commons, not an inevitable property of shared resources as such.

### 2c. Hardin's own later concession — VERIFIED against the primary text

**Citation:** Hardin, Garrett. "Extensions of 'The Tragedy of the Commons.'" *Science*,
280(5364), 1 May 1998, pp. 682–683.

**Confidence: SOLID.** Full primary text read directly this session (fetched and read as a
PDF, not summarized by a third party).

**The exact passage — this is a strong, quotable, and *verified* find:**
> "To judge from the critical literature, the weightiest mistake in my synthesizing paper
> was the omission of the modifying adjective 'unmanaged.' In correcting this omission, one
> can generalize the practical conclusion in this way: 'A "managed commons" describes either
> socialism or the privatism of free enterprise. Either one may work; either one may fail:
> "The devil is in the details." But with an unmanaged commons, you can forget about the
> devil: As overuse of resources reduces carrying capacity, ruin is inevitable.'"

**Important correction to a commonly-repeated paraphrase:** a widely circulated secondhand
line — "the title of my 1968 paper should have been 'The Tragedy of the *Unmanaged*
Commons'" — is often presented as a Hardin quotation. **I could not find that exact sentence
in the primary 1998 text.** What Hardin actually wrote is the passage quoted above, which
makes the same substantive point but is meaningfully more careful (he calls it "the weightiest
mistake," not a title he wishes he'd used, and he goes on to specify that even a *managed*
commons "may fail" — he is not saying management is a guarantee). **The chapter must quote
the verified 1998 passage, not the popular paraphrase**, or it repeats exactly the kind of
"quotation dropped in as authority-decoration" the `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` identifies as a
failure mode of the prior draft. Also worth noting for the chapter's tone: earlier in the
same essay Hardin traces the idea to William Forster Lloyd's 1833 Oxford lectures on
overgrazed English pastureland — Hardin did not originate the herdsman example, only the
"tragedy" framing and its modern application; the essay is explicit about this debt.

## 3. The Newfoundland cod collapse — causal literature

### 3a. Primary academic sources (cited via a peer-reviewed secondary synthesis)

**Confidence note on this whole section:** the richest single source found this session for
the *causal* question specifically is Fred Mason, "The Newfoundland Cod Stock Collapse: A
Review and Analysis of Social Factors," *Electronic Green Journal*, 1(17), 2002 (UCLA,
peer-reviewed, open access, DOI 10.5070/G311710480) — read directly and in full this session
(fetched as PDF, human-equivalent full read of all pages, not a search-summary extraction).
This paper is itself a *review* synthesizing the primary literature below; its citations are
listed here individually so the Drafter/Verifier can go to the primary sources directly.
Confidence on everything drawn from this source: **SOLID** as a faithful account of what the
cited primary literature says, since the source itself was read in full — but each primary
finding should still be re-verified against its own original text before print, per standing
practice.

- **Hutchings, J. A., & Myers, R. A. "The biological collapse of northern cod off
  Newfoundland and Labrador."** In *The North Atlantic Fishery: Strengths, Weaknesses and
  Challenges*, Institute of Island Studies, 1995. Found (via Mason 2002) to have
  specifically tested and **discounted** two of the environmental excuses offered by
  government spokespeople right after the 1992 announcement: lower water temperature and
  higher water salinity as drivers of cod mortality/migration. Hutchings & Myers showed
  temperatures had been similarly low in the 19th century with no comparable collapse,
  undercutting a "it was really the environment, not us" framing that was in circulation at
  the time.
- **Myers, R. A., Hutchings, J. A., & Barrowman, N. J. "Why do fish stocks collapse? The
  example of cod in Atlantic Canada."** *Ecological Applications*, 7(1), 1997, pp. 91–106.
  Three core lessons per search-summary extraction (not independently re-verified against
  the original article this session — REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE for the specific wording,
  though the paper's existence and general findings are well-corroborated): (1) optimistic
  stock-assessment forecasts contributed directly to overfishing; (2) using commercial
  catch-per-unit-effort as an abundance index is a major risk, because improving technology
  lets a shrinking stock still be "found" and caught at a steady rate, masking decline; (3)
  recruitment overfishing risk is high even in a highly fecund species like cod.
- **Alverson, D. L. (1987).** *A Study of Trends of Cod Stocks off Newfoundland and Factors
  Influencing Their Abundance and Availability.* Ottawa: Department of Fisheries and Oceans
  — commissioned, per Mason (2002), by the **Newfoundland Inshore Fisheries Association**,
  not by DFO itself, specifically because the inshore fishery didn't trust DFO's numbers.
  Findings, per Mason (2002) citing Harris (1998): the official "assessments of abundance"
  were wrong; fish mortality was higher than DFO estimated; fishermen were catching high
  numbers of the exact cohort the model was counting on for future reproduction; and there
  was "a mathematical fault in the model in use, which got expanded over the years." Direct
  quote attributed to Alverson himself, via Harris (1998), p. 104: **"it took several years
  to find it [the mathematical fault] and by then, the stocks were on the downturn. I think
  it came as a shock to all of the fishermen and some of the scientists."**
  Confidence on this Alverson quote: **REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE**, two removes from the
  primary (Alverson, quoted in Harris's 1998 book, quoted in Mason's 2002 review, read by me
  directly) — the Verifier should pull Harris, *Lament for an Ocean* (1998), p. 104 directly
  before this is printed as a direct quotation.
- **Finlayson, A. C. (1994).** *Fishing for Truth: A Sociological Analysis of Northern Cod
  Stock Assessments from 1977–1990.* St. John's: Memorial University of Newfoundland,
  Institute of Social and Economic Research. Per Mason (2002): documents that inshore
  fishermen's warnings, though covered by media, were "filtered out long before it got to
  the ears of policy makers" or simply disregarded, and that "even when doubt about the
  scientific certainty of things was raised in the early 1980s and the Alverson report was
  tabled in 1987, government decision makers either ignored or forcefully quieted the
  naysayers." This is the named academic source for the brief's requested "inshore fishermen
  warning vs. offshore trawler data" stock-assessment controversy.
- **Sinclair, P. R.** Multiple works cited (1985, 1988, 1992, 1996) tracking the shift in
  who was doing the overfishing over time. **The single most important corrective sentence
  for the chapter, quoted by Mason (2002) directly from Sinclair (1996), p. 231:**
  > "In the first period [1970s], foreign trawlers were clearly responsible for overfishing,
  > but in the second [post-1977], this part was played mainly by Canadian trawlers, despite
  > the highly publicized contribution of foreign fleets."
  **This is the single most load-bearing quotation in this packet for the causal-nuance
  section below.** Confidence: **REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE** as a direct quotation (one
  remove — Sinclair via Mason, not the Sinclair original directly) but Mason's own full-text
  paper was read in full this session, which is stronger sourcing than the search-summary
  extractions elsewhere in this packet.
- **Steele, D. H., Andersen, R., & Green, J. M. "The managed commercial annihilation of
  northern cod."** *Newfoundland Studies*, 8(1), 1992, pp. 34–68. Source for the
  19,000-direct / ~20,000-indirect jobs figure (see §4 below) and for the account of DFO
  officials, in the immediate aftermath, floating environmental explanations (water
  temperature) that the later Hutchings & Myers (1995) work discounted.
- **Marchak, Patricia. "Uncommon Property."** In *Uncommon Property: The Fishing and
  Fish-Processing Industries in British Columbia*, Methuen, 1987. Offers a named rival frame
  to "tragedy of the commons": the **"tragedy of state mismanagement"** — the argument that a
  managed fishery like Newfoundland's, where the state (DFO) held licensing power, set
  quotas, and made all access decisions, is not a true commons at all (fishers had no
  management rights of their own), so its collapse is better described as a failure of state
  management than an instance of Hardin's model. **This is directly useful for the chapter's
  "standard correction" beat** — it's a second, independent scholarly voice (not Ostrom, not
  McCay/Acheson) making essentially the same point in this specific case: an unmanaged
  commons this was not.
- **Harris, Michael.** *Lament for an Ocean: The Collapse of the Atlantic Cod Fishery, A True
  Crime Story.* McClelland & Stewart, 1998. Journalistic/narrative account; source of the
  Alverson quote above and general color; the title itself ("A True Crime Story") signals its
  argumentative stance (mismanagement/negligence, not inevitability) and should be read by
  the Drafter as an advocacy-adjacent narrative source, not neutral, if used for texture.

### 3b. Cross-corroborated figures (from journalism/reference sources, not the academic
literature above)

- **Jobs lost, commonly cited figure:** "about 30,000" fishers and plant workers put out of
  work in Newfoundland and Labrador, described as the single largest mass layoff in Canadian
  history and roughly 12% of the province's entire labour force. **Confidence: SOLID** —
  independently corroborated across CBC News, *The Canadian Encyclopedia*, Britannica
  (title: "Cod fishery collapse of 1992"), and the provincial Heritage NL site, all agreeing

  figure may be counting "fishers and plant workers" specifically, a subset of the wider
  economic-impact figure), but the two numbers should not be silently conflated. **Safest
  chapter framing: "about 30,000 fishers and plant workers directly, with the wider economic
  toll running higher."** Do not state a single precise number as if it were uncontested.

- **Stock decline, two different metrics — do not conflate:**
  - "Northern cod populations fell to 1% of historic levels" by 1992 — this figure recurs

### 3c. Causal nuance — the summary this packet exists to provide

**This is the single most important section in this packet, per the brief's explicit
instruction that "the causal story is contested and the chapter must not oversimplify it into
a clean textbook commons case."**

The popular, one-sentence version — "Newfoundland's cod fishermen overfished their own
resource and it collapsed" — is not what the peer-reviewed literature supports, and Ch. 1
cannot use it uncritically without contradicting its own thesis about epistemic honesty.
What the literature (Mason 2002's synthesis of Sinclair, Finlayson, Hutchings & Myers,
Steele et al., and others) actually supports is closer to:

1. **Local inshore fishermen are substantially exonerated as a driver of the collapse.**
   They fished with fixed gear and small boats, operated closer to a subsistence/
   family-crew economy than an industrial one, and — per Sinclair (1996), as quoted in
   Mason (2002) — "overfishing by local inshore fishers must be discounted as a major factor
   in the collapse of the cod stocks," even though they "seem to bear the brunt of the
   problems that have come from it." They are victims of the outcome more than authors of
   it, which is exactly the tension the brief wants: the people who suffer the collapse are
   not, mainly, the people whose choices caused it.
2. **The overfishing itself had two phases with two different culprits.** Before 1977,
   foreign deep-sea trawlers (Spain, Portugal, the USSR, Japan, Korea, and others) were the
   dominant driver, exploiting largely open access before Canada's 200-mile exclusive
   economic zone took effect. **After 1977, once foreign boats were mostly excluded, the
   Canadian offshore dragger and trawler fleet expanded rapidly to fill the gap** — and per
   Sinclair (1996), it was this *domestic* industrial fleet, not the inshore fishery and not
   (mainly) the foreign fleet, that did most of the damage from the late 1970s through the
   collapse, "despite the highly publicized contribution of foreign fleets." The popular
   story that blames either "greedy local fishermen" or, alternatively, purely foreign
   trawlers both miss this: the dominant post-1977 driver was Canada's *own* industrial
   fleet.
3. **Government mismanagement compounded the overfishing rather than merely failing to
   prevent it.** DFO set Total Allowable Catch quotas based on its own scientists' stock
   models, which were built substantially on commercial catch-per-effort data — a method
   that fails specifically when a fleet's technology improves fast enough to keep finding and
   landing fish at a steady rate even as the underlying population collapses, which is what
   happened. When independent (Alverson 1987) and eventually DFO's own scientists identified
   the error and recommended large cuts (50%+ by 1989), the government did not fully act on
   the recommendation, for reasons Finlayson (1994) and Mason (2002) both attribute to the
   political cost of imposing sudden hardship on an already economically fragile region. The
   1992 quota itself, at 187,969 tonnes, exceeded the entire prior year's catch of 129,033
   tonnes — a quota set *above* what the whole industry combined had managed to land the
   year before, which is very hard to read as anything other than a political, not a
   scientific, number.
4. **This is genuinely a live scholarly disagreement about framing, not just emphasis.**
   Marchak (1987) argues the whole "tragedy of the commons" label is a category error here —
   a state-managed fishery, where DFO controlled licensing and quotas and fishers had no
   independent management rights, isn't an unmanaged commons in Hardin's sense at all; she
   calls it a "tragedy of state mismanagement" instead. Mason's (2002) own conclusion, after
   reviewing the literature, is that **no single model — not Hardin's tragedy of the
   commons, not Marchak's state-mismanagement frame — fully accounts for the collapse**,
   because overfishing (real), state mismanagement (real), scientific error (real), and some
   environmental contribution (contested, and substantially discounted by Hutchings & Myers
   1995 for the specific temperature/salinity claims DFO offered right after the
   announcement) were all simultaneously true.

**What this means for the chapter, stated plainly:** Ch. 1 can and should use Newfoundland
cod as its opening case — it is real, well-documented, emotionally powerful, and structurally
apt. But if it's presented as a clean, symmetrical "everyone overfished the shared resource
and it collapsed" parable, a referee in fisheries sociology or Newfoundland studies will
correctly call it a textbook oversimplification, and — given this book's specific thesis
about epistemic honesty being a moral duty — that would be a self-undermining error in
exactly the chapter meant to establish the book's credibility. The honest version, which is
also the more interesting one: the people usually blamed (inshore fishermen) are largely not
the cause; the actual overfishing was concentrated in an industrial fleet (first foreign,
then Canadian) that inshore fishermen were competing against, not colluding with; and a
state management system that was supposed to prevent exactly this kind of collapse
contributed to it through a specific, traceable technical error (mistaking stable catch
rates for a stable population) that its own scientists eventually caught and that politics
then failed to act on in time. That's still a coordination-failure story — arguably a
richer one, because it shows the failure operating at *multiple* levels (fleet vs. fleet,
science vs. politics) rather than one flat "everyone versus the resource" plane — but it is
not the flattened version, and the chapter needs to say so rather than let the reader infer
the flattened version from an emotionally clean opening scene.

## 4. Thomas Schelling, *Micromotives and Macrobehavior* (1978)

**Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE-CLASS.** I did not fetch and read primary text of
the book directly this session (it is not freely available online in full); the summary
below is drawn from multiple independent secondary descriptions (publisher copy, academic
summaries, course materials) that agree closely with each other and with the book's
well-established scholarly reputation, but exact page-quotable sentences were not
independently verified against the primary text this session. **The Verifier or Drafter
should pull the book directly** — it is a widely available, short, non-technical work, and
the chapter would be strengthened by at least one page-cited direct quotation rather than
paraphrase, given the standing instruction against "quotations dropped in as
authority-decoration" — the fix for that failure mode is a *specific, cited* Schelling
quotation, not omitting Schelling.

**Core concept, relevant to Ch. 1:** the book's organizing idea is the gap between
*micromotives* (individually sensible reasons for individual choices) and *macrobehavior*
(the aggregate, often collectively regrettable, pattern those choices produce) — precisely
the brief's argument stated in Schelling's own vocabulary. Specific mechanisms Schelling
develops that are relevant to establishing "structures," per the brief's instruction to
present structures rather than a taxonomy:

- **Tipping and critical-mass dynamics:** small, individually mild preferences (e.g., a mild
  preference for neighbors "like oneself") can produce extreme, unintended collective
  outcomes (total segregation) once a threshold is crossed — extreme outcomes from
  non-extreme individual intentions.
- **Self-reinforcing/self-fulfilling collective patterns:** individual behavior responds to
  an environment made up of other people's responses, producing feedback loops where the
  "rational" individual choice depends entirely on what everyone else is expected to do —
  directly relevant to the stag-hunt structure the chapter is establishing (I'll stand if
  you'll stand; I'll blanket-apply if everyone else will).
- **Self-enforcing conventions** (Schelling's examples reportedly include things like
  daylight saving time and the standard typewriter keyboard) as the *positive* mirror image
  of the same mechanism — coordination equilibria that are stable and (unlike the tragedy
  cases) benign, useful for the chapter if it wants one sentence establishing that the same
  structural logic isn't inherently bad, only sometimes trapped in a bad equilibrium.

**Caution:** because this section is REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE-CLASS rather than
primary-verified, the chapter should not put invented specific wording in quotation marks
attributed to Schelling. Paraphrase attributed generally to the book's argument is safe;
a "quote" is not, until the Verifier pulls the primary text.
