## PART ONE — The opening case, built out: Newfoundland cod, 1992

### The people

- **John Crosbie** — federal Minister of Fisheries and Oceans, Progressive Conservative MP
  for St. John's West, a Newfoundlander himself. Announced the moratorium.
- **The Bay Bulls crowd, July 1, 1992** — roughly 1,000 fishers, plant workers, and family
  members at a Canada Day event in Bay Bulls, a fishing community about 30 km south of St.
  John's, the day before the moratorium was announced. Not individually named in the
  sourcing found this session; the exchange is on video and widely quoted.
- **D. L. (Donald) Alverson** — American fisheries scientist commissioned by the Newfoundland
  Inshore Fisheries Association in 1987 to independently audit DFO's stock assessments,
  because the inshore fleet's own experience (falling catches) didn't match DFO's official
  picture of a healthy, growing stock.
- **Richard Cashin** — founding president of the Fish, Food and Allied Workers union (FFAW),
  who led a delegation to Ottawa in February 1989 to warn the federal government about the
  socio-economic impact of the cod-quota cuts the union believed were coming and necessary.
- **DFO's own scientists**, unnamed individually in what I found this session, who by 1989
  were recommending Total Allowable Catch (TAC) cuts of over 50% — recommendations the
  government did not fully act on.

### Place / dates

Newfoundland and Labrador, Canada. Key dates, in sequence:
- **1977**: Canada declares a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone, pushing out most
  foreign trawlers; the Canadian offshore trawler/dragger fleet expands rapidly to "pick up
  the surplus."
- **1987**: Alverson report commissioned and delivered — finds DFO's stock-abundance
  assessments wrong, identifies a mathematical fault in the assessment model.
- **February 1989**: Richard Cashin/FFAW delegation warns Ottawa.
- **1989**: DFO's own scientists recommend TAC cuts of 50%+; government does not fully
  comply.
- **July 1, 1992**: Crosbie confronted by a crowd of fishers and families at a Canada Day
  event in Bay Bulls.
- **July 2, 1992**: Crosbie announces the moratorium. The 1992 quota he had set was 187,969
  tonnes — against a catch of only 129,033 tonnes the *previous* year, i.e., the government
  had authorized a catch well above what the fleet had even managed to land the year before.
- **1993**: Moratorium, initially framed as temporary, is extended indefinitely as five more
  Atlantic cod stocks collapse.
- **1994**: Northern cod stocks measured at less than 5% of their already-depleted 1990
  level.
- **2024**: Limited commercial cod fishing reopens after 32 years — twelve times longer than
  the "two-year" moratorium originally announced.

### What happened

By 1992 the Northern cod stock — which had sustained a fishery for roughly 500 years — had
collapsed to a small fraction of its historic size. On July 2, 1992, Crosbie announced a
moratorium on Northern cod fishing that he described as short-term. It put approximately
**30,000** fishers and plant workers directly out of work (a more granular breakdown in the
peer-reviewed literature puts the figure at 19,000 directly affected plus roughly 20,000
more in indirect/economic-backlash job losses — see `evidence.md` for the discrepancy), in
what's described as the single largest mass layoff in Canadian history — about 12% of
Newfoundland and Labrador's entire labour force, in a single announcement. More than 400
coastal communities lost their only major employer. Over the following decade the province's
population fell by roughly 10% as people left for work elsewhere.

**The proof-of-structure detail the brief wants** (the cynic's objection answered): this
was not a surprise. Inshore fishermen had been reporting falling catches for years and
pointing to what the offshore drag­ger and trawler fleets were hauling in. Distrusting DFO's
official assessments — which said the stock was healthy — the inshore sector paid for its
own independent scientific study (the Alverson Commission, 1987) specifically to prove what
they already believed from experience. The study found DFO's numbers were wrong. It was
substantially ignored for years. By 1989, DFO's *own* scientists were recommending TAC cuts
of over 50%; the government still set quotas higher than that recommendation. The
1992 quota itself (187,969 tonnes) was set higher than the entire prior year's catch
(129,033 tonnes) — meaning the government authorized more fish to be taken than the
industry, with the *whole* offshore and inshore fleet combined, had even been able to find
and land the year before. Nobody in this story is confused about what's happening. The
inshore fishermen aren't wrong. DFO's own scientists aren't wrong, eventually. And the
outcome happens anyway.

### The Crosbie/Bay Bulls confrontation — verified

On July 1, 1992 (Canada Day), the day before the formal announcement, Crosbie attended a
Canada Day event at Bay Bulls, a fishing community roughly 30 km south of St. John's. A
crowd of roughly 1,000 fishers, plant workers, and their families — who by then knew or
strongly suspected what was coming — confronted him on the wharf. Video of the exchange
exists (YouTube: "The end of the Cod - John Crosbie in Bay Bulls Canada Day 1992").

**Verified quote, cross-confirmed across two independent written sources (CBC, The
Independent) with matching wording**:
> "Why are you yelling at me? I didn't take the fish from the goddamn water, so don't go
> abusing me."

Context of the exchange, per The Independent's account: a fisher asked Crosbie something to
the effect of "Are you a Newfoundlander or is Ottawa more important?"; Crosbie told the crowd
"There's no need to abuse me"; the fish-from-the-water line followed; a voice off-screen
answered "Well, who took it then?" and another shouted back, "You and your goddamn people
took it. You and your people took it."

**Confidence: SOLID** on the core quote and its wording — corroborated independently by CBC
News (which calls the moratorium "his hardest political moment") and The Independent's
detailed account, both consistent on exact phrasing. I did not personally watch the archival
video this session to confirm audio against the transcribed quote; the Verifier should do
that before print, given the book's stated standard that every quotation be exact. See
`evidence.md`.

### The one vivid physical detail

Crosbie stood on the wharf in his Ottawa uniform — navy wool coat, dark suit, white shirt,
tie — visibly out of place among fishers and plant workers in bright nylon jackets and ball
caps, the day before he ended their livelihoods, telling them he didn't personally put the
net in the water. The clash of costume is not a metaphor added after the fact; it's what's
in the footage and the reporting on it.

### What the moratorium was supposed to last vs. what happened

Crosbie framed it, and it was widely reported and remembered, as a **two-year** measure to
let the stock rebuild. It became indefinite in 1993 as five additional Atlantic cod stocks
collapsed alongside Northern cod. It did not meaningfully lift until **2024** — thirty-two
years later, sixteen times the announced duration — and even then only a limited commercial
reopening, not a return to the pre-1992 fishery.

### Why this case fits the brief precisely

Every actor in this story is being reasonable given their position. The inshore fishermen
are not lying about what they're seeing; they pay for their own science to prove it. DFO's
scientists, once they update their model, recommend the correct cut — and are overruled or
diluted by a political process weighing 400 communities' immediate survival against a
biological forecast. Crosbie, a Newfoundlander himself, is not a cartoon villain profiting
from the collapse; he's the one standing on the wharf taking the abuse, and his own defense —
"I didn't take the fish from the goddamn water" — is *true*. No single actor's individually
reasonable choice explains the outcome. That is exactly the shape the chapter needs, and it
is why the case survives the cynic's "this is just greed" objection: the people who saw it
coming most clearly (the inshore fishermen) are the ones who could do the least to stop it
alone, and the person with formal authority to stop it (Crosbie) inherited a trajectory
already locked in by a decade of decisions distributed across thousands of people and two
governments.

**The causal-nuance warning — must be handled in the chapter, not smoothed over.** The
popular, one-line version of this story ("Newfoundland fishermen overfished the cod and it
collapsed") is not what the specialist literature supports, and using it uncritically would
be exactly the kind of oversimplification `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` and this book's whole thesis
warn against. See `evidence.md`, "Causal nuance," for the full breakdown — in short: local
inshore fishermen, using small boats and fixed gear, are substantially exonerated in the
peer-reviewed literature as a driver of the collapse; the dominant overfishing pressure came
first from foreign deep-sea trawlers (pre-1977) and then from the **Canadian** offshore
dragger and trawler fleet (post-1977, after the 200-mile limit pushed foreign boats out and
Canadian vessels expanded to fill the gap) — compounded by a government stock-assessment
methodology that mistook steady catch rates (achieved through improving technology finding
an ever-shrinking stock) for a healthy population, and by a political system that kept TACs
above what its own scientists recommended for years after the error was identified. If the
chapter frames "the fishermen" as an undifferentiated collective who overfished their own
resource, a referee in fisheries sociology will flag it immediately, correctly, and the
chapter's credibility is exactly what's on the line given the book's epistemic-honesty
thesis.

**One structural note on the brief's naming of "FRCC/DFO scientists":** the Fisheries
Resource Conservation Council (FRCC) was **created in 1993, in the wake of the moratorium** —
it did not exist to warn anyone before 1992. The relevant pre-1992 scientific-advisory body
was DFO's own internal science branch (through CAFSAC, the Canadian Atlantic Fisheries
Scientific Advisory Committee). If the Drafter wants a named post-collapse institutional
voice, FRCC is right; if the Drafter wants the pre-collapse warning voice, it needs to be
DFO/CAFSAC scientists and the independently-commissioned Alverson report, not FRCC. Flagging
this now so it isn't caught later at Gate 4.

## PART TWO — Second structure candidates (arms race / race-to-the-bottom / stag hunt)

Per the brief: the Prologue already used a WWI-Christmas-truce-adjacent register is reserved
for Ch. 2, and the brief explicitly steers away from a payoff-matrix taxonomy toward one
second structure "explained properly." Ranked below.

### 1. Amazon HQ2 — the interstate/intercity bidding war (2017–2019) — RANK 1

**The structure:** a race-to-the-bottom / tax-competition trap. Individual cities and states,
each acting rationally to try to win a prize (Amazon's promised 50,000 jobs), compete by
escalating public subsidies to a private company — producing an outcome (massive public
giveaways, thin or negative net public benefit, and in the most-cited case a public backlash
so severe the winner walked away from half the prize) that serves no city's actual interest,
while no individual city can unilaterally stop bidding without simply losing to a rival that
keeps bidding.

**The people:**
- **Amazon** — solicited competitive bids from North American cities starting September
  2017; 238 cities submitted proposals.
- **Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA)**, publicly warned in a series of tweets, **September 7, 2017**,
  that the contest would become a "race to the bottom," and separately asked why the ~20
  shortlisted cities didn't collectively bargain rather than "collectively beg."
- **Gov. Steve Bullock (D-MT)**, on the same dynamic: "It really does become a race to the
  bottom" — it "can also mean gutting long-term tax revenues." (Reported February 2020.)
- **Mayor Rahm Emanuel (Chicago)** and **Mayor Marty Walsh (Boston)** — both cities
  submitted competing bids (Chicago's included roughly $2–2.5 billion in tax breaks and up
  to $100 million in free land) even as the "race to the bottom" framing was being publicly
  debated.
- New York's incentive package for the Long Island City site — before its Feb. 14, 2019
  withdrawal — totaled roughly **$1.525 billion** (a $1.2B refundable Excelsior tax credit
  plus a $325M cash grant). Virginia's winning package for the "National Landing" site
  (Arlington/Alexandria) was roughly **$573 million**.
- The Long Island City bid collapsed after sustained local political opposition (including
  from Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, whose district bordered the site) forced Amazon to
  withdraw rather than proceed through the city's Public Authorities Control Board — meaning
  the "winning" outcome for one of the two announced sites was cancellation, not victory.

**The cost:** hundreds of millions to billions of dollars in forgone public revenue per
bidding city, a public-relations and political crisis in the "winning" city that ended in
withdrawal, and — per multiple named officials, on the record, *while their own cities kept
bidding* — a dynamic every serious participant called self-defeating in real time, not in
hindsight.

**The one vivid detail:** a sitting member of Congress publicly calling the process a "race
to the bottom" in September 2017, while dozens of cities — including ones whose own mayors
would later be quoted using nearly identical language — kept competing, because unilaterally
dropping out doesn't stop the race, it just forfeits your city's chance in it.

**Why it's ranked first:** unlike the cod case, this is a race-to-the-bottom / tax-
competition structure rather than a commons tragedy — giving the chapter's "two structures,
not five" a genuine second shape, not a restatement of the first. It is extremely
well-documented, recent, has named officials on the record using the book's own vocabulary
("race to the bottom") about their own behavior, and has a clean resolution (NYC's
withdrawal) that dramatizes the cost without requiring invented drama.

**Factual risks:**
- Some sourcing (dollar figures, specific incentive-package breakdowns) is aggregated from
  business-press reporting (GeekWire, Bloomberg, Fortune, Axios) rather than an audited
  government filing pulled directly this session — treat exact dollar amounts as
  REPORTED-BUT-NOT-INDEPENDENTLY-AUDITED pending Verifier re-check; the *existence* and
  rough scale of the packages, and the quotes, are corroborated across multiple independent
  outlets.
- This is a *corporate-subsidy* race-to-the-bottom, not a classic interstate tax-rate race
  (e.g., corporate income tax competition). If the Drafter specifically wants the "states
  lowering general tax rates to compete for any business" version rather than the
  "one-company bidding war" version, this case is adjacent but not identical — Wisconsin
  Gov. Scott Walker's 2011 "open for business... Illinois is raising taxes, we are lowering
  them" line (documented in search results this session but not independently re-verified)
  is a possible substitute/supplement if the Drafter wants the more general tax-competition
  framing instead of the single-company-bidding-war framing.

### 2. Standing at concerts/stadiums — the pocket illustration, not a full case — RANK 2

**The structure:** a pure stag-hunt/coordination trap in miniature. Once the people in front
of you stand, standing is your individually rational best response — you can't see over them
sitting — even though every row of people would collectively prefer everyone stayed seated.
It's a stable, Nash-equilibrium-consistent outcome (stand/stand) that's worse for everyone
than the alternative equilibrium (sit/sit), and no individual can unilaterally return the
group to the better equilibrium.

**The people:** none named — this is a structural/behavioral pattern, not a real, dated
event with real people, and it should not be presented in the book as if it were. Scott Page
(University of Michigan) has formalized it as a 2×2 game in his "Model Thinking" course
material, but that's a teaching example, not a case.

**Why it's ranked second, and how it should be used:** the brief explicitly floats this as a
possible "pocket example," not a co-equal second case — and that's the right instinct. It
has no named people and no verifiable date/place, so per Standing Instruction #1
("never invent a citation, a quotation, a statistic, or a date"), it cannot be dressed up as
a documented case. It works well as a one-or-two-sentence illustration *inside* the chapter
(every reader has lived it, it needs no citation because it's making a point about structure,
not reporting a fact) but should not be positioned as the chapter's second worked example in
place of Amazon HQ2.

### 3. Cigarette advertising and the 1971 broadcast ban — considered, ranked third/backup

**The structure:** a genuine arms race with an unusually clean resolution. Before 1971, U.S.
cigarette makers (reporting names include Philip Morris, American Blend, Tareyton, Liggett &
Myers) spent heavily on TV/radio advertising, largely to fight for market share rather than
to grow the total market — a classic prisoner's-dilemma arms race where every firm's
individually rational move (advertise) left all firms worse off collectively (high costs,
flat aggregate demand) than mutual restraint would have. The Public Health Cigarette Smoking
Act banned broadcast cigarette advertising starting January 1, 1971 — and by several
accounts, industry profits *rose* afterward, because the ban ended the arms race for
everyone simultaneously in a way no single firm could have unilaterally achieved by just
stopping its own ads (a competitor would have taken the share).

**Why it's ranked third and not used as the primary second case:** two problems. First, I
could not find, within this session's search budget, a named individual on the record at the
time saying anything like "I hate this arms race and I'm doing it anyway" — the best-sourced
executive quote found (Frank Saunders of Philip Morris, October 1971) is post-ban commentary
downplaying TV advertising's effect on new smokers, not a "trapped and hating it" statement.
This is a corporate/aggregate story, weaker on the brief's "real people, named" requirement
than Amazon HQ2. Second, and more seriously: an AEI piece surfaced in this session's search
argues the ad ban *increased* smoking (by removing pro-competitive brand-switching ads and
leaving only the industry's own point-of-sale and non-broadcast marketing, or by other
mechanisms), which is a genuinely contested empirical claim about the case's real-world
effect that this session did not have the budget to adjudicate. Given Standing Instruction
#2 ("never state a contested empirical claim at high confidence"), this case carries a
built-in risk of the chapter either ignoring a real controversy in its own evidence base or
needing to spend words litigating it. Not recommended over Amazon HQ2 for that reason, but
flagged here in case the Drafter or Showrunner wants the cleaner "structure" (true arms race,
not race-to-the-bottom/tax-competition) that this case offers over Amazon HQ2's version.
