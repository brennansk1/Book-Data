## (a) The collapse of the English duel — Appiah's account

### The scene: Wellington v. Winchilsea, Battersea Fields, 21 March 1829

**Named people:** Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington — at the time serving Prime Minister
of the United Kingdom — and George Finch-Hatton, 10th Earl of Winchilsea. Seconds: Sir Henry
Hardinge for Wellington, Lord Falmouth (Edward Boscawen) for Winchilsea.

**The cause:** Winchilsea, an ultra-Protestant opponent of Catholic emancipation, wrote a public
letter (printed in *The Standard*, 14 March 1829) accusing Wellington of "an insidious design for
the infringement of our liberties and the introduction of Popery into every department of the
State" — specifically over Wellington's support, as head of government, for the Catholic Relief
Bill and a related King's College London controversy. Wellington demanded a retraction; Winchilsea
refused to give one without a fight, because in the honour code of the time simply withdrawing the
words on demand — rather than either fighting or being forced to apologise after standing on the
field — would itself have been dishonourable. **This is the case's real argumentative payload
before a shot is even fired: the code required the confrontation regardless of whether either man
actually believed dueling settled anything about who was right.**

**What happened on the field, sequence verified across multiple sources (see `evidence.md` §1 for
full sourcing and the discrepancy history):** the two men stood twelve paces apart, at eight in the
morning. **Wellington fired first and missed** — by his own later account deliberately, though
contemporaries noted drily that he was known as a poor shot, so the claim of deliberate misses was
not universally believed. **Winchilsea then fired into the air** — a recognized dueling gesture
called "deloping," signalling that he would not shoot at Wellington and had never intended to,
almost certainly agreed with his second beforehand. Winchilsea then handed over a written apology
retracting the "disgraceful and criminal motives" charge; one source (the Woolley & Wallis auction
house's account of surviving letters) states Winchilsea's first draft ("I deeply lament...") was
judged insufficient and a fuller, unconditional retraction was required before the men would shake
hands — a small, vivid, almost bureaucratic detail (an insufficient apology sent back for a
redraft, in the middle of a duel) worth checking against a fuller primary source before use.

**The vivid detail that makes this scene rather than summary:** nobody was hurt, both men had
almost certainly pre-arranged not to actually try to kill each other, and the whole affair still
had to be staged in full — pistols, paces, seconds, dawn, a public field — because the *form* of
honour satisfaction mattered independent of anyone's actual desire to fight. That gap between the
form's seriousness and its participants' evident lack of interest in mortal combat is, per Appiah,
close to the whole story of how duelling actually died: not by force of argument but because the
form kept being performed by people who visibly no longer meant it, until performing it became
embarrassing rather than honourable.

**Fit to the argument:** near-perfect. This is Appiah's own opening case in *The Honor Code* (the
book "begins with the end of dueling in England, which came swiftly following the news in 1829 of
pistol shots between the Duke of Wellington and the Earl of Winchelsea" — see `evidence.md` §2),
and it does exactly the chapter's required double duty: it shows an honour code functioning as
real coordination technology (a costly, legible, third-party-verifiable signal that settles who
backs down without actual violence in the vast majority of cases — most duels, including this one,
ended without a fatality) while also being the case that best sets up the interlocutor's objection
("if honour is just a useful convention, why did anyone think it was *binding*, and why does the
book's own account of its collapse — via ridicule, not refutation — make it sound like it never
really was binding at all?").

**Factual risks / what needs the physical book:**
- The "insufficient first draft of the apology" detail is REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE
  (Woolley & Wallis, an auction house citing its own manuscript holdings) — plausible and
  specific enough to be real, but not independently corroborated this session. Flag before use.
- Whether Wellington's miss was genuinely deliberate is *itself* a live period dispute, not a
  settled fact — contemporary opinion split, per the sources reviewed. The chapter should state
  this as contested ("he said he missed on purpose; he was also known as a poor shot") rather than
  asserting deliberate marksmanship as fact.
- All Appiah-specific material (his framing of "honour worlds," the exact sentence and paragraph
  structure of how he opens the book with this scene, any additional period detail he supplies
  beyond what's independently verifiable) is drawn from reviews, an NPR excerpt, and a scholarly
  review (Philosophical Quarterly) quoting him — not from the book directly. **The Verifier must
  check the book itself** before any sentence is presented as a direct Appiah quotation rather than
  a quotation-of-a-quotation. See `evidence.md` §2 for exactly which phrases are attributed to
  which layer of sourcing.

## (b) The Maghribi traders — Greif's coalition, the geniza, and the Edwards–Ogilvie dispute

**Named people:** the case is unusual among the five in that it does not center on one or two
named protagonists but on a merchant *network* — eleventh-century Jewish traders from the Maghrib
(North Africa) operating across the medieval Mediterranean and Indian Ocean trade, reconstructed by
economic historian **Avner Greif** (Stanford) from the **Cairo Geniza** documents. The Geniza itself
has a findable origin story with named people: a storeroom in the Ben Ezra Synagogue in Fustat
(old Cairo), a repository for worn-out documents bearing God's name that Jewish custom forbade
destroying, containing roughly 200,000-plus fragments accumulated over centuries; **Solomon
Schechter** brought a large portion to Cambridge beginning in 1896–97; the documentary trade
material specifically (letters, accounts, legal deeds, mostly in Judaeo-Arabic) was opened up as a
historical source above all by **S.D. Goitein** (1900–1985), whose multi-decade study underlies
Greif's own work. Greif's foundational papers: "Reputation and Coalitions in Medieval Trade:
Evidence on the Maghribi Traders," *Journal of Economic History* 49:4 (1989), 857–882; and
"Contract Enforceability and Economic Institutions in Early Trade: The Maghribi Traders'
Coalition," *American Economic Review* 83:3 (1993), 525–548.

**The mechanism, in brief (full sourcing in `evidence.md` §3):** overseas trade in this period
required merchants to hire agents abroad to sell and buy goods on their behalf, with no fast
communication and essentially no cross-border legal system that could enforce an agency contract.
Greif's claim is that the Maghribi traders solved this — the classic principal–agent problem — with
a **reputation-based coalition**: merchants only hired agents from within their own network;
information about any agent's cheating (embezzling, misreporting a sale, shirking) circulated
through the group; and a merchant caught cheating one principal was collectively frozen out by
*all* of them, not just the one he wronged, forever, unless and until he made restitution. Because
future employment was worth more than any single act of theft, this "multilateral punishment
strategy" made honesty individually rational even with no court to appeal to. Greif's own
quantitative finding (his 1989 and 1993 papers): the documents show explicit legal/court activity
was a small minority of dispute-relevant material; most enforcement ran through the reputation
network itself, not litigation.

**Vivid detail:** the mechanism was, in effect, an ancient distributed credit-rating system running
on gossip and correspondence instead of a database — a trader thousands of miles from home, with no
enforceable contract in hand, was kept honest by the knowledge that a single act of embezzlement,
once reported in a letter that would circulate through the community, would end his career
permanently. This is a clean, almost mechanical illustration of "reputation as coordination
technology" — arguably the cleanest of any candidate in this packet, because Greif's own economic
modeling makes the "why does this work" logic explicit and legible rather than left implicit in a
narrative.

### The required flag: the Edwards–Ogilvie dispute

Per the brief, **this controversy must be flagged, not silently omitted.** Economic historians
**Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie** published a series of papers (working paper 2008; revised
2009; published version, *Economic History Review*, 2012) disputing Greif's reading of the same
Geniza evidence. Their claim, in its most forceful early form: the Maghribi traders' actual
contract enforcement ran mainly through **formal legal mechanisms** (Jewish courts and,
secondarily, Islamic courts), not an informal multilateral reputation coalition, and that Greif's
own cited examples do not actually show a "coalition" imposing collective punishment on cheaters —
in Edwards and Ogilvie's words (as summarized in Greif's own rebuttal), "not a single empirical
example adduced by Greif shows that any 'coalition' actually existed." Notably, their own position
**shifted across the three iterations of the paper** — from "the legal system was the main
mechanism" (2008) to "legal system alongside informal reputation mechanisms" (2009) to "legal
mechanisms as a last resort alongside reputation-based sanctions, similar to European merchant
practice" (2012) — which is itself worth noting in the chapter or its endnotes as a sign the debate
narrowed rather than resolved.

Greif responded at length (2008 CESifo working paper reply; a longer 2012 "reappraisal" piece), 
arguing Edwards and Ogilvie mischaracterize his original claim as asserting the Maghribi traders
were a single, monolithic, fully cohesive coalition — a claim he says he never made — and that his
own quantitative reading of the documents (less than roughly one percent of the relevant material
concerning legal activity) still supports reputation, not courts, as the dominant mechanism.
Even Edwards and Ogilvie, per Greif's characterization of their position, concede "a form of
collective punishment does appear to have been used" among the Maghribis at least sometimes — so
the live disagreement is over **how central and how systematic** the reputation mechanism was,
not over whether anything resembling it existed at all.

**How the chapter should handle this, if it uses the case:** one clean sentence is enough — something
like "a later generation of economic historians has pushed back hard on how systematic this
reputation mechanism really was, and the fight isn't fully settled" — rather than adjudicating the
dispute in text. The book's own epistemic-honesty thesis (CANON.md standing instruction #2) means
this cannot be silently dropped if the case is used at all, but a 4,500-word chapter with an
opening case, an interlocutor, and an Axis-2 foreshadow does not have room to litigate it either.

**Fit to the argument:** very strong on the mechanism, weaker on "opening scene" per the brief's own
rule, because there is no single dated incident with named individual protagonists — the case is
a pattern across a documentary corpus, best told through Greif's own reconstructed *logic* rather
than through one merchant's story (none of the individual Geniza letters found in this session's
research surfaced a single, clean, nameable narrative comparable to Wellington/Winchilsea or
Gunnar of Hlíðarendi). Strongest as the chapter's mechanism-made-explicit beat, second to a more
scene-able opener.

**Factual risks:** solid — Greif's own journal articles were read directly this session (PDFs
located and reviewed via search-and-fetch, not merely summarized secondhand), which puts the core
mechanism claim on firmer footing than the Appiah or Boehm material. The controversy itself is
well-documented via Greif's own hosted "debate" page, which is inherently one-sided (it's his own
site) — the chapter/Verifier should ideally also pull Edwards and Ogilvie's own published 2012
*Economic History Review* piece directly rather than relying only on Greif's characterization of
it, which this session did not do. **Flag: the Edwards–Ogilvie side of this dispute is currently
represented only through Greif's own summary of their claims, not their own words** — a
one-sidedness the chapter's own honesty standard should not inherit silently.

## (c) Medieval Iceland — Njal's saga and the feud-law system

**Named people and dates:** the Icelandic Commonwealth ran from the founding of the Althing
(national assembly) in 930 CE to submission to the Norwegian crown in 1262–64. *Njal's Saga*
(*Brennu-Njáls saga*), written by an unknown author around 1280, dramatizes events set c. 960–1020,
centered on two named protagonists: **Njal Þorgeirsson**, described in the saga as the era's
greatest lawyer, and his friend **Gunnar Hámundarson of Hlíðarendi**, a celebrated warrior. Modern
economic/legal treatment: **David Friedman**, "Private Creation and Enforcement of Law: A
Historical Case," *Journal of Legal Studies* 8:2 (March 1979), 399–415, and a later, expanded
chapter-length treatment in his more recent book-in-progress on historical legal systems.

**The system, briefly:** Iceland had no king, no executive, and no police. All law was, in
Friedman's own framing, effectively civil rather than criminal — there was no public prosecutor;
a wronged party (or their kin) had to bring the case themselves to a court convened at the Althing
or a regional assembly. Judgment produced not state-enforced punishment but a liability: the
loser owed compensation (a scaled wergild, varying by the victim's status) or, for the most
serious offenses, was declared an **outlaw** — stripped of all legal protection, meaning anyone
could kill him without consequence, and (for "full outlawry") banished from Iceland entirely.
Enforcement of a judgment was left to the winning party and their allies to carry out themselves.
Political power over the system was held by **goðar** (chieftains), whose office (**goðorð**) was,
remarkably, a form of transferable private property — it could be bought, sold, inherited, or
split — rather than a fixed territorial jurisdiction, meaning a farmer unhappy with his chieftain's
conduct could in principle switch allegiance to another.

**The vivid scene — Gunnar's return, and the case's own honesty about the system's limits:** after
a feud escalates (started, in the saga's telling, over an argument between Njal's and Gunnar's
wives, and compounded by a string of killings), Gunnar is sentenced at the Althing to **lesser
outlawry** — three years' exile — as part of a negotiated settlement rather than a fight to the
death. He accepts the judgment and rides toward the coast to leave the country with his brother
Kolskegg. On the way, his horse stumbles; Gunnar looks back up at his home hillside and, per the
saga (paraphrase pending exact-translation check — see flag below), says something to the effect
of "how lovely the slopes are — I have never seen them so lovely before" — and decides on the spot
to ride home instead of leaving. This breaks the terms of his outlawry. His enemies, led by
**Gizur the White**, assemble roughly thirty men and besiege his farmhouse; Gunnar is killed.
**This is simultaneously the saga's most famous romantic image and a precise illustration of the
system's real limit: the Commonwealth had no police to make Gunnar leave, and no army to make his
enemies stand down once he stayed — enforcement, in the end, ran entirely through the private
violence of the injured party's allies, exactly as Friedman's account says it structurally had to.**

**Fit to the argument:** strong on "here is a functioning, non-obvious coordination technology" —
the wergild/outlawry system converted potentially endless blood feud into a court process with
graduated, negotiable stakes, for roughly three centuries, without a state. Slightly better fit,
arguably, to Ch. 3's "neither market nor state" argument than to this chapter's "morality as
technology invented repeatedly" argument — Iceland's law code is closer to Ostrom-style mechanism
design (Axis 1) than to a pure honour/reputation case (though outlawry is, in effect, a
reputation-and-exclusion mechanism functionally similar to the Maghribi coalition's, which is a
usable connective thread if the Drafter wants one). If used, its strongest role in *this* chapter
is as connective tissue back to Ch. 3's Ostrom material, or as an example of a rule/punishment
system whose underlying purpose (contain feud, prevent runaway retaliation) is legible in
hindsight exactly the way the brief specifies.

**Factual risks:**
- The "how lovely the slopes are" line is a widely repeated paraphrase across summaries and
  popular treatments; **the exact wording depends entirely on which English translation is used**
  (Magnus Magnusson and Hermann Pálsson's Penguin Classics translation is the standard one; their
  exact rendering was not independently checked against the primary text this session). Treat as
  paraphrase-with-attribution until the Verifier checks a specific edition.
- Friedman's essay content could not be directly fetched this session (persistent access errors
  across three attempted URLs/mirrors); the summary above rests on secondary description of his
  argument (a blog excerpt that quoted one passage directly, plus search-engine summaries of the
  essay's contents), not a direct read of the 1979 *Journal of Legal Studies* piece. **Flag for
  the Verifier: obtain and read Friedman's essay directly before quoting him.**
- The saga is a literary text written roughly 250–300 years after the events it depicts, by an
  author using earlier oral tradition and possibly earlier written sources; historians treat its
  specific dialogue, and probably some of its specific incidents, as dramatized rather than
  documentary, even though the broader legal and political institutions it depicts are
  independently corroborated by *Grágás* (Iceland's actual medieval law code, surviving in
  manuscript) and other sources. **The chapter should not present saga dialogue as a transcript**
  — this is exactly the kind of "legend vs. documented" distinction Ch. 3's packet had to draw for
  the Tribunal de las Aguas, and it applies here with even more force, since Njal's Saga is
  explicitly a literary saga, not primarily a legal record the way the Valencia case had actual
  court archives behind it.

## (d) Boehm's reverse dominance hierarchies

**Named person and works:** anthropologist **Christopher Boehm**. Foundational paper:
"Egalitarian Behavior and Reverse Dominance Hierarchy," *Current Anthropology* 34:3 (June 1993),
227–254. Expanded into the book *Hierarchy in the Forest: The Evolution of Egalitarian Behavior*
(Harvard University Press, 1999).

**The argument:** humans, like chimpanzees, have an evolved individual disposition toward
dominance-seeking — but unlike chimpanzee troops, human forager bands are (per Boehm's cross-
cultural ethnographic survey) reliably, deliberately egalitarian among adult males. His claim is
that this isn't the absence of hierarchy; it's hierarchy *inverted* — a **"reverse dominance
hierarchy"** in which the group, acting collectively, dominates any individual member who tries to
dominate them. Egalitarianism, on this account, is not a natural default but "a political
achievement" — an actively maintained coalition of the many against the ambitions of any one.

**The mechanism — a graduated toolkit, per Boehm's own cross-cultural survey (societies cited
include San/Bushmen groups of southern Africa, Hadza and other East African foragers, and
Australian Aboriginal groups, among others):** criticism and ridicule of anyone acting
arrogant or self-aggrandizing; disobedience/non-compliance with anyone attempting to give
unilateral orders; ostracism; and, in the most extreme and persistent cases, execution of a
would-be dominator by group consensus. The escalation ladder itself — gossip and mockery first,
banishment later, killing only as a last resort against someone who won't stop — is the most
directly usable piece of theory for this chapter: it is a punishment *system*, graduated exactly
the way Ostrom's Design Principle 5 (graduated sanctions, already featured in Ch. 3 via Valencia)
describes, aimed not at outsiders but at insiders who threaten to capture the group.

**Fit to the argument:** this is arguably the chapter's best *theoretical* material — it pushes
the argument to its most general form (even the suppression of tyranny itself is a coordination
technology, invented independently across unrelated forager societies worldwide, which is exactly
the brief's "invented repeatedly because the problems recur" claim in its starkest version) — but
it is the weakest *opening scene* of the five, because Boehm's evidence is aggregated cross-
cultural pattern rather than one dated, named incident. If the Drafter wants a scene rather than a
pattern, the material would need a single ethnographic vignette pulled from one of Boehm's cited
field studies (e.g., a specific named upstart being ridiculed or ostracized in a specific
documented case) — this session did not locate one specific, nameable incident of that kind in the
material reviewed; the Verifier/Drafter should check Boehm's book directly for whether he supplies
one that could anchor a scene.

**A genuine complication worth flagging, not hiding, if this material is used:** critics of
Boehm's thesis (noted in passing in some of the secondary material reviewed, not independently
verified this session) have questioned how much of his ethnographic record reflects genuinely
"pristine" pre-state forager egalitarianism versus societies already shaped by centuries of contact
with states and colonial powers that suppressed indigenous hierarchy formation from outside — a
methodological worry structurally similar to the "how much of this is really ancient vs. recently
reconstructed" flags Ch. 3's packet raised about Valencia's own origin legends. **Not independently
confirmed this session — flag for the Verifier if Boehm's material is used at any length.**

**Factual risks:** the core citation and definition are solid (a full copy of the 1993 paper was
located and reviewed directly this session, via a hosted PDF). The book (*Hierarchy in the
Forest*) itself was not accessed — only reviews, summaries, and publisher description. **Any
extended quotation from the book, as opposed to the 1993 paper, needs the physical book.**

## (e) A modern honour-code-as-technology case — dueling scars (Mensur)

**What it is:** the *Mensur*, a still-practiced (though now rare and heavily safety-regulated)
ritual fencing bout among German and Austrian student fraternities (*Studentenverbindungen*),
dating to the late eighteenth century. Two students face off at a fixed, close distance with sharp
sabres, wearing protective gear everywhere except the face, and fence — not to injure, and not
really to "win" in any competitive sense, since the bout is not scored as a contest — until a
referee calls first blood, almost always a cut to the scalp or cheek. The resulting scar
(*Schmiss*, literally "smite," documented as a recognized status marker at least since the 1820s)
was historically worn as a visible, permanent, unfakeable badge of class membership and personal
composure: proof a man had stood still under a blade without flinching. In the German civil
service of the 1920s, membership in these dueling fraternities correlated with career advancement —
one figure found in this session's research states roughly one-fifth of senior Prussian civil
service posts in 1928 were held by former members of one particular federation of these
fraternities (REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE this session — needs a stronger citation before use).

**Fit to the argument:** this is one of the brief's own three suggested options for a modern case
(alongside tipping and queueing), and it's the most vivid of the three by a wide margin — a scar is
a piece of the body converted into a costly, permanent, third-party-legible signal, functionally
identical in logic to the honour-satisfaction mechanism in case (a), just running today in a small,
self-selected community instead of an entire national gentry class. It's a strong closing beat for
the chapter: proof the underlying mechanism (costly, hard-to-fake signaling of in-group
trustworthiness/toughness) isn't a historical curiosity confined to the duel proper — it recurs,
almost unchanged in structure, wherever a group needs a way to verify who's really committed.

Tipping and queueing (the brief's other two suggestions) were also researched this session and are
real, well-theorized examples of norm-as-coordination-technology (tipping as a self-enforcing
social-pressure equilibrium; queueing as a low-cost fairness convention enforced by mutual
observation, structurally similar to the huerta's own "watching each other while waiting" monitoring
mechanism already used in Ch. 3), but neither produced a comparably vivid, scene-able concrete
detail in this session's research — they're better suited to a single explanatory sentence than a
worked case, if the Drafter wants a lighter modern touch than Mensur.

**Factual risks:** the practice's history and general contours (origin, form, the scar-as-status-
marker point, its persistence today in muted/regulated form) are corroborated across multiple
independent sources reviewed this session and are reasonably solid at the REPORTED-AND-CROSS-
CHECKED tier (see confidence key in `evidence.md`). The specific 1928 civil-service statistic is
weaker — single-sourced this session — and should not be used without a stronger citation. No
physical book is at issue here (this is web-native historical material, not drawn from a single
scholarly monograph), but the Verifier should still independently confirm the civil-service figure
before it appears in print.
