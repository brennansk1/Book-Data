## 1. The Wellington–Winchilsea duel, 21 March 1829 — verified core facts

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED for the sequence of events**, cross-referenced across
Wikipedia's "Wellington–Winchilsea duel" article, the King's College London archives exhibition
page, the Woolley & Wallis auction house's account (drawing on its own manuscript holdings related
to the Finch-Hatton family), and the British Newspaper Archive's blog treatment of contemporary
coverage. No primary newspaper or manuscript source was read directly this session — all of the
above are secondary accounts, though they converge closely on the sequence of events, which raises
confidence in that sequence specifically.

**Date and place:** Saturday, 21 March 1829, 8:00 a.m., Battersea Fields (then just outside
London, in Surrey). **CONVERGED across all sources checked.**

**The participants:** Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington, then serving as Prime Minister —
the second sitting PM in British history to fight a duel (after William Pitt the Younger, who
dueled George Tierney in 1798). His opponent: George Finch-Hatton, 9th or 10th Earl of Winchilsea
(sources differ on the numbering — Wikipedia's Finch-Hatton article gives "10th Earl"; one earlier
search summary this session returned "9th Earl" — **flag: confirm the correct ordinal before print,
likely a simple discrepancy in different peerage-numbering conventions, not a different person**).
Seconds: Sir Henry Hardinge for Wellington; Lord Falmouth (Edward Boscawen) for Winchilsea.

**The cause, with an exact quoted phrase, REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED:** Winchilsea, in a public
letter dated 14 March 1829 and printed in *The Standard*, accused Wellington — over his support as
head of government for Catholic emancipation and a related King's College London dispute — of "an
insidious design for the infringement of our liberties and the introduction of Popery into every
department of the State." This exact phrase appears consistently across the sources checked and is
treated as REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED rather than SOLID only because no source checked this session
reproduced a photograph or transcription directly from *The Standard* itself — the phrase is
consistently reported as a quotation of the letter, not paraphrased differently by different
outlets, which is a good sign but not the same as reading the original.

**What happened when they fired — the discrepancy this session had to resolve:** an early search
pass returned a garbled/ambiguous account (possibly conflating the two men's actions). The
cross-checked, converged sequence, confirmed independently by the Woolley & Wallis account and the
British Newspaper Archive blog treatment: **Wellington fired first and missed.** Wellington's own
later claim was that he missed deliberately; several sources note dryly that he was well known as a
poor marksman, so contemporaries were not universally convinced the miss was intentional — **this
is a genuine, worth-keeping ambiguity, not a resolved fact, and the chapter should present it as
such** ("he said he missed on purpose — he was also famous for missing"). **Winchilsea then fired
into the air**, a recognized dueling gesture called "deloping" — a deliberate refusal to fire at
one's opponent, understood in the code duello as a way of satisfying honour's formal requirement
(standing and firing) while making clear no injury was intended. This was, per multiple accounts,
almost certainly agreed between Winchilsea and his second beforehand, not a spontaneous mercy.

**The apology:** Winchilsea provided a written retraction on the field. One source (Woolley &
Wallis, REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE this session) states his first draft, beginning "I deeply
lament," was judged insufficiently unconditional and a fuller retraction was demanded and produced
before the parties considered the matter closed — a vivid, almost bureaucratic detail (an apology
sent back for a redraft, mid-duel) that would strengthen the chapter's "form matters more than
content" point if independently confirmed, but should not be used as stated fact until the
Verifier locates a second source.

**Aftermath, REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED:** Wellington's standing was, by multiple accounts,
strengthened rather than damaged by the episode — press coverage was scandalized in tone but
public reaction largely favored him for having stood and fired rather than declined the challenge.
The two men subsequently reconciled; Wellington attended Winchilsea's 1834 wedding (to Wellington's
own great-niece, Emily Bagot, per Wikipedia — **flag: this specific family-relation detail was not
independently cross-checked beyond Wikipedia this session**).

## 2. Appiah's argument and mechanism — sourced entirely through secondary material

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED for the shape of the argument; individual quoted phrases
below are marked by tier. No sentence from the book itself was read directly this session.**

Full citation: Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen* (New York:
W.W. Norton, 2010).

**That the book opens with this exact case, REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED (NPR's own promotional
excerpt page, independently corroborated by a review citing the same opening):** "Appiah's case
begins with the end of dueling in England, which came swiftly following the news in 1829 of pistol
shots between the Duke of Wellington and the Earl of Winchelsea." The book goes on to treat two
further "moral revolutions" through the same honour-code lens: the end of footbinding among
upper-class Chinese women, and the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade within the British Empire
— **the chapter should not need either of the other two cases, but the Drafter should know they
exist in case a connective one-line reference is useful.**

**The core mechanism, as summarized by reviewers (this is the passage the chapter most needs to
get right, and it is the least directly sourced):**
- A review by Michael Gibb (Philosophical Quarterly, fetched directly this session,
  REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED against other summaries): "The popularity of the duel waned...as the
  honour world it served lost its influence and ability to flout the law," combined with an
  erosion of the practice's *exclusivity* — once dueling spread down the social scale to men who
  were not gentlemen, it stopped functioning as a distinctively gentlemanly signal and its point
  (proving membership in an exclusive honour-bound class) was lost. Gibb's review also notes
  Appiah's own key term, **"honour world"** — a group sharing a common code, within which
  membership confers a distinctive kind of respect unavailable to outsiders — and flags, as a
  mild criticism, that Appiah uses the concept "a little too loosely" and that the sample of three
  moral revolutions may not be very representative of moral change in general. **This is worth
  keeping in mind for the chapter's own epistemic honesty: Appiah's account is itself a contested,
  single-author reading of a historical pattern, not a settled finding.**
- A quoted phrase attributed directly to Appiah, appearing in David Brion Davis's *New York Review
  of Books* review (fetched directly this session — this is a quotation-of-a-quotation, REPORTED
  via a single reviewer, not independently checked against the book): **"ridicule at last did more
  than morality to kill dueling."** If used as a direct quotation in the manuscript, this needs
  Verifier confirmation against the book itself — it is presented by Davis as Appiah's own words,
  and Davis is a serious historian reviewing carefully, which raises confidence, but it is still
  one layer removed from the primary text.
- A related summary, cross-checked across two independent search results this session: gentlemen
  kept dueling in defiance of "reason, Christian duty, and the law...until such time as the
  practice is rendered ridiculous by its adoption by the lower orders" — i.e., Appiah's account is
  explicitly that **moral and legal arguments against dueling had existed for a long time and had
  already failed** to stop it; what actually killed it was a change in who else was doing it and
  how it was perceived, not a new argument. This is the single most load-bearing claim for the
  chapter and it converges across multiple independent secondary sources, which is the strongest
  confidence this packet can offer without the book itself.

**Davis's (NYRB) criticism of Appiah, worth building into the chapter's own honesty rather than
overclaiming Appiah's account as settled, REPORTED via direct fetch of the review:** Davis argues
Appiah's honour-only account is too monocausal — that moral and legal arguments against dueling did
not simply become irrelevant but acquired "new pragmatic force" as social conditions changed (rising
"self-made men," a newly assertive middle class, new codes of respectable domestic masculinity
exemplified by figures like Cardinal Newman's ideal gentleman as "one who never inflicts pain"),
and that treating honour-recoding as the *whole* explanation obscures a genuinely multicausal
historical process. **This is a useful, ready-made version of the "concede a real complication"
move the book's own standing instructions favor** — the chapter can use Appiah's mechanism as the
core story while noting, in a clause rather than a paragraph, that historians reasonably dispute how
much of the causal weight it carries alone.

**On the collapse's actual timeline — approximate, not from a single canonical source:** dueling in
England is generally treated by the sources reviewed this session as reaching a late peak in roughly
the first three decades of the nineteenth century and then collapsing across the following
generation: the Army formally banned dueling among officers in 1844 ("hastening its demise
throughout the kingdom," per one source); the last fatal duel between two English protagonists on
English soil was fought at Gosport on 20 May 1845 (Lieutenant Henry Hawkey vs. James Alexander
Seton, over Hawkey's wife); the last duel of any kind fought on English soil was in 1852, between
two French political exiles (Frederic Cournet and Emmanuel Barthélemy) at Priest Hill, near
Englefield Green. **The brief's phrase "the ~50-year window in which duelling died" is a reasonable
characterization of this span (roughly the 1800s–1810s peak to 1852) but this session did not find
one canonical source stating "fifty years" as a precise, agreed figure — treat as an approximate,
defensible characterization, not a quotable statistic.** REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED across three
independent sources for the individual dates (1844 army ban, 1845 Hawkey–Seton duel, 1852
Cournet–Barthélemy duel); the "fifty-year" framing itself is this packet's own synthesis, not a
sourced quotation, and should be phrased in the chapter as an approximation.

## 3. Avner Greif and the Maghribi traders — primary-source-grounded

**Confidence: SOLID for the core mechanism claim and full citations — Greif's own journal articles
were located and read directly this session, not merely summarized secondhand.** This is the
best-sourced material in this packet.

**Full citations:**
- Avner Greif, "Reputation and Coalitions in Medieval Trade: Evidence on the Maghribi Traders,"
  *Journal of Economic History* 49, no. 4 (1989): 857–882.
- Avner Greif, "Contract Enforceability and Economic Institutions in Early Trade: The Maghribi
  Traders' Coalition," *American Economic Review* 83, no. 3 (1993): 525–548.

**The evidentiary base:** the **Cairo Geniza** — a storeroom (a *genizah*, Hebrew for a repository
for documents bearing the name of God, which Jewish custom forbids destroying) in the Ben Ezra
Synagogue in Fustat (old Cairo), containing an estimated 200,000-plus medieval manuscript fragments
preserved for roughly nine centuries. **Solomon Schechter** recovered and transported a large
portion of the collection to Cambridge University beginning in 1896–97. The specific "documentary"
Geniza material — letters, business accounts, legal deeds, largely in Judaeo-Arabic (Arabic written
in Hebrew script), dating mostly c. 1000–1250 — was opened up as a historical source above all
through the multi-decade study of **S.D. Goitein** (1900–1985), on whose foundational work Greif's
own economic analysis directly builds. REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED across multiple independent
encyclopedia/academic sources this session; the Schechter/Goitein background is well-established
historical fact, not contested.

**The mechanism, SOLID (drawn directly from Greif's own papers and their abstracts/framing as
read this session):** eleventh-century long-distance Mediterranean and Indian Ocean trade required
merchants to entrust goods to overseas agents they could not directly supervise, with no fast
communication and no reliable cross-border court to enforce an agency agreement. Greif's claim: the
Maghribi traders solved this principal–agent problem through a **coalition** — a closed network in
which merchants would only do business with agents drawn from within the group; information about
any agent's conduct (honest dealing vs. cheating — embezzlement, misreported sales, shirking)
circulated through community correspondence; and an agent found to have cheated even one merchant
was collectively frozen out by the *whole* network — a "multilateral punishment strategy" — not just
denied further work by the one merchant he wronged. Because the expected value of a long honest
career within the network exceeded the one-time gain from cheating, this made honesty the
individually rational choice even in the total absence of an enforceable contract. Greif's own
quantitative finding, cited in his rebuttal materials (see §4 below): legal/court-related content
made up a small fraction of the documents' relevant material — his cited figure is "less than one
percent," REPORTED via Greif's own summary of his findings, not independently recomputed this
session — with most enforcement running through the reputation network rather than litigation.

## 4. The Edwards–Ogilvie dispute — summarized fairly

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED for the existence, timeline, and general shape of the
dispute; CONTESTED as a matter of substance (that's the point — this is a live, unresolved
disagreement in economic history, not a settled question either side has stated is closed). One
important asymmetry to flag: this packet accessed the dispute primarily through Greif's own
hosted summary of it (his personal "debate" page and his own rebuttal papers), not through
Edwards and Ogilvie's published work directly — see the flag at the end of this section.**

**The critics:** Jeremy Edwards (Cambridge) and Sheilagh Ogilvie (Cambridge), a well-established
economic historian whose broader body of work has repeatedly challenged reputation-based and
"social capital"-based explanations of pre-modern economic institutions in favor of formal,
legally grounded ones.

**Their claim, across three published iterations (dates and characterizations REPORTED via
Greif's own rebuttal summaries, not Edwards/Ogilvie's papers themselves — flag below):**
- 2008 (CESifo Working Paper No. 2254): the main contract-enforcement mechanism the Maghribi
  traders actually used was **the legal system**, not an informal reputation coalition.
- 2009 (Cambridge Working Papers in Economics No. 0928): a revised position — enforcement ran
  through the legal system **alongside** informal reputation-based mechanisms.
- 2012 (published version, *Economic History Review*): a further-revised position — the evidence
  is insufficient to determine the legal system's relative importance with confidence, but legal
  mechanisms were used "as a last resort" alongside reputation-based sanctions, in a pattern the
  authors argue resembles enforcement practices among medieval European merchants more broadly
  (i.e., the Maghribi traders were not exceptional, contra one implication some readers draw from
  Greif's account).

**Their strongest specific claim, quoted via Greif's rebuttal (so this is Greif's transcription
of their words, not independently verified against their own paper this session):** "not a single
empirical example adduced by Greif shows that any 'coalition' [imposing multilateral punishment]
actually existed." Greif's rebuttal states that Edwards and Ogilvie nonetheless concede "a form of
collective punishment does appear to have been used" among the Maghribis in at least some
instances — meaning, on Greif's own characterization, the live disagreement concerns how
**systematic and central** multilateral reputation enforcement was, not whether any collective
punishment happened at all.

**Greif's response:** he argues Edwards and Ogilvie attack a stronger claim than he made — that
they read him as asserting the Maghribi traders were a fully "monolithic" and "cohesive" single
coalition, a characterization he says he explicitly rejected in his own original work. His 2008
reply and 2012 "reappraisal" paper (both located and reviewed this session) maintain his original
reading of the documents, while acknowledging that both reputation and courts likely mattered to
some degree, with their relative importance varying by period and context — and, in his 2012
piece, drawing a contrast with contemporary Genoese traders, whom he argues relied on a genuinely
different, more court-centered enforcement mix, as a way of showing the Maghribi case isn't just
"how merchants worked everywhere" but a distinct institutional choice.

**Required flag for the Drafter and Verifier:** **this packet did not access Edwards and Ogilvie's
own published papers directly this session** — only Greif's characterizations of their claims,
across his own hosted "debate" page and his own rebuttal PDFs. This is a real limitation: Greif is
an interested party summarizing his critics' position, and even a scrupulously fair summary (which
his reads as, including the concession he attributes to them) is not the same as reading Edwards
and Ogilvie's 2012 *Economic History Review* article directly. **Before the chapter states the
dispute's shape as fact, the Verifier should pull Edwards and Ogilvie's own published version and
confirm Greif's characterization of their claims and concessions is accurate.** This is exactly the
kind of one-sidedness the book's own epistemic-honesty thesis would flag if found in someone else's
work, and it should not be allowed to pass silently here.

## 5. Christopher Boehm — reverse dominance hierarchies

**Confidence: SOLID for the 1993 paper (located and read directly this session, full PDF).
REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED only for the 1999 book (Boehm's own subsequent, fuller treatment) —
that book itself was not accessed this session, only reviews and publisher description.**

**Full citations:**
- Christopher Boehm, "Egalitarian Behavior and Reverse Dominance Hierarchy," *Current Anthropology*
  34, no. 3 (June 1993): 227–254.
- Christopher Boehm, *Hierarchy in the Forest: The Evolution of Egalitarian Behavior* (Cambridge,
  MA: Harvard University Press, 1999).

**The core claim, SOLID (from direct review of the 1993 paper):** humans share with chimpanzees
and other great apes an evolved individual disposition toward dominance-seeking, yet documented
nomadic forager societies are reliably, actively egalitarian among adult males — not through the
absence of hierarchy, but through hierarchy *inverted*: the group, acting in coalition, dominates
any individual member who tries to dominate it. Boehm's own term for this, quotable and precise:
**"reverse dominance hierarchy."**

**The graduated suppression toolkit, SOLID (from direct review of the 1993 paper), roughly in
escalating order:** criticism and ridicule directed at anyone behaving arrogantly or trying to
accumulate disproportionate status; disobedience and simple non-compliance with anyone attempting
to issue unilateral orders; ostracism (social and, in some cases, physical exclusion from the
group); and, reserved for persistent cases that don't respond to the lower rungs, execution of a
would-be dominator by group consensus. Boehm frames this explicitly as a *political achievement*
— a maintained coalition, not a natural default state — which is a useful, quotable framing for the
chapter's own thesis (morality-as-technology, actively built and maintained, not simply "how people
naturally are").

**Named societies cited in the 1993 paper (REPORTED via direct review, cross-cultural survey
rather than single case studies):** San/Bushmen groups of southern Africa; the Hadza and other
East African forager societies; Australian Aboriginal groups; among others in a broader
cross-cultural survey. **No single, dated, individually-nameable incident (a specific named upstart
being ridiculed or executed on a specific occasion) was located in this session's review of the
1993 paper** — the evidentiary base as reviewed is aggregate ethnographic pattern across many field
studies, not a single scene. If the Drafter wants a scene-level anecdote from Boehm rather than the
general pattern, the 1999 book (not accessed this session) is the more likely place to find one, and
should be checked directly.

**A methodological complication, flagged but not independently verified this session:** some
secondary discussion encountered in passing raises a question about how much of the ethnographic
record Boehm draws on reflects forager egalitarianism as it existed before sustained contact with
colonial and state authorities, versus societies already reshaped by that contact — a concern
structurally similar to the "how much of this tradition is really ancient vs. later-reconstructed"
flags raised in Ch. 3's packet about the Tribunal de las Aguas. **This was not independently
confirmed or sourced to a specific critic this session — flag for the Verifier if Boehm's material
is used at length rather than in the graduated-sanctions/reverse-dominance-hierarchy framing above,
which rests on Boehm's own theoretical claim rather than on any single contested case study.**

## 6. Modern case — dueling scars (Mensur)

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED for the practice's general history and form (converged
across multiple independent sources this session: a general history piece, a "missed history"
feature, and general encyclopedic treatment). REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE for the specific
civil-service statistic below — do not use without further sourcing.**

**The practice:** the *Mensur*, a ritual fencing bout among German (and Austrian) university
fraternities (*Studentenverbindungen*), originating in the late eighteenth century. Two students
face off at a fixed, close distance, protected everywhere except the face and scalp, and fence with
sharpened sabres until a referee calls first blood — not a scored contest, and historically not
intended to seriously injure. The resulting facial or scalp scar, called a *Schmiss* ("smite"),
functioned since at least the 1820s as a recognized, permanent, visible badge of class membership
and of the bearer's proven composure under a blade.

**A specific, flagged statistic (REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE, do not use without a second source):**
one source located this session states that in 1928, roughly one-fifth of senior civil service
positions in Prussia were held by former members of one particular federation of these dueling
fraternities (the Kösener Senioren-Convents-Verband). **This is a striking, quotable number if the
chapter wants a "this wasn't just social color, it was a literal career credential" beat — but it
was found in only one source this session and must be independently confirmed by the Verifier
before appearing in the manuscript.**

**Fit:** functions in the chapter as a modern instance of the same costly-signal logic already
established through Wellington/Winchilsea — a scar as a permanent, hard-to-fake, third-party-legible
proof of commitment, doing for a small self-selected community today roughly what the duel did for
the whole English gentry two centuries ago.

## 8. Note on Axis 2 foreshadowing (per CANON.md §5 and the brief's "must not overclaim")

Nothing in this packet's material licenses more than the chapter is allowed to claim. Worth flagging
explicitly for the Drafter: **several of this packet's own cases contain their own internal
foreshadowing of Axis 2 (circle failure) without any extra material being needed.** The Wellington–
Winchilsea case is explicitly a code that applied only to gentlemen — a coordination technology
functioning beautifully for its in-group while conferring nothing on everyone outside it. The
Maghribi coalition is, by definition, an in-group reputation mechanism that worked precisely because
it excluded outsiders (an agent who wasn't part of the network couldn't be trusted or extended
credit within it) — the same structural feature that makes coordination technology able to curdle
into cartel-like exclusion is visible in the same case that illustrates the chapter's main thesis.
The Mensur case is even sharper on this point — a scar that functioned as a career credential in the
Prussian civil service is coordination technology and social gatekeeping in the same gesture. **The
chapter does not need to introduce new material to gesture at Axis 2 — it can foreshadow it by
noting, in one sentence near the end, that every mechanism just described also drew a line around
who counted, and ask what happens when the excluded show up — without answering the question.**
This keeps the interlocutor hinge ("if morality is just useful, it isn't binding") and the Axis-2
foreshadow doing complementary work rather than competing for the chapter's limited space.
