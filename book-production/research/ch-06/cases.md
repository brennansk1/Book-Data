# Ch. 6 ("Whose Suffering") — Candidate Cases

Per `CHAPTER_BRIEFS.md`, the brief offers three opening-case candidates (Somerset v Stewart 1772;
the first veterinary anaesthesia debates; Singer's own conversion account) and does not rank them —
Gate 1 is where the Showrunner picks. Per `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` §2.4, this packet expands to five

## RANKED RECOMMENDATION

1. **(a) Somerset v Stewart, 1772 — RECOMMENDED OPENING CASE.** The brief's own first suggestion,
   and it earns the slot: a named, still-enslaved man, a named abolitionist who spent years
   engineering the test case, a judge who tried very hard not to make the ruling he ended up making,
   and — this is the payoff for the chapter's actual argument — a legend about what the ruling said
   that is **bigger than the ruling itself**. That gap between the legend ("slavery ended in England
   in 1772") and Mansfield's actual narrow holding (a master cannot forcibly remove a man from the
   kingdom for sale abroad; nothing more) is not a debunking footnote, it's the chapter's thesis in
   miniature: Mansfield drew the line as narrowly as he could, and the line did not hold — it kept
   eroding past the point he tried to fix it at, because a narrow ruling resting on "no positive law
   supports slavery here" left the actual boundary undefended and exploitable. Risk: the "air too
   pure" line is a live misattribution trap (see §a below) — the Drafter must not put it in
   Mansfield's mouth.

2. **(b) The erosion sequence as a case-set — STRONGEST SUPPORTING MATERIAL, use across the chapter
   rather than as the cold open.** Five documented boundary-redrawing moments, each anchored to a
   named person, tracking CANON §3's own list (property, all men, women, abolition, species). This
   is not a single scene the way Somerset is, but it is the material that lets the chapter show a
   *pattern* rather than one lucky anecdote — which is exactly what the instability argument needs
   to not look like cherry-picking. Recommend using two or three of the five inline as a rapid
   sequence after the opening case, not all five (audio pacing).

3. **(c) Bernard Rollin's documented veterinary-pain history, paired with the Jeffrey Lawson infant
   surgery case — RECOMMENDED SUBSTITUTE for the brief's "first veterinary anaesthesia debates."**
   Flag for Gate 1: this session could not find documentation of an early (18th/19th-century) named
   veterinary anaesthesia controversy that meets the chapter's evidentiary bar. What **is** solidly
   documented, and arguably serves the chapter's argument better, is a genuinely startling 20th-century
   case: American veterinary students were taught, as late as the 1980s, that animal pain responses
   were reflexive rather than felt, and — the harder-hitting parallel — American infants routinely
   underwent major surgery, including open-heart surgery, with paralytic agents but **no anaesthesia**,
   into the mid-1980s, on the same "not really felt" reasoning, until a single mother's public campaign
   after her son's 1985 surgery forced the reversal. This is a boundary drawn in the wrong place
   *within the species the axiom is supposedly least contested for*, which may be a stronger case for
   "arbitrary lines are unstable, not just cruel" than an animal case would be, precisely because it
   removes species membership as a variable. See risk note below on whether this substitution needs
   Showrunner sign-off given the brief specifically names "veterinary."

4. **(d) Singer's own conversion account — the Oxford lunch with Richard Keshen.** Solidly documented
   in Singer's own retellings (per Wikipedia's synthesis of his interviews and writing), vivid, short,
   personally embarrassing to Singer in exactly the way that makes a case memorable (he was the one
   still eating the meat sauce). Best used as connective tissue between the historical cases and the
   chapter's turn toward the philosophical argument — it's the one case that is explicitly about a
   line moving in an individual's head, which mirrors what the chapter wants the reader to do. Risk:
   this session verified the account only through a Wikipedia synthesis of Singer's own statements,
   not a primary Singer text read directly (Singer's own book-length account, in the preface to
   *Animal Liberation* or his "Intellectual Autobiography" essay, is referenced in the *Practical
   Ethics* bibliography — Files/ has *Practical Ethics* but the autobiographical essay itself was not
   located and read this session). Verifier should confirm exact wording against Singer's own text
   before the Drafter quotes him directly.

5. **(e) The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012 — RECOMMENDED AS THE CHAPTER'S CLOSING
   BEAT, not the opening case.** The only fully primary-sourced item in this packet (verified against
   the conference's own site, not a secondary summary), and it gives the chapter something the older
   cases can't: proof the boundary is still moving, in the reader's own adult lifetime, signed by
   named scientists in a named room with Stephen Hawking sitting in the audience. Strongest used to
   close the chapter's historical sweep — "this is not a story about the past" — rather than to open
   it, since it lacks a single protagonist to hang a cold open on.

**Overall recommendation for Gate 1:** open on Somerset (a), use two beats from the erosion sequence
(b) — recommend abolition (Wilberforce/Buxton, 1833) and the species line (Bentham via Singer, or
Martin's Act 1822) — as the rapid middle sequence, land the Rollin/Lawson pairing (c) as the chapter's
argumentative pivot into "and this keeps happening, inside the human boundary too," use Singer's own
conversion (d) as the bridge into the philosophical argument and the indexical-egoist interlocutor,
and close on Cambridge 2012 (e). This is more cases than a 4,500-word chapter can hold in full — the
Drafter should treat (b) as a reservoir to draw one or two beats from, not a mandate to use all five.

## (a) Somerset v Stewart, 1772

**What's documented about James Somerset himself — thin, and the packet should say so rather than
invent texture.** Somerset was an enslaved African man purchased in Virginia (some accounts say
Boston) by Charles Stewart, a Boston customs official, around 1749. Stewart brought him to England in
1769. In October 1771, Somerset escaped; he was recaptured in late November and imprisoned aboard the
ship *Ann and Mary*, under Captain John Knowles, bound for Jamaica to be resold. Three godparents from
Somerset's Christian baptism in England — John Marlow, Thomas Walkin, and Elizabeth Cade — applied for
a writ of habeas corpus on his behalf. That is close to the limit of what is independently documented
about Somerset as a person; nothing is reliably known about his life after the case. **Confidence:
REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED**, not primary-sourced this session — flag for the Verifier to check
against a legal history (e.g., Wiecek, or the Blackstone/Lofft law reports) rather than Wikipedia alone.

**Granville Sharp.** An abolitionist layman (not a lawyer) who had already spent years intervening in
similar cases and had published a legal tract, *A Representation of the Injustice and Dangerous
Tendency of Tolerating Slavery* (1769), arguing English law gave no support to slavery. He organized
and personally funded Somerset's legal team. This was a deliberately engineered test case, not a
chance dispute — Sharp had been looking for exactly this fact pattern.

**Charles Stewart** — the "villain" role, though a mundane one: a customs officer who, notably,
initially seemed willing to let the matter drop (Mansfield reportedly urged a private settlement) but
was pushed by fellow slaveowners and West India interests to see the case through, because they wanted
a precedent, not a favor. This detail is useful for the chapter: the boundary was being defended not
by Stewart's personal cruelty but by a coalition with a stake in the outcome — a small illustration of
Axis 2 ("circle failure" as coordination among the in-group) if the Drafter wants the connective tissue
to Ch. 5/6's diagnostic apparatus.

**Lord Mansfield (William Murray, 1st Earl of Mansfield) — the ruling, and its actual scope.**
Delivered **22 June 1772**. Mansfield tried repeatedly, before ruling, to get the parties to settle
rather than force a sweeping precedent — he is on record worrying aloud about the property
implications of a broad ruling (an estimated 14,000–15,000 enslaved people were then in England).
**The ruling itself was narrow.** Its holding, in Mansfield's own words as reported: *"The state of
slavery is of such a nature, that it is incapable of being introduced on any reasons, moral or
political, but only by positive law... it's so odious, that nothing can be suffered to support it, but
positive law... whatever inconveniences, therefore, may follow from a decision, I cannot say this
case is allowed or approved by the law of England; and therefore the black must be discharged."*
The narrow legal upshot, as most legal historians now read it: **a master cannot use force to compel
an enslaved person to leave England against his will for sale abroad.** Mansfield did **not** rule
that slavery itself was illegal in England, did not free every enslaved person in the country, and
did not create the sweeping precedent popular legend credits him with. Slavery persisted in fact in
England for years afterward; the legend that "Somerset ended slavery in England" outran the judgment
almost immediately and is itself a small case study in the chapter's own thesis — a boundary being
redrawn faster in public memory than the actual ruling licensed, because the public wanted the bigger
boundary redrawn. **Confidence on the exact wording: REPORTED, with a caveat that matters** — the
judgment was never formally published by Mansfield; it survives only through contemporaneous
third-party law reports (Lofft's and Howell's *State Trials*), which differ slightly in wording. The
Verifier should treat any quoted line from the judgment as needing cross-check against Lofft 1 or
Howell's *State Trials* vol. 20, not taken on secondary-source authority alone.

**The "air too pure" line — VERIFY CAREFULLY, this is the trap the brief flagged, and it is real.**
The famous line ("this air is too pure for a slave to breathe in") is **not from Mansfield's
judgment**. Per this session's research, it originates with **William Davy**, serjeant-at-law arguing
*for* Somerset, who invoked a much older precedent — the 1569 *Cartwright* case — in which (per the
legend Davy himself was citing) it was said "England was too pure an air for a slave to breathe in."
Legal historians dispute whether the *Cartwright* case record actually contains this wording at all;
it may be apocryphal even at that earlier remove. **The chapter must not put this line in Mansfield's
mouth or treat it as part of the judgment** — if used at all, it belongs to Davy's argument for the
plaintiff, cited from a two-centuries-earlier case, and should be flagged in text as folklore layered
on folklore, which is itself a nice illustration of how legends accrete around boundary-moving moments.
Some secondary sources attribute a similar line to Serjeant Hargrave (Somerset's other counsel) rather
than Davy — this session found convergent-but-not-identical attribution and could not fully resolve
Davy vs. Hargrave without the original trial transcript. **Flag for Verifier: resolve Davy vs.
Hargrave attribution against Howell's State Trials before the Drafter uses either name.**

**Fit:** excellent. Names, dates, a real courtroom, a real gap between what was decided and what
people needed it to have decided, and a documented misattribution trap that itself demonstrates the
chapter's point about how boundaries get renegotiated in the telling as much as in the ruling.

**Risks:** (1) the misattribution trap above — highest-priority fact-check before draft; (2) don't
overstate what Mansfield decided — the temptation to use the popular version ("slavery ended here")
is strong because it's a better story, and CANON's own instability argument does not need the
inflated version, it needs the *true* version (a deliberately narrow ruling that still couldn't hold
the line); (3) very little is recoverable about Somerset as a person, which the brief wants ("real
people... doing things") — the chapter has more to work with on Sharp and Mansfield than on Somerset
himself, and should not invent interiority for him.

## (b) The erosion sequence — one documented moment per boundary

CANON §3 names the sequence of boundaries that have eroded: tribe, race, nation, sex, species,
cognitive threshold. The brief's shorthand — property → all men → women → abolition → animals — maps
onto this. Below is one solidly documented, named-person moment per step. These are not meant to be
used all five in one chapter; they're a reservoir.

**Property → all men: France, 1848.** Under the property-qualified July Monarchy, roughly **1% of
the French population held the franchise**. The February 1848 revolution produced a provisional
government including **Alexandre Ledru-Rollin** (Minister of the Interior) and **Alphonse de
Lamartine**, which decreed universal manhood suffrage. **Confidence: REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE on
the exact date** — this session could not independently pin the specific March 1848 decree date
against a second source; flag for Verifier (commonly cited as 2 or 5 March 1848). The "1% of the
population" figure is corroborated. Fit: dramatic scale (roughly a hundred-fold jump in the
electorate almost overnight) but weaker on individual vividness — Ledru-Rollin is a name, not a
scene.

**All men → women: New Zealand, 1893.** The Electoral Bill received royal assent on **19 September
1893**, making New Zealand the first self-governing country where all women could vote in
parliamentary elections. **Kate Sheppard** led the Women's Christian Temperance Union petition
campaign that forced the issue: 1891 petition, over 9,000 signatures; 1892, nearly 20,000; 1893,
roughly 32,000 signatures — close to a quarter of the country's adult European female population.
Women first voted on 28 November 1893. **Confidence: SOLID**, cross-checked, primary-adjacent
(Wikipedia's account of the petition numbers is well-corroborated in NZ historiography generally).
Fit: strong — a named organizer, an escalating documented campaign with real numbers, a specific date.
Note for the chapter's UK material: the UK's own 1918 Representation of the People Act enfranchised
practically all men over 21 (removing the last property qualifications) *and* women over 30 in a
single act — useful if the Drafter wants "property" and "women" folded into one scene, with **Millicent
Fawcett** accepting the age-30 compromise while noting Britain "is governed not by logic, but by
Parliament" — a genuinely quotable line about the arbitrariness of where the new boundary got redrawn
even as the old one fell.

**Abolition: the Slavery Abolition Act, 1833.** Received royal assent **28 August 1833**; took effect
1 August 1834. **William Wilberforce** — who had campaigned for abolition for over four decades — died
on 29 July 1833, one week before the bill's second reading in the Commons; he lived to know it would
pass but not to see it pass. **Thomas Fowell Buxton** forced the government's hand by threatening to
introduce his own emancipation bill. **Thomas Clarkson**, the Anti-Slavery Society's longtime
campaigner, was also central. The act freed roughly 800,000 enslaved people across most of the
British Empire (explicitly excluding British India, which required separate 1843 legislation) — and
paid £20 million in compensation to slaveowners, none to the enslaved, with a 4–6 year "apprenticeship"
transition period. **Confidence: SOLID.** Fit: the Wilberforce death-timing detail is genuinely
striking (a man who spent his working life on one boundary, dying at the exact threshold of it moving)
but the chapter should resist romanticizing the act itself — the compensation-to-owners-only detail is
exactly the kind of "the boundary moved, but look who still got paid" fact that keeps the chapter
honest about how messily and self-interestedly these moves actually happen (useful ammunition for
§8 of `evidence.md`, on threats to the instability argument).

**Species: Jeremy Bentham, 1789, and/or Martin's Act, 1822.** Two candidate anchors. **(i)** Bentham's
own line, from *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), quoted directly
from Singer's *Practical Ethics* (Files/, read this session — **SOLID, verbatim**): *"The question is
not, Can they reason? nor Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"* — from a longer passage anticipating
that "the blackness of the skin is no reason why a human being should be abandoned... to the caprice
of a tormentor," and asking what should "trace the insuperable line" if not an equally arbitrary
feature like "the number of the legs, the villosity of the skin." This is the single cleanest textual
link between the race-boundary and species-boundary material in the whole packet — Bentham draws the
analogy himself, in 1789, which lets the chapter show the *pattern-recognition* was available a
half-century before any of it happened. **(ii)** **Martin's Act, 1822** — formally the Cruel
Treatment of Cattle Act, the world's first national animal-welfare legislation, championed by Irish
MP **Richard Martin** ("Humanity Dick"). Its first prosecution, against **Bill Burns** for beating a
donkey, became the world's first known conviction for animal cruelty — and Martin reportedly brought
the donkey itself into the courtroom as evidence. Vivid and small-scale; good contrast beat to
Bentham's abstract argument if the Drafter wants both. **Confidence: SOLID** on both.

## (c) Bernard Rollin's veterinary-pain history and the Jeffrey Lawson infant-surgery case

**What the brief asked for vs. what this session could source.** The brief's phrase is "the first
veterinary anaesthesia debates" — this session could not locate a documented, named, dated 18th- or
19th-century controversy over veterinary anaesthesia meeting the chapter's evidentiary bar (this may
exist in specialist veterinary-history literature not accessible via web search this session; flag
for a follow-up pass with library/journal access before concluding it doesn't exist). **What is
solidly documented, and may in fact serve the argument better, is a 20th-century case that is not
strictly "veterinary" but sits on exactly the same axis** — a boundary drawn around whose pain counts,
justified by the era's best available science, that then cracked.

**Bernard Rollin**, philosopher of veterinary ethics (author of *The Unheeded Cry: Animal
Consciousness, Animal Pain and Scientific Change*, 1989), documented that American veterinary
students **trained before roughly 1989 were taught that animal pain responses were reflexive rather
than subjectively felt**, and that this was standard veterinary-school teaching, not a fringe view.
Donald Griffin later coined the term "mentophobia" for the discipline-wide reluctance to attribute
inner experience to animals. **Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED** (converges across two
independent sources this session) but **not primary-sourced** — Rollin's own book is not in `Files/`
and was not read directly; flag as needing the physical text before any direct quotation is used.

**The stronger, better-documented parallel: Jeffrey Lawson, 1985.** From the 1880s through the 1980s,
mainstream American medicine held that **infants could not meaningfully feel pain**, an idea traceable
to a genuine misreading of 19th-century embryological research (Paul Flechsig's findings on
unmyelinated infant nerve fibers, wrongly read as evidence the infant nervous system couldn't
transmit pain signals). On this basis, infants — including newborns — routinely underwent major
surgery, up to and including open-heart surgery, with a paralytic to prevent movement but **no
anaesthesia or analgesia**. In 1985, **Jeffrey Lawson**, a premature infant, underwent open-heart
surgery on this basis; his mother, **Jill Lawson**, discovered afterward what had (and hadn't) been
administered and launched a public campaign that reached the medical press and general press. Combined
with **K.J.S. Anand's** published research demonstrating measurable hormonal and metabolic stress
responses to pain in neonates, medical consensus reversed within about two years — by 1987 the
practice was considered malpractice. **Confidence: SOLID on the broad shape** (cross-checked across
independent sources), **REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE on some specifics** (exact hospital, exact surgery
type) — flag for the Verifier to pin down a primary account (Jill Lawson's own 1986 *Birth* journal
letter, or Anand & Hickey's 1987 *NEJM* paper, would be the gold-standard sources; neither was read
directly this session).

**Fit:** this may be the single most argument-load-bearing case in the packet, because it removes
species as a variable entirely — the boundary being drawn in the wrong place here is *within* the set
of beings the axiom is least contested for (human infants), which pre-empts the reader's temptation to
think "well, animals are a harder case, humans obviously wouldn't get this wrong." The chapter's thesis
is that arbitrary lines erode wherever they're drawn, not just at the species boundary — this case
proves the mechanism operates even at the boundary readers assume is settled.

**Risks:** (1) this is a substitution for what the brief named, not what it asked for — flag
explicitly for Gate 1, since "veterinary" specifically is in the brief and infant surgery is not a
veterinary case at all; the Showrunner may prefer the Drafter keep looking for an actual veterinary
case, use this as a supplement rather than a replacement, or drop the "veterinary" framing entirely.
(2) The Rollin material is under-sourced this session (no primary text) and should not be quoted
directly until verified. (3) Genuinely upsetting material (infant surgery without anaesthesia) —
STYLE_BIBLE and VOICE should be checked for how the book wants to handle content at this register;
this is far more visceral than the Somerset case.

## (d) Singer's own conversion — the Oxford lunch with Richard Keshen

At Balliol College, Oxford, around 1970–71, Peter Singer had lunch with fellow graduate student
**Richard Keshen**. When Keshen chose the salad rather than the spaghetti after learning the sauce
contained meat, Singer asked why; Keshen gave a straightforward ethical objection to eating meat.
Singer's own recollection, per his later writing: *"I'd never met a vegetarian who gave such a
straightforward answer that I could understand and relate to."* Keshen introduced Singer to
vegetarian friends; Singer subsequently found and read Ruth Harrison's *Animal Machines*, and within
a week or two raised the question with his wife of whether they could keep justifying eating meat.
They became vegetarian in 1971. This predates and directly feeds into *Animal Liberation* (1975).
**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED** via a Wikipedia synthesis of Singer's own public
statements and interviews — **not verified against a primary Singer text read directly this session**.
Singer's fullest own telling is likely in his "An Intellectual Autobiography" essay (in Jeffrey
Schaler, ed., *Peter Singer Under Fire*, 2009), which is cited in the *Practical Ethics* bibliography
(Files/) but was not itself located or read this session. **Flag for Verifier: locate and confirm
exact wording against that essay, or against the preface to any edition of *Animal Liberation*,
before the Drafter quotes Singer directly.**

**Fit:** good — short, personally unflattering to Singer in the right way (he's the one still eating
the meat sauce, asking the question), and it's a case about a line moving inside one specific person's
head over roughly a week, which is a useful scale-shift after the historical/institutional cases.

**Risks:** sourcing tier is the main one — this is the weakest-verified case in the packet and needs
a Verifier pass before any direct quotation survives into the manuscript.

## (e) The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, 2012

Signed on the evening of **7 July 2012**, in the Balfour Room of the Hotel du Vin, Cambridge, UK, at
the close of the Francis Crick Memorial Conference on Consciousness in Human and Non-Human Animals.
**Stephen Hawking was present** at the signing. The declaration was written by **Philip Low** and
edited by **Jaak Panksepp, Diana Reiss, David Edelman, Bruno Van Swinderen**, and **Christof Koch**.
Its core claim: that "non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures,
including octopuses," possess the neurological substrates that generate consciousness, and that "the
absence of a neocortex does not appear to preclude an organism from experiencing affective states."
**Confidence: SOLID** — verified directly against the conference's own site (fcmconference.org), a
primary source, not a secondary summary. Note for completeness: a broader successor document, the
**New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness** (2024, NYU, 500+ signatories), extends a similar
claim tentatively to a much wider range of species including many invertebrates — useful if the
Drafter wants a second, even more recent data point showing the line is still moving now, in 2026.

**Fit:** the chapter's best "this isn't ancient history" beat — named scientists, a specific room, a
specific evening, a famous witness, and a text whose wording can be quoted exactly because it was read
from the primary source. Best used to close the chapter's historical sweep, not open it — it has no
single protagonist and works better as culmination than as a cold open.

**Risks:** minimal, given the primary sourcing — the only caution is not to overstate scientific
consensus beyond what the declaration itself claims (it is a declaration by conference attendees, not
a peer-reviewed finding with the evidentiary weight that implies, and it drew — as any
consciousness-related claim does — some pushback in the years after; the chapter should present it as
what it is: informed scientific opinion converging, not a settled experimental result).
