## 1. What CANON claims — restated, not re-derived

the rest of this packet's cross-references are legible): the derivation splits across two foundations.
The axiom (suffering of a conscious being matters) supplies the *currency*; the coordination layer

every non-universal line has eroded because none is a natural focal point and each is exploitable by
whoever has an interest in redrawing it.

has no obvious reason, on this argument alone, to converge on impartial norms.** POS-05 already logs

reciprocity-capable agents, and weaker for boundaries drawn against those who can never bargain at all.

## 2. Somerset v Stewart, 1772 — the ruling's exact scope and the "air too pure" misattribution

See `cases.md` §a for full narrative build. Summary for the evidence record:

**Confidence: REPORTED, with a documented sourcing caveat that the Verifier must not skip.** The
judgment was never formally published by Mansfield; it survives only in third-party law reports
(Lofft's report, and Howell's *State Trials* vol. 20), which vary slightly in wording. This session's
quoted line — *"The state of slavery is of such a nature, that it is incapable of being introduced on
any reasons, moral or political, but only by positive law... the black must be discharged"* — is
consistent across the secondary sources checked this session but was not cross-checked against Lofft
or Howell directly. **The actual legal holding is narrow**: a master may not use force to remove an
enslaved person from England for sale abroad. It is **not** a ruling that slavery was illegal in
England generally, and slavery continued in fact in England for years afterward. The popular claim
("Somerset ended slavery in England") is a legend that outran the judgment almost immediately.

**The "air too pure" line: verified as a misattribution, per this session's sourcing.** It does not
appear in Mansfield's judgment. It traces to **William Davy**, serjeant-at-law arguing for Somerset,
who invoked the 1569 *Cartwright* case as the source of the phrase — meaning even Davy was quoting an
(possibly apocryphal) older precedent, not coining the line himself. Some secondary sources instead
credit Hargrave (Somerset's other counsel) — this session found convergent-but-not-identical
attribution between Davy and Hargrave and could not fully resolve it without the trial transcript.
**Action item for the Drafter and Verifier: do not attribute this line to Mansfield under any
circumstances; resolve Davy-vs-Hargrave before quoting either by name.**

## 3. The indexical egoist's strongest form — and a model of NOT shifting the burden

This is the packet's highest-priority find. The brief says: "Give this its strongest form; it's the
objection the prior draft ducked," and CANON bans the burden-shifting move outright (§9 item 5). Singer's
*Practical Ethics* (Files/, read directly, **SOLID**) contains a passage where Singer works through
exactly this question — is the egoist irrational? — using Sidgwick, Nagel, and Parfit, and **explicitly
declines to call the egoist irrational.** This is worth quoting at length because it is a working
example of the correct move, not just an assertion that the move exists.

**Sidgwick's own formulation, quoted directly by Singer (verbatim, SOLID):**

> "It would be contrary to Common Sense to deny that the distinction between any one individual and any
> other is real and fundamental, and that consequently 'I' am concerned with the quality of my existence
> as an individual in a sense, fundamentally important, in which I am not concerned with the quality of
> the existence of other individuals: and this being so, I do not see how it can be proved that this
> distinction is not to be taken as fundamental in determining the ultimate end of rational action for an
> individual."

**Singer's own gloss, immediately following (verbatim):** Nagel had attempted, in *The Possibility of
Altruism*, an argument for the rationality of altruism structurally parallel to the argument for
temporal neutrality (treating "seeing oneself as merely one person among others" the way we treat
"seeing the present as merely one time among others"). Singer reports this attempt **failed**, and that
**Nagel himself no longer holds that it succeeds.** Singer's conclusion, stated in his own voice: *"Nagel
no longer holds that the argument in* The Possibility of Altruism *succeeds, and Parfit is largely in
agreement with Sidgwick about the rationality of acting to further one's own interests, even when this
is contrary to the greater interests of others."* And, a few pages later, discussing his own rejection
of Nagel's rationality-of-altruism argument: *"there is nothing irrational about being concerned with
the quality of one's own existence in a way that one is not concerned with the quality of existence of
other individuals... those who do not take part in [the move to the ethical point of view] — or who, in
taking part, do not follow it all the way to the ethical point of view — are not irrational or in
error."*

**Why this matters for the chapter specifically:** Singer — the philosopher whose whole career runs on
getting readers to expand the circle — concedes in his own book that the egoist who declines to make the
move is not making a reasoning error. He does not try to prove the egoist wrong. He offers, instead, a
different kind of consideration (the "point of view of the universe," borrowed explicitly from Sidgwick,
as something reflective people may find themselves drawn toward, not something reason compels). **This
is close to CANON's own architecture** — the axiom is chosen, not derived, and impartiality comes from
the coordination layer, not from proving the egoist self-contradictory. The Drafter has a genuine,
citable precedent for doing this correctly instead of inventing the move from scratch.

**Parfit's own working of the same problem, from *Reasons and Persons* §54–56 (Files/, read directly,
SOLID — verbatim quote of Sidgwick, independently corroborating the passage Singer also quotes from a
different angle):**

> "From the point of view, indeed, of abstract philosophy I do not see why the Egoistic Principle should
> pass unchallenged any more than the Universalistic. I do not see why the axiom of Prudence should not
> be challenged, when it conflicts with present inclination, on a ground similar to that on which
> Egoists refuse to admit the axiom of Rational Benevolence. If the Utilitarian has to answer the
> question, 'Why should I sacrifice my own happiness for the greater happiness of another?', it must
> surely be admissible to ask the Egoist, 'Why should I sacrifice a present pleasure for a greater one in
> the future? Why should I concern myself about my own future feelings any more than about the feelings
> of other persons?'"

Parfit's own move (§55, "How S Is Incompletely Relative") is **not** to declare the egoist irrational
either. It's to point out that the self-interest theory (S) is a *hybrid* — it rejects impartiality
between persons but insists on impartiality between the different time-slices of one life — and to ask
why, if the agent may privilege himself over others, he may not equally privilege the present moment
over his own future. Parfit's argument is that **consistent partiality collapses toward the
Present-aim Theory** (care only about right now) while **consistent impartiality collapses toward full
neutrality** — the self-interest theory sits at an unstable midpoint between two purer positions.
**This is structurally close to CANON's Schelling-point language** (an unmotivated midpoint, not a
natural resting place) and is a genuinely different argument from the coordination-game instability
argument CANON runs at the interpersonal level — worth flagging to the Referee as a possible second,
complementary instability argument (temporal rather than interpersonal) that the chapter could
gesture at without fully absorbing, since developing it properly is outside this chapter's scope.

**Confidence: SOLID for both quoted passages** (read directly from PDFs in `Files/`). **Citation
caveat:** the exact original-source citation (which page of which edition of Sidgwick's *Methods of
Ethics* the dualism passage appears on) could not be pinned down from Parfit's endnotes this session —
Parfit's bibliography confirms he used the 1907 (7th) edition, and SEP independently cites the "Chaos"
formulation of the dualism to "1874 [1907: 473]" (the concluding chapter), but this session did not
confirm those are the same page reference as the passage quoted above. **Flag for Verifier: resolve
exact page citation against a physical or full-text copy of the 1907 *Methods of Ethics* before the
manuscript cites a page number.**

**Modern formulation matching the brief's exact phrasing** ("your pain is bad-for-you, mine is
bad-for-me, there is no view from nowhere"): per SEP's entry on egoism (secondary, REPORTED), this maps
onto the agent-relative-reasons literature associated with **Thomas Nagel** (*The View from Nowhere*,
1986) and **Jesse Kalin** (1970), who argued rational egoism cannot be dismissed as arbitrary merely
because it privileges one's own welfare. Nagel's own later position, per Singer's account above, is
that his attempt to show altruism is rationally required (in the earlier *Possibility of Altruism*)
did not succeed — i.e., **the philosopher most associated with the "view from nowhere" phrase himself
concluded the indexical egoist cannot be argued out of the position on pure rationality grounds.** This
is exactly the concession CANON's own architecture is built to not need — the impartiality comes from
the coordination layer, not from convicting the egoist of an error in reasoning.

## 4. Binmore's empathetic-preferences / naturalized-original-position machinery

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED, not primary-sourced.** *Natural Justice* (2005) is not in
`Files/`; this session's account comes from Wikipedia's summary, cross-checked against a passing
mention in the SEP contractarianism entry. Binmore's project: a "naturalistic reinterpretation of
Rawls's original position that reconciles his egalitarian theory of justice with Harsanyi's utilitarian
theory," grounding fairness norms in biological and social evolution rather than a priori argument —
building on his earlier two-volume *Game Theory and the Social Contract*. SEP independently confirms:
"Binmore (1998, 2005) claims to be able to show, in a robust and non-question-begging way, that
something very much like Rawls's 'justice as fairness' will be the result of this evolutionary
bargaining process." Neither source explains, at the depth this session could access, the mechanics of
how "empathetic preferences" (an agent modeling what it would be like to be in another's position,
weighted by some interpersonal comparison) actually generates the bargaining solution, nor how Binmore
specifically answers Gauthier's problem. **This is a real gap** — the brief names Binmore as a core
source and this packet cannot currently back that up with primary material. **Flag as the packet's
single largest sourcing hole; strongly recommend the physical book (or a detailed review/summary
article) before the Drafter leans on Binmore's specific mechanism rather than gesturing at it generally.**

**On the Gauthier problem specifically:** POS-04 already states the vulnerability precisely — CANON
claims its version "avoids Gauthier's fatal problem, because the base is value rather than preference
satisfaction," but POS-04's own known-vulnerability note calls this "Gauthier's own unresolved 'why
would the powerful bargain' problem, restated rather than dissolved by moving from preferences to
value." This session found nothing in the Binmore secondary material that resolves this restatement —
SEP's summary of Binmore's claim is that evolutionary bargaining produces something "very much like"
Rawlsian fairness, which is a claim about what evolution in fact tends to produce, not a proof that a
durably-advantaged agent has principled reason to expect its advantage won't last. **This is the same
gap POS-04 already logs. This packet did not find material that closes it — flag for the Referee as an
open question rather than a solved one.**

## 6. Singer's expanding-circle thesis and the Lecky attribution

**The thesis**, per a secondary (Wikipedia) summary of *The Expanding Circle* (1981; not in `Files/`,
not read directly — **REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE for the book's structure**): moral consideration has
historically widened from self/family to tribe, nation, race, and all humanity; the same logic extends
it to animals capable of suffering; human reason "generalizes or universalizes" natural altruistic
impulses beyond kin groups. This is CANON §3's boundary-erosion claim in an earlier, non-game-
theoretic form — Singer's version of "why" the circle expands leans on evolved altruism plus reason's
generalizing tendency, not on Binmore/Gauthier-style equilibrium instability. **This is a genuine
difference worth the Referee's attention**: CANON's mechanism (unstable equilibria under coordination
pressure) is not the same mechanism as Singer's (reason generalizing sentiment), even though both
predict the same historical pattern. The chapter should be clear about which mechanism it's claiming,
since Singer's own account doesn't actually support the specifically game-theoretic version of the
claim — it's a different argument that happens to agree on the observed pattern.

**The Lecky attribution — the "expanding circle" image itself.** This session located, via a direct
Project Gutenberg full-text fetch of Lecky's *History of European Morals from Augustus to Charlemagne*
(1869), a passage in Chapter I ("The Natural History of Morals") describing moral sentiment widening
outward: *"the infant learns to associate its pleasures with the idea of its mother, the boy with the
idea of his family, the man with those of his class, his church, his country, and at last of all
mankind."* This passage is real and was read directly (**SOLID** for this specific sentence). **A
second, more famous version of the same idea — using the literal word "circle" and explicitly
extending to animals** ("At one time the benevolent affections embrace merely the family, soon the
circle expanding includes first a class, then a nation, then a coalition of nations, then all humanity,
and finally, its influence is felt in the dealings of man with the animal world") — is **widely and
consistently attributed to Lecky's same book across multiple independent secondary sources this session
cross-checked (REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED)**, but this session's own direct full-text search of the
Gutenberg edition did not surface that exact "circle expanding... animal world" wording in the portion
of text searched. **This is most likely a tool/search-coverage limitation** (the automated full-text
search may not have covered the entire multi-hundred-page volume, or the passage may be in Volume 2,
which was not fetched) **rather than evidence the quote is spurious** — the cross-source convergence on
identical wording is a strong signal it's genuine. **Flag for Verifier: confirm the exact "circle
expanding... animal world" passage against a physical copy or a complete full-text search of both
volumes before the chapter quotes it as Lecky's, since this session's primary-source check was
incomplete rather than negative.** Whether Singer's book explicitly credits Lecky as the source of his
title's image could not be confirmed this session either way — not found, not ruled out.

## 8. The game-theoretic instability claim — what supports it, and what threatens it

**This is CANON's own argument (POS-05), not an external claim being verified — the packet's job here
is adversarial: find what a hostile Referee would find first.**

### What supports it (already covered above, summarized)

The erosion sequence in `cases.md` §b is real, cross-checked, and covers five independent domains
(property, sex, race/abolition, species) each eroding under pressure from a different direction, with
named people at each seam. That breadth is itself evidence for *something* systematic going on, though
not by itself evidence for the specific *mechanism* (equilibrium instability) CANON claims explains it.

### Threat 1: a boundary that held for centuries — the caste system

The Indian caste system persisted, on a naive reading, for **over 2,000 years** as a stable social
hierarchy — per historian A.L. Basham (cited on Wikipedia, **REPORTED**): "the four great classes are
stable. There are never more or less than four and for over 2,000 years their order of precedence has
not altered." On its face this is a long-lived arbitrary boundary that did **not** erode under its own
weight, which is a direct counterexample to POS-05's claim that arbitrary boundaries are unstable
equilibria.

**But the sourcing here cuts both ways, and the complication is itself useful, not just a threat.**
Per the same Wikipedia synthesis of more recent scholarship (Susan Bayly, Nicholas Dirks —
**REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED**): caste in practice was considerably more fluid pre-colonially than the
"2,000 years unchanged" reading suggests, and the rigid, sharply-bounded modern form is substantially a
product of the **post-Mughal period (1700–1850) and British colonial administrative categorization** —
i.e., the boundary got *more* rigid, not less, under a specific historical actor with an interest in
legible, administrable categories. **This cuts two ways for the chapter's argument:**

- **As a threat**, it's a genuine multi-century case of a hierarchical boundary that did not erode on
  its own — the Referee will find this if the Drafter doesn't get there first.
- **As a partial defense**, the colonial-rigidification story is arguably a textbook instance of
  exactly the vulnerability POS-04 already logs: a boundary imposed and *hardened* by an agent (the
  colonial administration) that believed itself durably advantaged and had no expectation of ever
  being on the excluded side of the line it was drawing. POS-04's own "what would change my mind"
  criterion is a demonstration that even durably-advantaged agents have principled reason for
  uncertainty about their future position — **the caste case, read this way, is not a counterexample
  to the instability claim so much as a worked example of the exact condition (confident, durable
  advantage) under which CANON's own logic predicts a boundary can hold.** That is a genuine, honest
  answer — but it requires CANON to bite the bullet on POS-04's vulnerability being real and
  load-bearing, not just a footnote. **Flag for the Canon Keeper: this may be the strongest available
  argument for Ch. 6, but it only works if the chapter is explicit that its own mechanism predicts
  exactly the cases where boundaries persist, not just the cases where they erode — a falsifiable,
  two-sided claim rather than a one-sided "everything erodes eventually" claim.**

### Threat 2: erosion tracks economics/power, not equilibrium logic

The **Williams thesis** (Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery*, 1944) argues British abolition was
driven by the declining profitability of West Indian sugar plantations, not moral sentiment — an
economic-determinist account. **Seymour Drescher's** *Econocide* (1977) is the standard counter,
arguing abolition happened *despite* continued profitability, driven by genuine public moral outrage.
**This is a live, unresolved historiographical dispute** (**CONTESTED**, per this session's sourcing):
Engerman's and Richardson's calculations suggest slavery was a small fraction of British economic
activity by the abolition era (supporting Drescher — moral sentiment, not economic necessity, did the
work); Ryden's 2009 data suggests a genuine early-19th-century profitability crisis (supporting
Williams). **If Williams (or some version of the economic account) is closer to right, the historical
erosion CANON leans on tracked material self-interest, not a game-theoretic instability in the boundary
itself** — a different causal story that happens to produce the same historical pattern the chapter
wants to explain. The UK's 1918 Representation of the People Act supplies an adjacent version of the
same threat: it was substantially framed and justified, at the time, as recognition of wartime labor
and service (a power/leverage story — women's wartime contribution bought them a seat at the table),
not as the resolution of a previously-unstable equilibrium argument. **This is the packet's second
major threat and probably the harder one to answer**, because it doesn't require reinterpreting a
single hard case (as the caste example does) — it's a standing alternative causal mechanism for the
entire dataset the chapter relies on. **Flag for the Referee explicitly: CANON needs either (a) an
argument for why the equilibrium-instability mechanism and the economics/power mechanism are not
actually competitors — e.g., that shifting economic incentives are themselves partly downstream of the
same coordination pressures — or (b) a concession that the historical record underdetermines which
mechanism is doing the work, with the chapter's confidence calibrated accordingly (POS-05 is currently
rated STRONG; this threat, if unanswered, may argue for dialing that back toward MODERATE).**

### Threat 3 (minor, flagged for completeness): POS-05's own logged vulnerability about non-reciprocal beings

Already on record in `POSITIONS.md` — the equilibrium-selection logic is strongest for boundaries drawn
among agents capable of threatening, bargaining, or coalition-shifting, and weaker for boundaries drawn
against beings (infants, animals, future people) who can never do any of those things. This packet did
not find new material that resolves this; it is the same gap POS-05 already names as needing "a version
of the argument that shows instability even for boundaries drawn against permanently non-reciprocal
beings." Worth the chapter addressing directly rather than leaving implicit, since it is precisely the
class of beings the animal and infant cases in `cases.md` are about.
