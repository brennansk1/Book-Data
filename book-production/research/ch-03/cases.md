## PART ONE — The opening case: the Tribunal de las Aguas, Valencia

### What Ostrom actually documents (the primary-source spine of the case)

**The founding scene the brief wants, and it's real:** On **May 29, 1435** — about fifty years
before the much-cited Swiss village of Törbel signed its own formal articles of association —
**84 irrigators** served by the Benàcher and Faitanar canals near Valencia gathered at **the
monastery of St. Francis** to draw up and approve formal written regulations: who had rights to
water, how it would be shared in good years and bad, how maintenance would be shared, what
officials would be elected and how, and what fines applied to rule-breakers (Ostrom, p. 69).
This is a genuine documentary event with a specific date, place, and headcount — usable as a
scene, not a legend.

**The deeper history behind that 1435 meeting:** the canals themselves, "like many others in
the region, had been constructed in even earlier times." Valencia was "recaptured from the
Muslims in 1238" — two centuries before the Benàcher/Faitanar meeting — and "some of the rules
carried into medieval and modern practice were developed prior to that reconquest." Ostrom's
own summary sentence: **"Thus, for at least 550 years, and probably for close to 1,000 years,
farmers have continued to meet with others sharing the same canals for the purpose of
specifying and revising the rules that they use, selecting officials, and determining fines and
assessments"** (p. 69). Note Ostrom's own hedge — "at least 550 years" is the documented floor;
"probably... close to 1,000 years" is her own informed extrapolation, not a claim she treats as
settled fact. The chapter should use this exact asymmetry rather than flattening it into a bare
"1,000 years" claim.

**The Tribunal itself — the core passage, verbatim from the primary text (p. 71):**
> "In Valencia, the irrigators from seven of the major canals are organized into autonomous
> irrigation communities whose syndic, or chief executive, participates in two weekly
> tribunals. The Tribunal de las Aguas is a water court that has for centuries met on Thursday
> mornings outside the Apostles' Door of the Cathedral of Valencia. The many Islamic features of
> its traditions have led scholars to argue that the court evolved during the period of Islamic
> rule. Its proceedings are carried on without lawyers, but with many onlookers."

Three load-bearing corrections to the brief's assumptions, all found in Ostrom's own primary
text and resolved against a live web-verification pass this session (see "Legend vs. documented"
below and `evidence.md` §5 for full sourcing and a summary table):

1. **Noon, not "mornings" — use the current, well-documented figure.** Ostrom says "mornings"
   twice (also true of Murcia's parallel court, p. 77: "The Murcian court meets every Thursday
   morning in the City Hall"), but every current source (the Tribunal's own site, Visit
   Valencia, the Comunitat Valenciana tourism board) confirms the court convenes at **noon**,
   when the Cathedral's Micalet bell tolls twelve. This isn't really a contradiction — noon falls
   at the boundary of "morning" — but the chapter should use "noon," which is both more precise
   and more vivid (a bell, not just a time of day).
2. **"Apostles' Door," not "Gate."** Ostrom's own translation is "Door" (Puerta de los
   Apóstoles). "Gate" is not wrong in spirit but isn't Ostrom's wording; use "Door" if quoting
   her, and check the Spanish original if the Drafter wants the more common English rendering.
3. **Eight seats today; Ostrom's "seven" is a real historical data point, not an error.**
   Ostrom's own text says the Turia River is "divided into eight major canals serving the
   16,000-hectare huerta" (p. 71, opening sentence of the Valencia section) but then says "the
   irrigators from **seven** of the major canals are organized into autonomous irrigation
   communities" that sit on the Tribunal (same page, next paragraph) — read twice against the
   extraction, this is genuinely what her primary text says, not an OCR artifact. The
   web-verification pass confirms the modern, standard figure is **eight syndics representing
   nine underlying communities** (right bank: Quart, Benàger i Faitanar, Mislata, Favara,
   Rovella; left bank: Tormos, Mestalla, Rascanya), and turned up a plausible (though not
   archivally pinned down this session) explanation: the Benàger-Faitanar canal — the same one
   from the 1435 founding scene above — appears to have split off from Quart's community to
   become its own seat at some point, which would make Ostrom's 1990 "seven" an accurate
   snapshot of an earlier organizational state rather than a mistake. **Use "eight" for the
   chapter's present-tense description of the Tribunal; Ostrom's "seven" belongs in a footnote as
   a historical data point, not silently corrected or silently used as the headline number.**

**How a dispute actually runs, per Ostrom (pp. 71–72, 73–74):**
- No lawyers. Many onlookers. A presiding officer questions those involved in the dispute and
  anyone else who can add information.
- The court members — excluding the syndic whose own canal is involved, a real recusal rule —
  "make an immediate decision regarding the facts of the case in light of the specific rules of
  the particular canal." Fines and damages follow the rules of that particular canal, not a
  single Tribunal-wide code — each of the seven/eight communities keeps its own rules.
- **"The final decisions of the court are recorded, but not the proceedings"** (p. 72). This is
  the single most important correction to the brief's "no written records" framing — see
  "Legend vs. documented," point 4, below.
- A separate second weekly tribunal, convened by the syndics themselves (minus the disputants),
  coordinates cross-canal issues like seasonal low-water procedures.
- Fines were real but deliberately small. From the fine books of the similarly structured
  Castellón huerta (Glick 1970, cited by Ostrom, pp. 74–75 — a close analogue to Valencia, not
  Valencia's own huerta directly; see the flag in `evidence.md`): 441 fines assessed in 1443,
  499 in 1486, "more than one per day." **Actual fines were kept deliberately low** — "a few
  pennies at the most" — because "the guards did not deeply antagonize the farmers, who
  generally adhered to the rules" (Glick 1970, p. 56, quoted in Ostrom p. 75). The syndic kept
  two-thirds of each fine, the accuser one-third — a built-in incentive to bring accusations
  without making punishment so harsh that it created lasting grudges.
- The "watching each other" mechanism (Figure 3.2 in Ostrom, p. 74, a diagram of "reciprocal
  monitoring relationships"): irrigators waiting their turn on the canal have nothing to do but
  watch the ditch-riders and the farmers ahead of them — monitoring is a free byproduct of
  people just standing around waiting for their own water, not a costly separate enforcement
  apparatus. This is the case's best illustration of Ostrom's Design Principle 4 (monitoring)
  and belongs in the chapter's monitoring beat regardless of which three principles are
  ultimately featured.

**The scale of what's being governed (p. 71):** the Turia River's water serves a 16,000-hectare
huerta; farms are small and fragmented — over 80% are less than 1 hectare, few exceed 5. Water
right is not a separate commodity; it "inheres in the land itself." This is useful texture: the
Tribunal isn't managing an abstraction, it's managing which specific farmer's specific field
gets water this week, in a semi-arid region where, per Ostrom, "water was never abundant... not
even after major canals were constructed," and "conflict over water has always been just
beneath the surface of everyday life" (p. 69).

**The Aragon/Castile political-autonomy point (p. 81), useful for the chapter's "neither
markets nor states" argument:** the huertas' self-governing autonomy was not typical of Spain as
a whole. The medieval kingdom of Aragon (Valencia, Aragon, Catalonia) had, by the late 13th
century, "already secured the power to legislate and even to limit the king's power to issue
legislation under certain conditions" (Veliz 1980, p. 34, quoted in Ostrom), while Castile's
parallel institution (the cortes) was rarely even summoned. Ostrom notes this is *why* the
huertas' self-governance survived: they existed inside a polity that had already accepted
constrained, non-absolute royal power, not despite the surrounding state. This is a genuinely
useful complication for the "neither markets nor states" argument — the Tribunal's survival
wasn't purely bottom-up; it needed a permissive, decentralized *political* environment above it
to avoid being extinguished, which is itself an early version of the scale/nesting concession
the chapter has to make anyway (see below, and Design Principle 8).

**Ostrom then arriving to study it — for the "then Ostrom arriving" framing the brief wants:**
Ostrom's own account (p. 70) credits Arthur Maass and Raymond L. Anderson, *...And the Desert
Shall Rejoice: Conflict, Growth, and Justice in Arid Environments* (MIT Press, 1978; cited by
Ostrom throughout as "Maass and Anderson 1986," apparently a later edition/printing — **flag for
Verifier to confirm the correct publication year**, as Ostrom's own in-text citations
consistently read "1986" while the commonly catalogued original edition is 1978) as having
"devoted much effort to studying the institutions" of the Spanish huertas, and Thomas F. Glick's
*Irrigation and Society in Medieval Valencia* (Harvard University Press, 1970) as "an
authoritative study of the huerta of Valencia during the Middle Ages" (p. 71). **Ostrom herself
did not conduct original fieldwork in Valencia** — she is synthesizing Maass/Anderson's and
Glick's prior scholarship into her comparative framework, alongside her own team's fieldwork on
other cases (Alanya, the Swiss/Japanese commons, the California groundwater basins). This is
worth being precise about in the chapter: the "then Ostrom arriving to study it" beat is true in
the sense that *Governing the Commons* is where this case entered the general theoretical
literature and the reason a book like this one can point to it at all, but it should not be
staged as Ostrom personally showing up in Valencia with a notebook — that's not what happened,
and the book's own epistemic-honesty thesis means this distinction matters.

## Legend vs. documented — the required flag

The brief asks explicitly: "how long has it really operated (claims range from Roman to Moorish
origins — flag what's actually documented vs. legend)." Findings, combining Ostrom's primary
text with a live web-verification pass this session (full sourcing and a summary table in
`evidence.md` §5):

1. **Roman origin claims: legend, not documented — confirmed by the Tribunal's own materials.**
   Ostrom doesn't mention a Roman origin at all. The Tribunal's own official history page hedges
   explicitly: some water-dispute institution "would already exist from Roman times," but "the
   organization which we have inherited dates from times of Al-Andalus." No archaeological or
   documentary record ties a continuous institution back to Rome. **Treat as legend/unproven
   antecedent** — even the institution's own promotional materials don't claim it as fact.
2. **Moorish/Islamic origin: civic tradition, not archival fact — but Ostrom's own hedge (p. 71)
   is already the right level of confidence.** The commonly cited ~960 CE founding date (under
   Caliph Abd al-Rahman III) is widely repeated — Valencia even held a civic "millennial
   anniversary" commemoration in 1960 built around it, which is itself a sign the date functions
   as commemorative tradition rather than documented fact. No contemporary 10th-century document
   supports it. Ostrom's own formulation — "the many Islamic features of its traditions have led
   **scholars to argue** that the court evolved during the period of Islamic rule" (p. 71,
   emphasis added) — is a citable, already-calibrated way to state this: a structural/comparative
   scholarly argument, not a founding-charter claim. **Use Ostrom's hedge, not the flat "founded
   in 960" popular version.**
3. **The documented floor: 13th–14th century, confirmed independently of Ostrom.** Ostrom's own
   floor is the 1435 Benàcher/Faitanar articles (above), with the 1238 reconquest as the outer
   bound of what she treats as reasonably inferable ("some of the rules carried into medieval and
   modern practice were developed prior to that reconquest"). The web-verification pass
   independently confirms no documentary evidence predates the 13th century, and surfaces one
   additional, earlier anchor point Ostrom doesn't mention: **a 1321 royal charter from King
   Jaume II** confirming Turia water-distribution rights, cited by the Tribunal itself and by
   Visit Valencia. Ostrom's "close to 1,000 years" is *her own* extrapolation stated as a
   probability, not a documented certainty — the chapter should preserve that hedge rather than
   upgrading it to a flat "a thousand years" claim. This is exactly the kind of calibration
   distinction `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` and `CANON.md` require.
4. **"No written records" — Ostrom's own framing turns out to be the correct one; the flatter
   popular claim is what needs correcting.** Ostrom's text already says the court's **decisions
   are recorded, but not the proceedings** (p. 72) — and the fine books Glick used for his 1970
   study (Ostrom pp. 74–75, discussing the closely analogous Castellón huerta) are themselves
   surviving 15th-century written archival records of individual fines. The web-verification pass
   confirms and sharpens this: the Tribunal's own archive holds a 19th-century "book of
   judgments" and 20th-century case-record minute books, consultable today. What's genuinely
   oral/unwritten is (a) the proceedings/arguments themselves — no transcript then or now — and
   (b) the substantive canal-by-canal customary rules, which the Tribunal's own site says weren't
   committed to writing until "the beginning of the 18th century." **The chapter's framing should
   be "no lawyers, no transcript of the arguments" — Ostrom's own precise wording — rather than
   the broader, inaccurate "no written records" full stop.** A referee in Spanish legal history
   would catch the flat version immediately; Ostrom's own sentence already avoids the trap.
5. **Black smocks — real, but the word "robes" needs a qualifier, not a bare use.** The
   Tribunal's own site and Visit Valencia both confirm the eight syndics wear traditional black
   Huerta farmer's work blouses (*blusa*) — described by the Tribunal itself as worn "with great
   dignity like a magistrate's robe," and by Smithsonian Magazine as syndics "dressed head to toe
   in black." It's real, it's documented by the institution's own materials, and it's a better
   detail than a costume would be: eight men judging in the same rough clothes they farm in, not
   borrowed authority. **Recommended phrasing: "black smocks — the same clothes any Huerta farmer
   wore into his own fields" rather than "black robes" unqualified**, which overclaims formal
   judicial dress.
6. **Current canal/community count: eight, not seven — but Ostrom's "seven" isn't simply wrong.**
   The modern, standard figure (Tribunal's own site, UNESCO's nomination text, Spain's Ministry
   of Culture, University of Valencia's heritage catalogue) is **eight syndics representing nine
   underlying communities** (right bank: Quart, Benàger i Faitanar, Mislata, Favara, Rovella;
   left bank: Tormos, Mestalla, Rascanya — one seat evidently covers two historically distinct
   communities). A plausible (though not primary-document-confirmed this session) explanation:
   the Acequia de Benàger-Faitanar split off from Quart's community to become its own represented
   seat at some point after Ostrom's count — which would make her 1990 "seven" an accurate
   snapshot of an earlier organizational state, not an error. **Use "eight" (or "eight seats,
   nine communities" if being precise) for the chapter's present-tense description of the
   Tribunal, and treat Ostrom's "seven" as a historical data point worth a footnote, not a
   contradiction to silently paper over.**
7. **UNESCO listing: confirmed.** Inscribed 2009 (specifically September 30, 2009) on the
   Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, under the joint title
   "Irrigators' Tribunals of the Spanish Mediterranean Coast: the Council of Wise Men of the
   Plain of Murcia and the Water Tribunal of the Plain of Valencia" (UNESCO ref. RL/00171) — a
   **joint listing with Murcia's water court**, not Valencia alone. This nicely echoes something
   Ostrom's own primary text already does independently: her account of Murcia's water court
   (p. 77, "the fortuitous name of the Council of Good Men... The Murcian court meets every
   Thursday morning in the City Hall... 'oral, public, summary, and cheap'") pairs the two courts
   on the same grounds UNESCO later used, decades before the joint listing existed.

## PART TWO — The documented failure case (required for honesty)

Per the brief: "a documented Ostrom-style failure." I evaluated the two candidates in Ostrom's
own Chapter 5 ("Analyzing institutional failures and fragilities," pp. 142–182) that best fit —
**Bodrum and the Bay of Izmir** (Turkish inshore fisheries, pp. 143–146) and **Mawelle** (a Sri
Lankan beach-seine fishery, pp. 149–156) — plus noted the San Bernardino County groundwater and
Mojave Water Agency case (pp. 145–148) as a third, less narratively useful option. Full
comparative table sourcing (Ostrom's own Table 5.2, p. 179 — which of the seven principles each
failure case does and doesn't meet) is in `evidence.md` §4.

### RECOMMENDED: Mawelle, Sri Lanka (Ostrom pp. 149–156)

**Why this is the better failure case for this specific chapter, not just a failure case in
general:** Bodrum and the Bay of Izmir are failures of *design* — per Ostrom's own Table 5.2
(p. 179), "none of the principles characterize" either fishery; the community never built
working rules at all. That's a clean illustration of "commons without governance fails," but it
doesn't complicate the chapter's actual argument. **Mawelle is a failure of a community that
*did* build the rules correctly and had them destroyed by exactly the kind of external political
capture that Design Principle 7 ("minimal recognition of rights to organize... not challenged by
external governmental authorities") is meant to name.** That's a sharper, more honest failure
case for a chapter whose argument is "here are three of the conditions under which self-
governance works" — it shows a case that met the *other* principles and still failed, because
the missing seventh principle wasn't cosmetic.

**The case, built out:**
- Mawelle, a fishing village at the southern tip of Sri Lanka, ~300 Sinhalese fishers, mostly of
  the Karave caste, using half-mile-long beach seine nets (*madelia*) that could only be
  launched from two specific spots on the beach (p. 149).
- The fishers had devised **"quite elaborate rules regulating access to the beach and the use of
  the beach seines"** (p. 150) — a genuinely sophisticated sequencing system (Ostrom's Table 5.1,
  p. 151, shows the actual weekly rotation) that balanced four real environmental/economic
  constraints (which launch site produces bigger catches, morning prices being highest, weather
  variability, labor cost of resetting nets). Disputes over the *sequencing itself* were rare —
  Ostrom notes the researcher who lived there two years, Paul Alexander, "never observed a
  dispute about the sequence in which nets would be beached," even though the same village had
  extraordinary levels of *other* violence (three murders, seventeen serious assaults during
  Alexander's stay, p. 152). **The internal rule-design worked. That's the twist that makes this
  case honest rather than a strawman** — this isn't "primitive people who never figured out
  Ostrom's principles"; per Ostrom's own words, "This was not a problem of ignorance... It was
  not a case of individuals being incapable of devising and enforcing rules well tailored to
  their local circumstances" (p. 155).
- **What they could not do: hold the line on the number of nets.** 1933 national legislation
  registered and capped the nets at the number then in use (32). The cap was not enforced. By
  1945, entrepreneurial pressure (population growth, a new road, an ice factory, a fourfold rise
  in fish prices 1938–41) had pushed the number to 71 nets on a beach that could profitably
  support roughly 20–30 (p. 164; Ostrom's own arithmetic: with only 20 to 30 nets, all could be
  used efficiently; by 1971 they owned 100).
- **The mechanism of failure, named plainly by Ostrom, is political, not technical:** villagers
  petitioned repeatedly (1940, 1942, 1945) to get the existing net-limit law enforced; a 1946
  petition succeeded temporarily. Then in 1964 a new entrepreneur, David Mahattea, unable to buy
  into existing nets, went around the local district revenue officer (who had refused him) by
  going to his local Member of Parliament — and got permission to register new nets anyway.
  **"Between the two faction leaders, 24 new nets were constructed and added to the 84 nets then
  in operation"** (p. 169), each expansion tied to political patronage rather than to any change
  in the resource's carrying capacity. It escalated to an actual brawl at sea in 1966 — "the boat
  carrying net 1 overturned," and only the arrival of three jeep-loads of armed police (which
  Mahattea had pre-arranged) prevented a riot on the beach (p. 169). The government then froze
  the count at 108 nets — nearly four times the 1933 legal cap, and roughly five times what
  Ostrom's own arithmetic says the beach could use efficiently.
- **Ostrom's own diagnostic verdict, which is close to a thesis statement the chapter can use
  almost directly (p. 169):** "It does illustrate what happens in a dynamic local setting when
  appropriators do not have autonomy to make and enforce new rules." Her Table 5.2 (p. 179)
  scores Mawelle after 1938 as meeting only **two** of the seven principles — congruent rules and
  monitoring — having lost, specifically, the "minimal recognition of rights to organize"
  principle to outside political interference.

**Confidence: SOLID.** Read directly from the primary text this session, cross-checked against
Ostrom's own summary table (p. 179) and her own explicit diagnostic sentence (p. 169). Ostrom's
own citations are Paul Alexander's ethnographic fieldwork (1977, 1982) — the Verifier should
ideally pull Alexander directly (full citation needed; Ostrom cites "Alexander 1982" repeatedly
without a full bibliography page captured in this extraction — **flag for Verifier to pull
Ostrom's own bibliography/reference list pages for the complete Alexander citation** before
print) but Ostrom's own text was read in full for this packet, which is a stronger sourcing tier
than a secondary paraphrase.

### Alternate/backup: Bodrum and the Bay of Izmir, Turkey (Ostrom pp. 143–146)

Two Turkish inshore fisheries near Alanya (the site of Ostrom's own well-known *success* case,
already used in the prior draft per the Showrunner's note — do not reuse Alanya as this
chapter's opening case, but a one-line mention that Bodrum/Izmir sit right next to a case that
*did* work is a strong "same country, same fish, different outcome" contrast if the Drafter
wants it). Ostrom's own label for Bodrum: **"a 'textbook example of rent dissipation in a
fishery'"** (Berkes 1986b, p. 79, quoted in Ostrom p. 144-145) — early trawler success drew in
too many entrants, a local cooperative "struggled unsuccessfully" through the 1970s and had
"disappeared by 1983" (p. 145), leaving six competing subgroups of fishers with no shared rules.
The Bay of Izmir, nearby and larger, is described in Ostrom's own phrase as suffering from
national-level **"benign neglect"** (p. 146): licensing was required but uncapped, restrictions
existed on paper but the responsible ministry "employed no agents to enforce those rules." Per
Table 5.2 (p. 179), **none** of the seven principles characterize either fishery.

**Why this is the second choice, not the first, for this chapter specifically:** it's a cleaner,
simpler failure (nobody built working rules, so of course it failed) and doesn't engage with the
chapter's central "here are the conditions under which it works" argument as sharply as Mawelle
does. It is however a very safe, unambiguous backup if the Drafter finds Mawelle's net-
sequencing detail too technical to compress into the chapter's word count — Bodrum/Izmir can be
summarized in two sentences and still lands the honesty beat.

### Considered and set aside: San Bernardino County groundwater / Mojave Water Agency

Ostrom pp. 146–148, 163. A genuine failure (San Bernardino's groundwater basins remain in
overdraft "even after they initiated litigation and created a special district," p. 143) but the
causal story is about scale and heterogeneity of interests inside a single large regional agency
rather than about any of the three design principles likely to be featured (see `evidence.md`
§1) — Ostrom's own diagnosis is that "individuals who do not have similar images of the problems
they face... are unlikely to solve their problems even when the institutional means to do so are
available to them" (p. 163), which is a real and important finding but a poorer thematic match
for a chapter built around Valencia's specific mechanics. Not recommended as the primary failure
case; flagged here in case the Drafter wants a domestic-U.S. example instead of Sri Lanka or
Turkey.

## Continuity notes for the Drafter

1. **Alanya (Turkey) is Ostrom's other famous case and is explicitly *not* this chapter's
   opening case** per the brief — Valencia is required. Ch. 1's evidence packet already gestures
   at Ostrom's fieldwork generally (Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village forests, Spanish and
   Filipino irrigation) as "the standard correction" to Hardin; this chapter should not re-tread
   that ground, only build the Valencia case specifically. If the Drafter wants a one-line
   callback to Ch. 1's Ostrom mention, that's a continuity opportunity, not a requirement.
2. **The "seven vs. eight canals" discrepancy (see above) must be resolved or explicitly hedged
   before the chapter states a number in prose** — do not let the chapter's title ("Eight Rules")
   accidentally imply eight canals when Ostrom's own text says seven were organized into the
   Tribunal's communities. (The title's "eight" refers to Ostrom's eight design principles, not
   the canal count — but a careless sentence could conflate them. Flagging so it doesn't happen
   silently.)
3. **The brief's instruction not to list all eight design principles** — the case material above
   most directly and vividly illustrates: **clearly defined boundaries** (land-tied water rights,
   canal-specific rules), **monitoring** (the "watching each other while waiting your turn"
   mechanism, Figure 3.2), and **graduated sanctions** (the deliberately small, publicly known
   fine schedule). See `evidence.md` §1–3 for the full argument for recommending exactly these
   three, with exact wording and pages for each.
4. **The interlocutor's "this doesn't scale" objection has a direct, primary-sourced answer in
   Valencia itself**, not just in abstract nesting theory: the huertas' survival depended on
   sitting inside Aragon's relatively decentralized medieval polity rather than Castile's
   centralized one (Ostrom p. 81), and Ostrom's own Design Principle 8 (nested enterprises,
   p. 101) uses the Spanish huertas as its lead example: **"irrigators are organized on the basis
   of three or four nested levels, all of which are then also nested in local, regional, and
   national governmental jurisdictions."** See `evidence.md` §6 for the full scale-concession
   material and exact wording.
