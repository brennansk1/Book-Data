## 1. The three recommended design principles to feature

The brief is explicit: **do not list all eight of Ostrom's principles. Pick three, explain them
properly through Valencia, cite the rest to the notes.** Table 3.1 (Ostrom, p. 89) lists all
eight verbatim — full text below for the Drafter's and Continuity's reference, so the other five
can be accurately cited to endnotes even though they're not developed in-text.

### Table 3.1, complete, exact wording (Ostrom, p. 89) — SOLID, read directly

> 1. **Clearly defined boundaries.** Individuals or households who have rights to withdraw
> resource units from the CPR must be clearly defined, as must the boundaries of the CPR itself.
>
> 2. **Congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions.**
> Appropriation rules restricting time, place, technology, and/or quantity of resource units are
> related to local conditions and to provision rules requiring labor, material, and/or money.
>
> 3. **Collective-choice arrangements.** Most individuals affected by the operational rules can
> participate in modifying the operational rules.
>
> 4. **Monitoring.** Monitors, who actively audit CPR conditions and appropriator behavior, are
> accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators.
>
> 5. **Graduated sanctions.** Appropriators who violate operational rules are likely to be
> assessed graduated sanctions (depending on the seriousness and context of the offense) by
> other appropriators, by officials accountable to these appropriators, or by both.
>
> 6. **Conflict-resolution mechanisms.** Appropriators and their officials have rapid access to
> low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts among appropriators or between appropriators and
> officials.
>
> 7. **Minimal recognition of rights to organize.** The rights of appropriators to devise their
> own institutions are not challenged by external governmental authorities.
>
> For CPRs that are parts of larger systems:
>
> 8. **Nested enterprises.** Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict
> resolution, and governance activities are organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises.

Ostrom's own framing note, immediately preceding the table, worth quoting for the chapter's
epistemic-honesty register (p. 89, also SOLID): **"This list of design principles is still quite
speculative. I am not yet willing to argue that these design principles are necessary conditions
for achieving institutional robustness in CPR settings."** This is a genuinely useful sentence
for a book whose own thesis is calibrated confidence — Ostrom hedging her own foundational
result, in the book that made her the only political scientist to win the Nobel in economics, is
a strong example the chapter could point to directly.

### RECOMMENDATION: feature Principles 1 (clearly defined boundaries), 4 (monitoring), and 5
(graduated sanctions)

**Why these three, and not e.g. 3+6+7 or some other combination:** the case material Ostrom
herself provides for Valencia is *asymmetrically rich* across the eight principles. Boundaries,
monitoring, and sanctions are the three where Valencia supplies a concrete, physical, scene-able
mechanism — exactly what this pipeline's brief and `PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md` both want (no payoff
matrices, no lists carrying the exposition, a case that does the argumentative work). The other
five are real and citable but are thinner in the Valencia material specifically:

- **Principle 1 (boundaries)** is the huerta's water-right-inheres-in-the-land rule — vivid,
  concrete, and it's the first thing that has to be true before anything else works. Ostrom's own
  discussion of this principle (pp. 90–91) uses the general CPR literature, but Valencia
  furnishes the cleanest illustration: regadiu land (irrigated, with a right) vs. secano land
  (dry, no right) vs. extremales (right only in years of declared abundance) — a boundary that is
  literally written into which parcels of dirt get water, not an abstract membership list.
- **Principle 4 (monitoring)** is illustrated by Valencia about as well as any case in the book:
  the "reciprocal monitoring" mechanism (Figure 3.2, p. 74) where a farmer waiting his turn on
  the canal has nothing to do *but* watch the farmer ahead of him and the ditch-rider — Ostrom's
  broader theoretical point (pp. 93–96) is that low-cost monitoring is a structural feature of
  well-designed rules, not a separately funded enforcement bureaucracy, and Valencia is one of
  her two lead examples for this (the other is the Alanya fishing-turn system, already used in
  the prior draft — see `cases.md`).
- **Principle 5 (graduated sanctions)** is illustrated by the fine-book data (441 fines in 1443,
  499 in 1486, "more than one per day," yet each fine kept deliberately tiny — "a few pennies at
  the most," Glick 1970 p. 56, quoted in Ostrom p. 75) — a genuinely surprising, counterintuitive
  finding (harsh punishment would have *undermined* compliance, per Ostrom's discussion of
  "quasi-voluntary compliance," pp. 93–98) that gives the chapter something to argue rather than
  just report.

Principles 2, 3, 6, and 7 are real and should be cited to the endnotes (their exact wording is
in the table above for that purpose); Principle 8 (nested enterprises) should NOT be cited only
to the endnotes — it is the chapter's answer to the "doesn't scale" interlocutor and needs
in-text treatment; see §6 below.

**A fourth candidate I considered and rejected:** Principle 7 (minimal recognition of rights to
organize) is tempting because it's the hinge of the Mawelle failure case (see `cases.md` and §4
below) and because it connects directly to the book's Axis 2 (circle failure / rights /
constitutional constraint) material elsewhere in the framework. But Valencia itself doesn't
illustrate Principle 7 as vividly as Mawelle's failure does — Valencia's autonomy from Castile
(p. 81) is more of a background condition than an in-scene mechanism, so it works better as part
of the failure-case contrast (Valencia had it; Mawelle lost it) than as one of the three features
built through Valencia's own mechanics. If the Drafter finds boundaries/monitoring/sanctions
too front-loaded toward "how compliance is enforced" and wants one principle about *how the
rules themselves get made or changed*, Principle 3 (collective-choice arrangements — exact
wording and page in the table above; discussion pp. 92–93) is the next-best alternate: Valencia's
hereters meet every two or three years to elect the syndic and set rules per canal, which is a
real, quotable mechanism, just a less scene-able one than the other three.

## 2. Design Principle 1 — Clearly defined boundaries, Valencia's version

**Confidence: SOLID**, read directly, pp. 71, 90–91.

The allocation rule, exact wording (p. 71): **"the right to water inheres in the land itself.
Land that was watered before the time of the reconquest is specified as irrigated land
(regadiu), and the remaining lands in these huertas are dry lands (secano)... Some land is
entitled to water only in times of abundance (extremales). The basic allocation principle in
Valencia is that each piece of regadiu land is entitled to a quantity of canal water
proportionate to its size."**

Ostrom's general discussion of why this principle matters (p. 91, SOLID): **"So long as the
boundaries of the resource and/or the specification of individuals who can use the resource
remain uncertain, no one knows what is being managed or for whom... local appropriators face
the risk that any benefits they produce by their efforts will be reaped by others who have not
contributed to those efforts."** And, on why she ranks this one principle among seven rather
than treating it as sufficient on its own (p. 91): **"Making this attribute one of seven, rather
than a unique attribute, puts its importance in a more realistic perspective. Simply closing the
boundaries is not enough."** This last sentence is useful ammunition against a reading of the
chapter that oversimplifies Ostrom into "just define property rights and you're done" — she
explicitly warns against that reading in her own text.

## 3. Design Principle 4 — Monitoring, Valencia's version

**Confidence: SOLID**, read directly, pp. 73–74, 93–96 (general theoretical discussion), p. 95
(Spanish-huerta-specific detail).

The scene (p. 73): **"The level of monitoring that is used in the huertas is very high. In this
environment of water scarcity and risk, many temptations occur to take water out of turn, or in
some way obtain illegal water. As the time approaches for a farmer to take his turn at the
water, he will tend his fields near to the canal so that he can be prepared to open his own gate
when the water arrives; if not prepared, he misses his turn entirely and must wait for the next
round. While waiting, it is relatively easy to watch what those ahead of him are doing and watch
the ditch-riders, whom he is paying."**

Ostrom's general theoretical payoff, stated directly and quotably (p. 95, SOLID): **"The costs of
monitoring are low in many long-enduring CPRs as a result of the rules in use. Irrigation
rotation systems, for example, usually place the two actors most concerned with cheating in
direct contact with one another... Neither has to invest additional resources in monitoring
activities. Monitoring is a by-product of their own strong motivations to use their water
rotation turns to the fullest extent."** This is the chapter's cleanest one-sentence statement
of why monitoring in Valencia isn't a bureaucracy: it's baked into the queue itself.

**The monitor-accountability detail (also p. 95, SOLID) that directly matches the exact wording
of Principle 4** ("accountable to the appropriators **or are the appropriators**"): **"In the
Spanish huertas, a portion of the fines is kept by the guards"** — the syndic's ditch-riders are
paid, in part, out of the fines they themselves levy, and per p. 74, the syndic "can lose respect
— and his job — if the allocation of water is not handled fairly and according to the farmers'
rules." The guards are watched by the same farmers they watch; "some redundancy is built into the
monitoring and sanctioning system" (p. 96).

**A genuine complication to flag, not hide:** Ostrom also notes (p. 88) that this same
transparency created real risk of violence: **"Given that everyone is watching everyone else,
there is considerable potential for violence among irrigators and between irrigators and their
agents. In medieval times, the norms related to honor probably exacerbated the potential for
conflict, and hereters 'were willing to fight in an instant if they felt that their water supply
was jeopardized in any way' (Glick 1970, p. 70). The actual violence never approached the
potential."** This is worth keeping in the chapter rather than cutting for tidiness — it's the
kind of honest complication the book's own standing instructions ask for, and it makes the
system's stability more impressive, not less, that it held despite real risk of violence rather
than in some idealized absence of tension.

## 4. Design Principle 5 — Graduated sanctions, Valencia's version

**Confidence: SOLID for the general finding and the theoretical framing; the specific fine-book
statistics are Castellón, not Valencia proper — a distinction that must not be silently
collapsed.** Pp. 74–75, 88–89 (Castellón fine-book data, via Glick 1970), pp. 93, 97–98 (general
theoretical discussion of graduated sanctions and "quasi-voluntary compliance").

**Important sourcing flag, parallel to Ch. 1's Newfoundland-cod discrepancy-flagging practice:**
the detailed quantitative fine data — 441 fines in 1443, 499 in 1486, "more than one per day,"
the two-thirds/one-third syndic/accuser split — comes from surviving fine books for **the
nearly similarly structured huerta of Castellón**, not Valencia's own huerta records (Ostrom,
p. 88, citing Glick 1970). Ostrom is explicit that Castellón is "nearly similarly structured" to
Valencia and uses it as the best-documented proxy because Valencia's own records for this level
of detail were apparently not what Glick worked from (or at least not what Ostrom is drawing on
here). **The chapter should either (a) attribute this specific data to Castellón as the closely
analogous case Ostrom herself uses, or (b) speak more generally about "the Valencian huertas,"
plural, which is accurate — but it must not state these specific numbers as Valencia's own
fine-book record**, because that's not what the primary source says.

The exact fine-schedule finding, quotable (Glick 1970, p. 56, quoted in Ostrom p. 75, SOLID):
**"[fines] were very low (a few pennies at the most) and also variable, depending on the gravity
of the offense, on general economic conditions, and probably on the individual's ability to
pay."** Ostrom's own gloss: **"Because the fines actually assessed were kept relatively low, the
guards did not deeply antagonize the farmers, who generally adhered to the rules. A farmer would
suffer some humiliation if detected cheating, but the monetary fine for cooperative farmers
would be quite low."**

**The theoretical payoff — why graduated (not harsh, uniform) sanctions work, in Ostrom's own
words (p. 90, SOLID), directly discussing this same Castellón/Valencia material:** **"Assessing
harsh punishment to someone who usually follows the rules, but in one instance errs in the face
of a desperate situation, can engender considerable antagonism and resentment (Oliver 1980)."**
And, on the repeat-offense structure (p. 89, SOLID, Glick 1970 p. 59 via Ostrom): **"Two-thirds
of those fined in a year were 'one-time offenders' and were not mentioned again in the fine
book."** The system's design implicitly assumes most rule-breaking is opportunistic and
situational rather than a fixed criminal type — which is exactly what "graduated" sanctions are
built to handle, and exactly the finding Ostrom's broader "quasi-voluntary compliance" framework
(drawing on Margaret Levi, pp. 93–94) explains: appropriators comply not because they're
coerced into 100% compliance by threat of maximum punishment, but because they trust that (1)
the rules produce a collective benefit and (2) most others are also complying — and graduated,
proportionate sanctions preserve that trust in a way that harsh, uniform punishment would not.

**One more usable, human-scale detail (p. 88, SOLID):** Glick notes one **"particularly
fractious individual"** in the Castellón records who made five accusations of theft against
others and was himself accused thirteen times during a single year (1486) — ten of the eighteen
incidents were conflicts between members of just two families. Ostrom/Glick's point: this kind
of chronic, personal conflict was rare in the data, "considered by Glick to be 'a tribute to
both the efficiency of the distribution system and the vigilance of the guards'" (Glick 1970,
p. 64). Good texture if the chapter wants one named human-scale irritant rather than only
aggregate statistics — though note this individual is not named beyond Glick's description, so
"a particularly fractious farmer" rather than an invented name.

## 5. Legend vs. documented — the Tribunal's rituals and age (web-verified)

Everything in this section outside of direct Ostrom quotations was checked in a separate,
live-web verification pass this session (not from memory), cross-checking the Tribunal's own
official site (tribunaldelasaguas.org), Spain's Ministry of Culture intangible-heritage portal,
UNESCO's own ICH record, Visit Valencia (the official regional tourism board), the University of
Valencia's Horta Valencia Chair heritage catalogue, Smithsonian Magazine, and the Tribunal's own
archive description (arxiumap.org). Confidence ratings below reflect that pass.

### 5a. Time of day — noon, not "morning"

**Confidence: SOLID for current practice; the apparent conflict with Ostrom is minor, not a
factual error to correct.** Every current source checked — the Tribunal's own site, Visit
Valencia, the Comunitat Valenciana tourism board — states the court convenes every Thursday **at
noon (12:00)**, some specifying "when the Micalet bell tolls noon," at the Apostles' Door. (On
Thursdays that fall on public holidays the session moves to Wednesday; there's a seasonal break
around Christmas.) Ostrom's own wording, confirmed directly against her text, is "Thursday
mornings" (p. 71) — not technically false if "morning" is read loosely to include midday, but
every contemporary source frames it specifically as **noon**. **Recommendation for the chapter:
use "noon," sourced to the Tribunal's own current practice, and treat Ostrom's "mornings" as
dated/informal phrasing rather than a contradiction worth litigating in text** — a footnote
noting the discrepancy is enough if the Drafter wants the belt-and-suspenders version.

### 5b. "Black robes" — real, but it's a farmer's smock, not judicial dress

**Confidence: SOLID that the detail is real; needs precise phrasing, not the word "robes"
unqualified.** The Tribunal's own site describes the syndic's garment as a huerta work-smock
"which he wears with great dignity like a magistrate's robe" — the comparison to a robe is the
Tribunal's own, but the garment itself is explicitly a farmer's traditional black blouse
(*blusa*), not a formal judicial vestment. Visit Valencia independently confirms members "wear
the distinctive black blouse characteristic of the people of the Huerta." Smithsonian Magazine
describes the syndics as "dressed head to toe in black" while clarifying these are "the
traditional blouses worn by orchard farmers of the Huerta." **Recommendation: "black smocks — the
same rough workwear any Huerta farmer wore into his own fields, not judicial robes" is both more
accurate and a better sentence than "black robes."** The detail that eight farmers who plow
their own land sit in judgment wearing their own field clothes, not borrowed authority, is
actually a stronger version of the chapter's point than a costume would be.

### 5c. Eight canals, nine communities — Ostrom's "seven" explained, not an error

**Confidence: SOLID for the modern figure; the explanation for Ostrom's "seven" is
well-sourced but not from a single dated primary document, so treat as a plausible resolution,
not certainty.** The modern, near-universal figure — confirmed by the Tribunal's own site, the
UNESCO nomination text, Spain's Ministry of Culture heritage portal, and the University of
Valencia's heritage catalogue — is **eight syndics**, representing (per UNESCO's own nomination
language) **nine underlying irrigation communities** grouped into eight represented seats: right
bank (Quart, Benàger i Faitanar, Mislata, Favara, Rovella) and left bank (Tormos, Mestalla,
Rascanya) — one seat evidently covers two historically distinct communities. A general web
synthesis (not itself a primary source, so treat this specific historical claim as
REPORTED-BUT-SINGLE-SOURCE) suggests the Acequia de Benàger-Faitanar split off to become its own
represented seat at some point, which would mean Ostrom's 1990 "seven" reflects either an older
historical count or the source material she was working from, rather than an OCR error or an
outright mistake on her part. **This is exactly the kind of case where the chapter should state
the current figure (eight, or eight seats / nine communities if being precise) and can, if the
Drafter wants a footnote, note that the number has not always been eight** — but should not
silently "correct" Ostrom by assuming her 1990 figure was simply wrong.

### 5d. Age and origin — Roman claims are legend; Islamic-era claims are civic tradition, not
archival fact; the documented floor is the 13th–14th century

**Confidence: SOLID on what's documented; SOLID on what's legend, since even the Tribunal's own
official materials hedge these claims rather than asserting them as settled.**
- **Roman origin: legend, not documented.** The Tribunal's own official history page hedges
  explicitly: some legal institution addressing water disputes "would already exist from Roman
  times," but "the organization which we have inherited dates from times of Al-Andalus." No
  archaeological or documentary record ties a continuous institution back to Rome. **Treat as
  legend/plausible-but-unproven antecedent, not the origin of the current court.**
- **The commonly cited ~960 CE Islamic-era founding date (under Caliph Abd al-Rahman III):
  widely repeated, plausible, but not backed by a contemporary 10th-century document.** Notably,
  Valencia held a civic "millennial anniversary" commemoration in 1960 built around this date —
  which is itself evidence the 960 figure functions as commemorative civic tradition rather than
  an archivally verified founding date. Even the Tribunal's own official history is more
  cautious than the popular 960 CE claim, saying only that the inherited institution "dates from
  times of Al-Andalus and, very possibly, from times of the Caliphate of Córdoba" — hedged, not
  asserted as fact.
  This is consistent with, and slightly more cautious than, Ostrom's own p. 71 formulation —
  "the many Islamic features of its traditions have led **scholars to argue**" — which is itself
  already appropriately hedged and should be the model for how the chapter states this.
- **The documented floor: no evidence predates the 13th century; a 1321 royal charter from King
  Jaume II is the first solid documentary anchor.** Multiple sources converge: no documentary
  evidence of the Tribunal exists prior to the Christian reconquest period (Valencia recaptured
  1238, per Ostrom p. 69); a specific, repeatedly cited **1321 privilege from King Jaume II**
  confirming water-distribution rights from the Turia is the documentary anchor point cited by
  the Tribunal itself and by Visit Valencia. This sits comfortably alongside Ostrom's own
  documented floor — the 1435 Benàcher/Faitanar articles (`cases.md`) — as a slightly earlier,
  independently-sourced data point on the same side of the legend/documented line.
- **Written codification of the customary rules themselves (as opposed to charters referencing
  the institution) is dated by the Tribunal's own site to "the beginning of the 18th century"**
  — i.e., the underlying canal-by-canal customary law was transmitted orally for centuries before
  being written down, consistent with Ostrom's own account of locally varied, customary
  (non-codified) rules (p. 89, "the specific rules for the various huertas differ rather
  substantially").
- **Recommended chapter framing, combining Ostrom with the web-verified material:** the
  Tribunal's own promotional materials and popular sources cite Islamic-era origins around 960
  CE and gesture at Roman antecedents; the earliest surviving documentary evidence is 13th–14th
  century (the 1321 Jaume II charter, and Ostrom's own 1435 Benàcher/Faitanar articles), with the
  customary rules themselves not committed to writing until the 1700s. State the "close to 1,000
  years" figure the way Ostrom herself does — as an informed, hedged estimate, not a documented
  certainty.
- **Remaining gap:** a full academic treatment of the origins question (Spanish-language sources
  — a paper titled "Sull'origine del Tribunal de las Aguas de la Vega de Valencia" and a UNED
  journal article on the court's "oral tradition and protocol" were located but only their
  abstracts were accessible this session, not full text) would sharpen this further; flagged for
  the Verifier if the chapter wants to go beyond what's above.

### 5e. UNESCO listing — confirmed exact name, year, and an important nuance

**Confidence: SOLID — primary-source confirmation (UNESCO's own record).** The listing is **not**
Valencia's Tribunal alone — it is a **joint listing with Murcia's water court**. Exact official
title, per UNESCO's own Representative List entry (reference RL/00171): **"Irrigators' Tribunals
of the Spanish Mediterranean Coast: the Council of Wise Men of the Plain of Murcia and the Water
Tribunal of the Plain of Valencia."** Inscribed on the **Representative List of the Intangible
Cultural Heritage of Humanity** in **2009** (specifically at the Committee's 4th session; the
Tribunal's own archive material gives the specific date **September 30, 2009**). Note that
UNESCO's English designation for the Valencia court itself is "Water Tribunal of the Plain of
Valencia," not a direct transliteration of "Tribunal de las Aguas de la Vega de Valencia" — worth
using the precise UNESCO wording if the chapter cites the listing formally, and worth noting for
the Drafter that this listing gives the chapter a second, independent, verified data point
tying Valencia and Murcia together — which is a nice continuity link, since Ostrom's own text
(p. 77, see `cases.md`) independently connects the two courts as sharing the same Thursday
meeting day and the same "oral, public, summary, and cheap" procedural character, decades before
UNESCO made the same pairing formal.

### 5f. "No written records" — Ostrom's own framing is the correct one; a flatter claim is not

**Confidence: SOLID — this closely corroborates and sharpens the flag already raised in
`cases.md`.** The live-web pass confirms a real, three-part distinction:
1. **The live proceedings themselves (testimony, argument) have always been, and remain today,
   purely oral** — no transcript is made, then or now. This part of "no written records" is true
   and remains true.
2. **The underlying customary rules were transmitted orally for centuries and not written down
   until the early 18th century** (per the Tribunal's own site) — consistent with Ostrom's
   account of locally varied, customary, non-codified canal rules.
3. **Written record-keeping of case outcomes did eventually begin, and survives as an archive
   today.** The Tribunal's own archive (Archivo del Tribunal de las Aguas) documents that
   material issued by and received by the court has been preserved from the **19th century**
   onward, including a notable 19th-century "book of judgments" (*libro de sentencias*), with
   "short minute books" recording case specifics kept "since the first third of the 20th
   century." These are consultable archival holdings today, not a legend.
**This directly confirms and sharpens the correction already flagged in `cases.md`: "no written
records" full stop is not accurate. The precise, defensible claim is "no transcript of the
arguments, and (until the 1700s) no written code of law" — which is exactly what Ostrom's own
1990 sentence already says ("the final decisions of the court are recorded, but not the
proceedings," p. 72). Ostrom's phrasing turns out to be the more accurate one, not a
simplification the chapter needs to correct — the correction is needed against the looser
popular "no written records at all" claim, not against Ostrom.**

## 6. The scale concession — nesting and polycentricity (Design Principle 8)

**Confidence: SOLID**, read directly, p. 101 (the principle's own discussion) and p. 71
(Aragon/Castile political-autonomy point, p. 81).

Per the brief: the economist interlocutor's "this doesn't scale" objection should be **conceded
substantially** — "it doesn't scale simply, which is why nesting matters and why some problems
need states." Per `canon/CANON.md` §7, this maps directly onto the framework's broader claim
that "Ostrom-style community governance [works] where the commons is local and the users can
monitor each other" but that other problems need Pigovian correction or state provision.

**Ostrom's own Principle 8 and its exact wording (Table 3.1, p. 89):** **"Nested enterprises.
Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution, and governance
activities are organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises."**

**Ostrom's own discussion, using the Spanish huertas as the lead example (p. 101, SOLID) —
this is close to a ready-made answer to the "doesn't scale" objection, in Ostrom's own words:**
> "All of the more complex, enduring CPRs meet this last design principle. In the Spanish
> huertas, for example, irrigators are organized on the basis of three or four nested levels,
> all of which are then also nested in local, regional, and national governmental
> jurisdictions... Establishing rules at one level, without rules at the other levels, will
> produce an incomplete system that may not endure over the long run."

**The direct textual link from Principle 7 to the failure cases, useful for connecting the
"doesn't scale" concession to the Mawelle failure case in `cases.md` (p. 101, SOLID):**
> "But if external governmental officials presume that only they have the authority to set the
> rules, then it will be very difficult for local appropriators to sustain a rule-governed CPR
> over the long run... In Chapter 5 we shall examine several cases in which this design
> principle is not met."

This is Ostrom's own foreshadowing sentence, written in 1990, pointing directly at the failure
material this chapter also needs — the "doesn't scale" concession and the "here's a documented
failure, for honesty" requirement are, in Ostrom's own book, the *same underlying point*: local
self-governance is not self-sufficient at any scale; it needs to be nested inside, and protected
by, a permissive layer above it (a state that recognizes its legitimacy, per Principle 7) or it
either never forms or gets captured. Valencia had that permissive layer (Aragon's relatively
decentralized medieval constitutionalism, p. 81); Mawelle's had it, then lost it to a single
ambitious local politician's intervention. **This is a strong, honest, primary-sourced version
of the "concede substantially" instruction** — the chapter isn't just saying "yes, yes, it needs
some help from institutions above it" as a grudging aside; Ostrom's own framework makes nesting
one of exactly eight necessary conditions, on par with monitoring and sanctions, not an
afterthought.

## 7. The "neither market nor state" framing — exact quote

**Confidence: SOLID**, read directly, p. 1 (the opening page of Chapter 1, "Reflections on the
commons" — not the book's formal introduction/preface, which precedes it in roman-numeral
pagination, but the first page of numbered text).

Full passage, exact wording (p. 1): **"Some scholarly articles about the 'tragedy of the
commons' recommend that 'the state' control most natural resources to prevent their destruction;
others recommend that privatizing those resources will resolve the problem. What one can observe
in the world, however, is that neither the state nor the market is uniformly successful in
enabling individuals to sustain long-term, productive use of natural resource systems. Further,
communities of individuals have relied on institutions resembling neither the state nor the
market to govern some resource systems with reasonable degrees of success over long periods of
time."**

This is the cleanest, most directly quotable statement of the chapter's central thesis in
Ostrom's own words, on the very first page of her book — strongly recommended as either an
epigraph or a direct in-text quotation early in the chapter. It also states the book's own
three-part method in the next paragraph (p. 1, SOLID): **"This book is an effort to (1) critique
the foundations of policy analysis as applied to many natural resources, (2) present empirical
examples of successful and unsuccessful efforts to govern and manage such resources, and (3)
begin the effort to develop better intellectual tools to understand the capabilities and
limitations of self-governing institutions."** Note item (2) explicitly promises both success
and failure cases — Ostrom's own stated method already requires the "documented failure, for
honesty" the brief asks for; it's not an add-on to her argument, it's built into her project from
page 1.

**Full citation:** Ostrom, Elinor. *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for
Collective Action.* Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1990. (Series: The Political Economy
of Institutions and Decisions, eds. James E. Alt and Douglass C. North — confirmed from the
book's own series page, p. ii of front matter.)
