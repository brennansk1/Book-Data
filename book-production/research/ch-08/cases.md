## THE INVERSION QUESTION — read this before picking

The brief asks for an opening case of "someone who calculated and got it wrong in a way that is sympathetic rather than villainous," but the brief itself also flags that the strongest case might run the other way: someone who *followed the rule* under enormous pressure to calculate instead, and was *right*. Kelsey is that case, and I think she is dramatically stronger than any of the standard-direction candidates, for a structural reason: the chapter's interlocutor is the act-consequentialist who says "surely you should do the thing with the best outcome." A standard-direction opening case (a sympathetic person who calculated and got it wrong) risks reading as a single cautionary anecdote that the act-consequentialist can shrug off ("bad luck, not a bad method — she should have calculated *better*"). Kelsey's case doesn't have that escape hatch: she is the calculator's own nightmare scenario, played straight. Every institutional and financial incentive told her to approve the drug — the 60-day statutory clock, a major pharmaceutical company's repeated, escalating pressure, doctors and patients who wanted relief from morning sickness, and no evidence in hand that would have let her *prove* the drug unsafe by any calculation available to her *at the time*. She had, by the applicable case-by-case cost-benefit standard, no defensible case for delay — and she delayed anyway, because the rule she was enforcing (no approval without adequate safety data, full stop) did not ask her to calculate. That is the trust argument and the prediction argument in one scene: she could not have justified her holdout to a skeptical act-consequentialist in real time, and she was right anyway, for reasons that had nothing to do with her being smarter than the people pressuring her.

If the Showrunner prefers the standard direction for pacing or tonal reasons (opening on someone who got it wrong, rather than someone who was vindicated, may better set up "here's a fence someone SHOULD have respected" before pivoting to the trust argument), Pou is the strongest standard-direction case — see Candidate 2.

## CANDIDATE 1 — Frances Oldham Kelsey and the Kevadon (thalidomide) application, FDA, September 1960 – March 1962 [RECOMMENDED, run as inversion]

**Why it fits:** A named, real, sympathetic official who had every incentive — professional, statutory, humanitarian — to approve a drug on a case-by-case judgment that it was probably fine, and instead enforced the rule that required adequate safety evidence, against sustained pressure, before she had any concrete proof the drug was dangerous. The rule was vindicated; the case-by-case calculation the company and the pressure campaign around her were implicitly asking her to make would have killed and maimed the same children thalidomide killed and maimed everywhere else it was approved.

**Timeline and named individuals:**
- Frances Oldham Kelsey, MD, PhD, joined the FDA in 1960 and was assigned as a medical reviewer.
- On **September 8, 1960**, she received a New Drug Application from Richardson-Merrell (Cincinnati) for Kevadon — the proposed U.S. trade name for thalidomide, already marketed in West Germany and dozens of other countries as a sedative and anti-nausea drug, including for morning sickness in pregnancy.
- Under the applicable FDA rule, the agency had 60 days to act on an application or it would go to market automatically. Kelsey found the safety data submitted inadequate — much of it was testimonial rather than rigorous clinical evidence — and repeatedly found the application incomplete, which reset the clock.
- Richardson-Merrell executive F. Joseph Murray, along with other company representatives, pressed her by phone, letter, and in person, submitting what the company considered proof of safety; Kelsey continued to reject each resubmission for inadequate evidence.
- Kelsey was specifically troubled by a British Medical Journal letter reporting peripheral neuropathy in patients on long-term thalidomide — evidence the drug had physiological effects beyond sedation that were not fully characterized, which she took as reason to ask whether it could cross the placenta and affect a fetus.
- Through the winter of 1960–61 and into 1961, the application remained pending; Kelsey continued to request additional data.
- In **November 1961**, William McBride (Australia) and Widukind Lenz (West Germany) independently linked thalidomide use in early pregnancy to phocomelia and other severe birth defects; the drug was withdrawn in Germany that month.
- On **March 8, 1962**, Richardson-Merrell formally withdrew its U.S. application.
- Thalidomide caused an estimated 10,000+ birth defects and thousands of miscarriages worldwide, overwhelmingly in countries where it had been approved; the U.S. incidence was limited to a small number of cases from an investigational-use distribution program that had already put samples in some U.S. physicians' hands.
- In **1962**, President Kennedy awarded Kelsey the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service — the first woman to receive it up to that time for something other than her uniformed service.
- The 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendment, passed in the aftermath, required drug manufacturers to prove both safety and efficacy before marketing — Kelsey's ad hoc, individually-defended holdout became codified as the standing rule for everyone.

**The vivid detail:** the mechanism by which she held the line was almost administratively boring — she kept declaring a completed corporate application "incomplete" and asking for more data, again and again, for a year and a half, against a company that wanted a yes/no answer inside a 60-day clock. There was no dramatic confrontation, no single act of defiance — just a bureaucrat repeatedly refusing to certify that she had enough information to say yes, while people on both sides of the transaction (the company, and by extension doctors who wanted the drug for suffering patients) had good-faith reasons to think she was being unreasonable.

**Confidence:** SOLID throughout — this is one of the best-documented episodes in FDA history, covered in the FDA's own historical archive, Scientific American's dedicated retrospective, and multiple biographical sources that agree on dates and mechanism. See `evidence.md` for source-by-source confidence.

## CANDIDATE 2 — Dr. Anna Pou, Memorial Medical Center, New Orleans, Hurricane Katrina, August 30 – September 1, 2005 [RECOMMENDED as standard-direction backup]

**Why it fits:** This is the cleanest "calculated and got it wrong, sympathetically" candidate in the standard direction. Pou is not remotely villainous in any mainstream account — Sheri Fink's Pulitzer-winning investigation and subsequent book treat her with real sympathy even while documenting exactly what she did — and the calculation she made (triage by survivability under catastrophic, resource-exhausted conditions, departing from any normal standard of care) is the paradigm case of an individual replacing an institutional rule with her own case-by-case judgment under extreme pressure, with fatal results for people who might otherwise have lived.

**Timeline and named individuals:**
- Memorial Medical Center flooded after the levee failures following Hurricane Katrina's landfall (August 29, 2005); by August 30–31 the hospital had no power, no working air conditioning, and indoor temperatures over 100°F. LifeCare Hospitals operated a long-term acute care unit on the hospital's seventh floor, treating the sickest and most immobile patients in the building.
- Evacuation was slow, chaotic, and prioritized by physical ability to be moved — patients had to be carried down darkened stairwells and lifted through a hole in a parking garage wall to helicopters. Triage categories were created ad hoc, including a category some staff have described as effectively "do not evacuate first" for patients judged least likely to survive evacuation or the aftermath.
- On **September 1, 2005**, the last full day before the remaining patients and staff were evacuated, Dr. Anna Pou, an ear-nose-throat and head-and-neck cancer surgeon (not an emergency or critical-care specialist by training), along with nurses Cheri Landry and Lori Budo, administered morphine and the sedative midazolam to a number of patients on the seventh floor.
- Louisiana Attorney General Charles Foti's investigation concluded that at least 17 patients were injected with morphine, midazolam, or both, in the hospital's final hours; Pou was personally linked to 4 of 9 deaths investigated in the LifeCare unit specifically (out of 45 total patient deaths at Memorial before evacuation was complete — most from the heat, dehydration, and lack of care, not from injections).
- In **July 2006**, Pou and the two nurses were arrested and Pou was booked on four counts of second-degree murder.
- Pou publicly and repeatedly stated her intent was to ease pain and suffering in patients she and colleagues believed could not survive evacuation or further care in the conditions, not to kill — a claim toxicology and dosage evidence in some cases complicated but did not conclusively refute.
- In **July 2007**, a New Orleans grand jury declined to indict Pou; the case against the two nurses (who had already received immunity for testimony) was also dropped. Pou was never tried.
- Pou subsequently helped draft and pass Louisiana legislation (2008–2010) granting healthcare workers civil immunity for good-faith actions during declared disasters, excluding willful misconduct.
- Sheri Fink's investigative journalism on the case ("The Deadly Choices at Memorial," ProPublica/*New York Times Magazine*, 2009) won a Pulitzer Prize (2010, feature writing category — verify exact category before citing in-chapter); her book *Five Days at Memorial: Life and Death in a Storm-Ravaged Hospital* (2013) won the National Book Critics Circle Award for nonfiction — the two prizes should not be conflated in text.

**The vivid detail:** the specific, defensible-sounding exception Pou applied was a triage category improvised in real time — under no formal protocol, with no realistic hope of outside authority weighing in, in 100-plus-degree heat, days after the normal chain of command and normal standards of care had already broken down. She was not replacing a bad rule with a better calculation from a position of comfort; she was filling a total vacuum where no rule reached at all, which is exactly what makes the case useful and exactly what a careful chapter needs to say plainly rather than let the reader assume by analogy.

**Confidence:** SOLID on the arrest, the grand jury's refusal to indict, the timeline, and the immunity legislation (multiple wire-service and network-news accounts, AMA Journal of Ethics case write-up, NEJM perspective piece). The precise intent behind specific doses is CONTESTED — Pou's own account and the grand jury's non-indictment on one side; the attorney general's original charging theory and some toxicology findings cited in Fink's reporting on the other. The chapter should not resolve this dispute; it should use the case for what is undisputed (a sympathetic, non-villainous professional made an extreme individualized judgment call, under conditions no protocol anticipated, when no protocol was reachable) rather than adjudicate exactly what she intended.

## CANDIDATE 3 — Governor Robert P. Casey's clemency for Reginald McFadden, Pennsylvania, 1994

**Why it fits:** A clean regulator/official case in the standard direction: an official body (Pennsylvania's Board of Pardons, with Governor Casey's sign-off) used individualized, case-by-case judgment to override the default position against releasing a life-sentenced violent offender, believing McFadden had been rehabilitated. The decision was catastrophically wrong and became a landmark cautionary case in clemency-reform literature. Flag: this case carries real electoral/political baggage (it became a defining issue in the 1994 Pennsylvania governor's race) and involves a Black defendant in a case some commentators have compared to Willie Horton — the Showrunner should weigh whether that resonance is a feature (a documented parallel structure) or a liability (unwanted political noise) before using it.

**Timeline and named individuals:**
- Reginald McFadden was serving a life sentence in Pennsylvania for the 1969 murder of a 60-year-old woman in Philadelphia.
- In **1994**, Pennsylvania's Board of Pardons recommended commutation on a 4–1 vote (Lieutenant Governor Mark Singel, a board member and the Democratic gubernatorial nominee that year, voted in favor); Governor Robert P. Casey signed off, and McFadden was released after roughly 24 years served, on the judgment that he had been rehabilitated.
- Within months of release, McFadden murdered two people in New York State and kidnapped and raped a third victim.
- The case broke in the press roughly a month before the November 1994 election; Singel's opponent Tom Ridge won the governorship, with McFadden widely credited/blamed as a contributing factor.
- The case directly drove Pennsylvania clemency reform: the state changed its rules so that a governor may grant a pardon or commutation for a life sentence only after a *unanimous* Board of Pardons vote, not a 4–1 majority — the fence was rebuilt higher after this specific failure, which is itself a clean illustration of the chapter's Chesterton's-fence material (a rule that gets tightened, not just defended, after a documented failure of discretion).

**The vivid detail:** the mechanism of failure was a single dissenting vote (the one board member who voted no) being procedurally overridable by a bare majority — meaning the system already had a built-in check, and the check was not strong enough, which let one board's confident individualized read of one man's rehabilitation stand in for the institution's default caution.

**Confidence:** SOLID on the sentence, the 1994 board vote and release, McFadden's subsequent crimes, and the subsequent unanimous-vote reform (Wikipedia, Northwestern law journal case study, NYU clemency-reform report, contemporaneous Philadelphia Inquirer coverage). SINGLE-SOURCE / CONTESTED on how much of Ridge's electoral victory to attribute causally to this case specifically — treat as "widely credited as a factor," not as a proven causal claim.

## CANDIDATE 4 — Makayla Sault, Ontario, 2014 [weakest of the four; flagged]

**Why it's here:** An 11-year-old First Nations girl with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (roughly 90%+ survival rate on standard chemotherapy) stopped chemotherapy after 11 weeks, at her own stated wish and with her parents' support, in favor of traditional Indigenous medicine and other alternative treatments; a Canadian court declined to intervene, citing the family's Aboriginal and treaty rights to pursue traditional healing alongside/instead of biomedicine; Makayla died in January 2015, reportedly of a stroke that may have been linked to leukemia relapse (a coroner's inquest examined but did not conclusively establish the direct cause).

**Why it's ranked low / red flags:**
1. This is a **calculation made largely by a child with her parents' support**, not cleanly "a parent calculated," which muddies the brief's frame.
2. The court explicitly protected the family's choice on **Aboriginal/treaty-rights grounds**, not on ordinary parental-discretion grounds — meaning the "rule" being set aside (mandatory-treatment norms enforced via child-protection law) was set aside for a reason the chapter is not equipped to relitigate in 4,500 words without either flattening Indigenous rights into a mere illustration of the chapter's point, or derailing into a different and much larger argument the book has not budgeted space for.
3. This involves the death of a real, named child within the last twelve years, in a case still emotionally live in her community — a materially different privacy/dignity register than the adult professionals and officials in Candidates 1–3.
Recommend using only if the Showrunner specifically wants the parent category and is comfortable with all three of the above; otherwise drop.

**Confidence:** SOLID on the medical facts, the court ruling, and the date of death (CBC News, multiple Canadian outlets). The precise cause of death is CONTESTED / not fully resolved by the coroner's inquest as reported.

## CANDIDATE 5 — Chesterton's own fence passage (*The Thing*, 1929) as fallback frame

Not a "case" in the named-person sense the brief otherwise asks for, but worth flagging as a legitimate fallback: the passage itself (see `evidence.md` for exact wording) already contains a small scene — two reformers standing in front of a fence, one wanting to tear it down on the spot, the other refusing until the first can explain what it's for. If none of Candidates 1–4 survives Gate 1, the chapter could open directly on that image, staged as Chesterton wrote it, before naming Chesterton and pivoting into the argument. This has the advantage of zero fact-risk (it is a 1929 published text, not a real person whose story could be gotten wrong) and the disadvantage of being the single most overused reference in this entire literature — nearly every popular treatment of the argument opens the same way, and the concept-index should be checked to confirm this exact device hasn't already been used or spoiled elsewhere in the manuscript before relying on it as the *opening* rather than a supporting beat later in the chapter.

## RED FLAGS AND ITEMS SET ASIDE

- **Stanislav Petrov (1983 Soviet nuclear false-alarm incident) — DO NOT USE as a standing-rules case; if used at all, it belongs in ch-09.** The brief's own note asks for a direction-check, and the check comes back clean: Petrov's own protocol was to report the missile-launch warning up the chain immediately; he instead judged the alert was a false alarm (a five-missile launch reading he considered tactically implausible) and did **not** report it as a confirmed attack, effectively overriding the procedure. He *broke* the standing procedure and was right to. That is a Ch. 9 case (an exception that was right, paired against one that was catastrophically wrong) — the opposite of what Ch. 8 needs. Including him here at all would contradict the brief's own "must not" for this chapter's argument.
- **Willie Horton furlough (Massachusetts, 1986–88) — set aside.** It is well-documented and does involve a discretionary program overriding a stricter default, but there is no single sympathetic named calculator to hang the opening scene on (a corrections bureaucracy administered a furlough program, not an identifiable person making a defensible judgment call), and the case is inseparable in most readers' minds from the 1988 presidential campaign attack ad and its well-documented racialized framing — baggage this book almost certainly does not want at a chapter opening on ordinary sympathetic calculation gone wrong.
- **Abraham Cherrix (Virginia, 2006) — set aside.** Well-documented, sympathetic, but the outcome does not fit: the court sided with the family, Cherrix was permitted to pursue an alternative treatment under oncologist supervision, and he survived. This is a case where discretion was *not* shown to be wrong, so it does not serve the brief's "got it wrong" requirement.
