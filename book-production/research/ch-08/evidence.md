## 1. CHESTERTON'S FENCE — exact wording and what the passage is actually about

**The claim as usually paraphrased:** "don't remove a fence until you know why it was put up." **This paraphrase is not wrong, but it compresses a two-part argument that the chapter should probably keep two-part**, because the compressed version is what every popular treatment already does and the full version is more interesting and more defensible.

**Source:** G.K. Chesterton, *The Thing: Why I Am a Catholic* (1929), chapter "The Drift from Domesticity."

**Exact text (as corroborated across multiple independent transcriptions of the passage, including chesterton.org, the Alignment Forum's sourcing writeup, and Futility Closet):**

> "In the matter of reforming things, as distinct from deforming them, there is one plain and simple principle; a principle which will probably be called a paradox. There exists in such a case a certain institution or law; let us say, for the sake of simplicity, a fence or gate erected across a road. The more modern type of reformer goes gaily up to it and says, 'I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away.' To which the more intelligent type of reformer will do well to answer: 'If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it.'"

**Confidence:** SOLID on the wording quoted above — it is stable across every independent source checked and is consistent with how the passage is quoted in scholarly treatments of Chesterton. I did not verify against a scanned first edition, so the Verifier should still confirm exact punctuation/wording against a facsimile or a critical edition before it goes to print, since online transcriptions of century-old texts do sometimes introduce small errors (a missing comma, a modernized spelling). Treat as SOLID with that one caveat.

**What the brief needs to know, and what gets flattened in almost every popular retelling:**

1. **The passage is explicitly framed as being about *reformers*, not about conservatives defending the status quo.** Chesterton is not arguing against change — the chapter heading is "the matter of reforming things, *as distinct from deforming them*." His target is the reformer who wants to tear down an inherited rule without first doing the work of understanding it. The payoff line ("Go away and think... I may allow you to destroy it") explicitly concedes the fence *can* be destroyed — after the reformer demonstrates understanding, not never. This maps precisely onto CANON §6.1's own formulation ("This is not traditionalism — rules get revised — but 'I can't see the point of this' is a fact about you before it's a fact about the fence") and the chapter should say so explicitly rather than let Chesterton read as a pure anti-reform quote, which is the version almost everyone has half-remembered.

2. **The passage is an *evidentiary* claim, not a *deference* claim, and the chapter's brief already frames it this way ("Chesterton's fence as an evidentiary claim") — this is the correct reading and should be kept precise.** Chesterton isn't saying "obey the fence because it's old" (an appeal to tradition as authority). He's saying "the fence's existence is *evidence* that someone, at some point, had a reason for it, and your inability to currently see that reason is evidence about the limits of your own vantage point, not evidence that no reason exists." That's a claim about what your ignorance licenses you to infer, which is exactly the epistemic move CANON wants (§6.1: "'I can't see the point of this' is a fact about you before it's a fact about the fence").

3. **What the passage does NOT establish, and the chapter should not claim it does:** it does not tell you *how much* evidentiary weight an unexplained rule should get, it does not distinguish between rules whose original rationale has actually lapsed (technology changed, the underlying threat is gone) and rules whose rationale is simply opaque to a particular observer, and it does not address who bears the burden of "going away and thinking" or for how long — a rule could in principle survive on this argument indefinitely if no one ever quite finishes the homework. The chapter's own commitment-device material (Schelling, below) is doing real independent work here and shouldn't be presented as if Chesterton's passage alone settles why case-by-case discretion is worse — the fence argument is about epistemic humility regarding a *specific* rule's rationale; the trust/prediction argument is about why rules-as-such beat calculation-as-such even when you *do* understand the rule's rationale. These are two different arguments and the brief asks for both (trust argument, prediction argument, "not intuition-thumping") — Chesterton alone would be intuition-thumping if the chapter stopped there.

## 2. SCHELLING — commitment as the source of bargaining power (page-cited, `Files/Strategy-of-Conflict_1773177521507_0.pdf`)

**Source:** Thomas C. Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960), Chapter 2, "An Essay on Bargaining."

**Method note:** extracted via `pdftotext -layout` on the file in `Files/`; page numbers below are the book's own printed page numbers, confirmed by locating the printed page-number line immediately before/after each quoted passage in the extracted text (not PDF-viewer page count, which would be offset by front matter).

**The core passage (p. 33), stated as the chapter's central mechanism for why binding yourself can be a source of strength rather than weakness:**

> "The purpose of this chapter is to call attention to an important class of tactics, of a kind that is peculiarly appropriate to the logic of indeterminate situations. The essence of these tactics is some voluntary but irreversible sacrifice of freedom of choice. They rest on the paradox that the power to constrain an adversary may depend on the power to bind oneself; that, in bargaining, weakness is often strength, freedom may be freedom to capitulate, and to burn bridges behind one may suffice to undo an opponent."

**Confidence:** SOLID — direct quotation, page-verified against the PDF.

**Why this is exactly the chapter's "trust argument," not a stretch:** Schelling's book is about adversarial bargaining (nuclear deterrence, price negotiation), and the chapter's argument is about a different context (why a rule-following *person* is more trustworthy than a case-by-case calculator) — the Researcher flags this gap explicitly so the Drafter doesn't overclaim direct applicability. What transfers cleanly: Schelling's core insight is that *removing your own future discretion* is what makes a commitment credible to someone else, because a party who retains the freedom to recalculate case-by-case retains the freedom to defect whenever recalculation favors defection — and a rational counterpart knows this and adjusts trust accordingly. Applied to the chapter's argument: a person known to be a case-by-case calculator is, by the same logic, known to be someone whose behavior in your specific case depends entirely on *their* calculation of what's best (for the outcome, or, self-servingly, for them) in that moment — which is precisely why they cannot be trusted the way a rule-follower can. A rule-follower has pre-committed; a calculator has not, and everyone dealing with them knows it. This is Schelling's mechanism transposed from adversaries to prospective trust in general — the chapter should be explicit that this is a transposition, not a direct citation of Schelling talking about personal ethics (he wasn't).

**Supporting passage on how a commitment must be difficult to reverse to be credible (pp. 37–39):** Schelling's discussion of "cross my heart" commitment ceremonies (p. 38) — a costly, hard-to-fake, publicly recognized way of signaling that one's freedom of choice really has been surrendered — is useful for the chapter's "costly signal" material if the Drafter wants a second, more concrete illustration of why a *visible, hard-to-reverse* commitment carries more trust value than a private, revisable intention: "Suppose only part of the population belongs to the cult in which 'cross my heart' is (or is believed to be) absolutely binding. If everyone knows (and is known to know) everyone else's affiliation, those belonging to this particular cult have the advantage. They can commit themselves, the others cannot." (p. 39) SOLID, page-verified.

**What Schelling does NOT provide, and the brief should not claim he does:** Schelling is not making a moral or trust-in-persons argument at all — he is analyzing rational strategic interaction between adversaries with opposed interests, largely in the context of deterrence and negotiation. The chapter's use of him is an analogical extension the Researcher recommends flagging in-text (something like "the logic Thomas Schelling worked out for nuclear brinksmanship and bargaining applies, with the stakes lowered, to something much more ordinary") rather than presenting Schelling as though he were already writing about personal moral trustworthiness.

## 3. TETLOCK — expert prediction findings, and the "dart-throwing chimp" line specifically

**Primary source (secondary-literature review, used because it summarizes the book's actual findings in citable, precise form):** Adrian E. Tschoegl & J. Scott Armstrong, "Review of: Philip E. Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know?*" *International Journal of Forecasting* (2007), pp. 339–342. [Retrieved and read in full via pdftotext from a saved copy of the Wharton faculty-hosted PDF.]

**What the book (Tetlock 2005) actually did, per this review:** Tetlock recruited 284 people whose professions involved "commenting or offering advice on political and economic trends," and collected forecasts on the probability of various political/economic outcomes across areas both inside and outside each forecaster's stated expertise. By 2003 he had accumulated 82,361 individual forecasts. He then compared expert accuracy against (a) simple statistical extrapolation models, (b) uninformed non-experts, and (c) well-informed non-experts ("attentive readers of the New York Times," in the popularization of this finding).

**The actual headline finding, quoted directly from the review:** "The experts barely if at all outperformed informed non-experts and neither group of forecasters did well against simple rules and models. When Tetlock divided outcomes into three states, 'Better', 'Same', and 'Worse', the forecasters frequently did worse than assuming that each state is equally likely." SOLID — this is a direct quote from a peer-reviewed, published review of the book, by two named academics, and it is consistent with every other secondary account checked.

**The fox/hedgehog finding, quoted directly:** "Tetlock found that foxes usually outperform hedgehogs; foxes produced more accurate forecasts than hedgehogs, though again neither beat simple rules." Foxes ("draw on many ideas and sources of information") outperformed hedgehogs ("interpret the world using their favorite theory or dogma") in calibration and accuracy, though Tetlock was also able to push more foxes than hedgehogs into forecasts that technically violated the axioms of probability (overconfident probability stacking). SOLID.

**The "dart-throwing chimp" line — status and correct handling for the chapter, since this is the single most commonly mangled fact in this literature:**
- Tetlock himself later used a version of this line (widely quoted as: the average expert was "roughly as accurate as a dart-throwing chimpanzee") in his and Dan Gardner's follow-up book *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction* (2015) — so it is not a pure media fabrication with zero authorial basis, but Tetlock has repeatedly and explicitly pushed back on how the line gets used once detached from context, because the popularized version ("experts are useless, an ape guessing at random does just as well") overstates and misdescribes the actual, narrower finding.
- What the finding actually supports, per multiple secondary accounts of Tetlock's own clarifications: expert forecasters as a *group*, averaged across many questions of the specific type Tetlock studied (long-range political/economic outcomes, several years out, in the forecaster's own stated domain), performed only marginally better than chance-level benchmarks and no better than attentive generalists — but this does not mean individual experts contribute nothing, does not mean expertise is worthless for well-defined near-term technical questions, and does not mean literally that primates would perform equivalently on a representative sample of real-world forecasting tasks. Tetlock's own point (per multiple accounts of his commentary on the line) is that it became "the mother of all soundbites" and got generalized far past what the data show.
- **Recommended chapter-safe formulation:** cite the real, citable finding (expert political forecasters barely outperformed simple statistical models and educated laypeople, across a large, long-running study) as SOLID, and if the "dart-throwing chimp" phrase is used at all, flag explicitly in-text that this is Tetlock's own vivid compression of a narrower finding, not a literal claim that expertise doesn't matter — per CANON's standing instruction not to state a contested/oversimplified empirical claim at higher confidence than the literature supports.
- **CONTESTED / needs Verifier follow-up:** I was not able to locate a primary quotation of Tetlock disavowing the chimp line in his own words with a page citation in this pass (I found consistent secondary paraphrase across several outlets, e.g., business/investing commentary sites, agreeing on the "mother of all soundbites" characterization, but I did not confirm exact wording against a primary source myself). The Verifier should pull the exact page from *Superforecasting*'s introduction before the chapter quotes Tetlock's own gloss on the line rather than just the line itself.

**What Tetlock's study does NOT show, and the brief/chapter should not overstate:** it is a study of long-range political and economic forecasting specifically — not a general finding about all expert judgment, professional judgment in well-structured domains (e.g., weather forecasting, or the doctor/regulator/parent judgments this chapter's opening case turns on), or short-term operational decisions. The relevance to this chapter is about the general unreliability of *individual, in-the-moment, high-confidence exception-making* — not a direct claim that Frances Kelsey-style rule-holding beats expert judgment on the specific kind of question Tetlock studied, which was a different kind of task (probabilistic forecasting of world events, not a single yes/no safety-threshold decision under a legal standard). The chapter should use Tetlock for the "we are bad at knowing when we're right" prediction argument in general terms, not as if it specifically validates any one of the case candidates.

## 4. PARFIT — indirect consequentialism / self-effacingness (page-cited, `Files/Reasons_and_persons_Parfit,_Derek_z_library_sk,_1lib_sk,_z_li.pdf`)

**Source:** Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part I, §§9–11, 14, 17. Page numbers below are the book's own printed pagination, confirmed the same way as the Schelling citations (via the printed running-header page numbers embedded in the extracted text, e.g. "10. HOW CONSEQUENTIALISM IS INDIRECTLY SELF-DEFEATING 25").

**§10, pp. 25–28 — the core "indirect self-defeat" argument, which is the technical name for exactly what this chapter needs (a consequentialist theory that itself recommends NOT calculating case-by-case):**

Parfit distinguishes objective rightness (what would in fact produce the best outcome) from subjective rightness (what it is rational to do given the agent's evidence), using a doctor example directly on-point for this chapter's opening-case domain (p. 25, SOLID, direct quote):

> "It may help to mention a similar distinction. The medical treatment that is objectively right is the one that would in fact be best for the patient. The treatment that is subjectively right is the one that, given the medical evidence, it would be most rational for the doctor to prescribe... A doctor should be blamed for doing what was very likely to kill his patient, even if his act in fact saves this patient's life."

Parfit then defines (p. 27, SOLID, direct quote) a theory as **indirectly collectively self-defeating** "when it is true that, if several people try to achieve their T-given aims, these aims will be worse achieved," and argues consequentialism (C) is indirectly self-defeating in exactly this sense: if everyone tried to act as a "pure do-gooder" — calculating the best outcome act-by-act, with no stable dispositions — the outcome would in fact be worse than if people instead had certain fixed dispositions (loyalty to family, a disposition not to kill, not to deceive) that they follow even in cases where calculation might suggest otherwise. Direct supporting quote (p. 28, SOLID): "Our disposition not to kill should give way only when we believe that, by killing, we would make the outcome very much better." This is Parfit's own version of "have a rule, and set the bar for overriding it very high" — directly usable for the chapter's override/standing-rules distinction, and for the trust argument specifically, since Parfit's reasoning is explicitly about what happens when *everyone* is known to be a pure calculator versus known to have stable dispositions.

**§14, pp. 31–34 — "Blameless Wrongdoing," the Clare example:** Parfit works through a case (a mother, Clare, who prioritizes her own child over a stranger, or in the extended Case Two, over several strangers whose lives she could have saved instead) to argue that a consequentialist can coherently hold that acting on a disposition (parental love) that occasionally produces a worse outcome than pure calculation would have is not blameworthy, provided the disposition itself is one of the best possible dispositions to have *on the whole*, even though it occasionally misfires in a specific case. Direct quote (p. 32, SOLID): "there can be moral immorality, or blameless wrongdoing. In such a case, it is the act and not the agent that is immoral." This is precise, technical support for the brief's "sympathetic rather than villainous" framing of the opening case — Parfit is independently arguing that a person can act wrongly-in-outcome while remaining blameless, provided they were acting on a disposition it would have been wrong to try to override in real time. This maps very cleanly onto whichever opening case is chosen (Kelsey resisting pressure to calculate; or, in the standard direction, someone like Pou acting on a defensible disposition under impossible conditions).

**§17, pp. 40–41 — how consequentialism might be "self-effacing":** Parfit considers, and is skeptical of, but does not fully dismiss, the claim that consequentialism might tell us to stop believing consequentialism and instead believe something like ordinary common-sense/rule-based morality, because believing and acting on the rule-based theory would itself produce better outcomes than believing and acting on the calculating theory. Direct quote (p. 40, SOLID): "If these claims were true, C would be self-effacing. It would tell us that we should try to believe, not itself, but some other theory." Parfit flags the "Government House" worry (from Bernard Williams, via Sidgwick) that a fully self-effacing or esoteric consequentialism — one that tells an elite to privately calculate while the masses are kept believing simple rules — is troubling, and Parfit does not endorse that version. **Chapter-relevant nuance:** Parfit's own considered view (per this section) is that consequentialism is at most *partly* self-effacing, not wholly — he does not conclude that no one should ever calculate, only that the disposition to calculate case-by-case, as a general policy for everyone, is self-undermining. The chapter should not overstate Parfit as fully endorsing "always follow rules, never calculate" — his actual position is closer to the book's own POS-12/CANON §6.1 framing (rules as the *default*, not an absolute), which is good news for fidelity but means the chapter shouldn't cite Parfit as a purer rule-absolutist than he is.

**Confidence:** SOLID throughout this section — all quotations are direct extractions from the PDF in `Files/`, page-verified against the book's own printed pagination.

## 5. SELF-SERVING BIAS IN CASE-BY-CASE MORAL JUDGMENT — the psychology backing for "a calculator can't be trusted"

**Core citable finding, well-established and replicated:** Linda Babcock & George Loewenstein, "Explaining Bargaining Impasse: The Role of Self-Serving Biases," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 11(1), 1997, pp. 109–126. SOLID — this is a well-known, widely cited (thousands of citations), peer-reviewed paper in a top economics journal, and its core finding (people asked to judge a fair/appropriate outcome in a dispute systematically skew their judgment toward outcomes that favor their own side, without being aware they are doing so, and this bias is a documented cause of real bargaining impasse) is not contested in the literature — the mechanism (which specific cognitive process produces it) has been refined by later work, but the basic phenomenon is not in dispute.

**More recent, more directly on-point work (2023–2025, per live search — treat these as SINGLE-SOURCE-strength pending the Verifier locating primary text, since I have abstracts/summaries rather than full papers in this pass):**
- Self-serving bias specifically in *moral character* evaluations (not just fairness/bargaining judgments) has been documented in more recent social-psychology work — people rate an identical act as more or less moral depending on whether they or someone else benefits from it, and this bias does not shrink when people are given more time or cognitive resources to deliberate, which is directly useful for the chapter's argument that the problem with case-by-case calculation is not fixable by "just calculate more carefully" — the bias persists under deliberation, not just under time pressure. CONTESTED at the level of precise effect sizes and exact mechanism (multiple competing accounts — moral identity, greed, self-interest motivation as moderators) — SOLID at the level of the basic phenomenon (self-interest skews moral judgment of one's own conduct, robustly and repeatedly).

**Recommended chapter-safe formulation:** cite Babcock & Loewenstein (1997) as the anchor citation — it is old enough to be extremely well-replicated and precise enough (a named journal, a named effect, a specific field study of real teacher-union/school-board negotiations showing the bias predicts real impasse) to state at SOLID confidence. Use the more recent moral-character-specific findings only as a supporting mention ("more recent work finds the same pattern in judgments of moral character specifically, and finds it persists under deliberation") rather than as a primary citation, since I was not able to pull the primary papers themselves in this pass — flagged for the Verifier.

**Why this belongs in the chapter and what it's for:** this is the empirical leg of the trust argument. The chapter's interlocutor says "surely you should do the thing with the best outcome" — the self-serving-bias literature is the answer to why a population of case-by-case calculators, even ones sincerely trying to calculate the best outcome, will systematically and unknowingly calculate in their own favor, which is exactly why a rule-follower is more trustworthy than a calculator: not because rules are smarter, but because a rule removes the specific decision point where self-serving bias does its damage.
