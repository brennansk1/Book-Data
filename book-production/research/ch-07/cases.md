## What makes a first-person mind-change opening actually work

Three requirements, all necessary, none sufficient alone:

1. **Specificity.** Not "I used to think X, now I think Y" as a summary — a scene. A date or a
   season, a place, who else was in the room, what was said, what the author was doing right before the
   moment the mind changed (arguing the opposite position out loud is the classic and strongest version:
   changing your mind *while making the case for the old view* is more vivid and more credible than
   changing it while reading alone).
2. **Cost.** The change has to have cost something — a friendship strained, a public position walked
   back, an argument lost in front of people whose good opinion mattered, work already done that the
   change makes look wrong or wasted. A mind-change with no cost reads as taste, not conviction, and
   undercuts the chapter's whole point (that holding a position at the right confidence, and being
   willing to revise it, is effortful and expensive, not a rhetorical pose).
3. **The discomfort staying.** Brief's own instruction: "with the discomfort intact." The case fails if
   it resolves too neatly — if it reads as "I was confused, then I saw the light, now I am at peace."
   The version that works still has an edge in it when the author tells it: irritation at how long it
   took, or at who was right, or at what it cost to admit; a residual uncertainty about whether the new
   view is actually correct or just the more defensible one. This is what keeps the chapter's opening
   from being a hedge dressed up as humility — the discomfort is the evidence that the confidence
   conventions the chapter is about to explain are being honored in real time, not just described.

A case that has specificity and cost but not the discomfort reads as a conversion narrative (satisfying,
but wrong genre for this book — CANON §9 item 8 already bans the Oath/conviction-community material from
Volume III, and a too-clean conversion story risks the same tone). A case with discomfort but no
specificity reads as free-floating anxiety, which is exactly the "hedging chapter" the brief prohibits.

## Three placeholder shapes

Each is a *type* of mind-change, not a proposed real story — offered so the Showrunner has a fast way to
say "closer to shape 2" at Gate 1 rather than starting from nothing. **None of these are real; do not
draft from them.**

### Shape 1 — changing his mind about an argument

The author held a position because an argument for it seemed to work, encountered a specific,
nameable objection or counterexample he could not answer, and the position had to give even though he
did not want it to. Structurally the cleanest fit for this chapter, because it maps directly onto the
chapter's actual philosophical content (a hard problem, an illusionist rejoinder, a quasi-realist
retreat are all "an argument stopped working" stories) — the opening case could rhyme with the chapter's
argument without being about the same topic. Risk: if the argument in question is too abstract or
technical, the specificity requirement above is hard to satisfy without the case turning into a second
philosophy lecture before the first one has started. Works best if the *occasion* (where he was, who
he was talking to, what he'd have said the week before) is concrete even if the argument itself is
compressed to a sentence or two.

### Shape 2 — changing his mind about a person

The author judged someone — dismissed them, distrusted them, was certain about their character or
motives — and then had to revise that judgment because of something specific the person did or said, or
because of something the author learned. Strongest on the cost dimension (revising a judgment about a
person usually costs a relationship, an apology, or visible embarrassment in front of others who
witnessed the original certainty) and strongest on discomfort (moral judgments about people are the
ones we're least willing to admit we got wrong, so the residual sting is easy to keep honest). Risk: the
brief's interlocutor is "the reader, who at this point should be suspicious" — a story about
misjudging a person can drift into a redemption narrative for the other person rather than staying
focused on the author's own epistemic failure, which is what the chapter actually needs modeled. Also
carries the most third-party exposure (a real named person's reputation is implicated) — flag for the
Showrunner that this shape, more than the other two, will need the subject's account handled carefully
regardless of whether they're named.

### Shape 3 — changing his mind about a policy or practical question

The author held a practical or policy position (something with real-world stakes, not a pure
abstraction), acted on it or advocated for it, and then reversed after encountering evidence or an
outcome that the old position couldn't accommodate. Strongest structural rhyme with the book's own
project — a book about calibrated confidence revising a practical conclusion is the clearest possible
demonstration of "here's what follows anyway" from the brief, because a policy reversal has visible
downstream consequences that make the "and here's what follows" half of the chapter's structure
concrete rather than abstract. Risk: policy reversals are the easiest of the three to accidentally
narrate as vindication ("and I was right to change my mind, as later events proved") rather than as
live, unresolved discomfort — the Drafter/Showrunner will need to resist the pull toward a
tidy-in-hindsight version.

## Recommendation to the Showrunner (not a ranking — this is not the Case Hunter's call on a first-person
case, per PRODUCTION_BIBLE §2.7)

Shape 1 (an argument) sits closest to the chapter's actual philosophical content and will require the
least narrative real estate to set up, which matters in a 4,000-word chapter that also has to carry
Chalmers, Frankish, Strawson, Blackburn, and the free-will and moral-blindness material. Shapes 2 and 3
are likely to produce a more viscerally effective opening (cost and discomfort come more easily) but
will need tighter cutting to leave room for the rest of the chapter. This is a space-and-shape
observation only — the actual story, and which shape it turns out to resemble, is the Showrunner's to
supply and ratify at Gate 1.
