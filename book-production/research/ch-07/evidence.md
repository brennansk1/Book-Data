## 1. Chalmers — the hard problem, canonical formulation

**Confidence: SOLID.** Source read directly this session: David J. Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem
of Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2(3): 200–19 (1995), via consc.net (the paper is
freely hosted by Chalmers himself).

**The defining passage, as extracted from the primary text:**

> "The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience. When we think and perceive,
> there is a whir of information-processing, but there is also a subjective aspect."

Immediately following, Chalmers poses the question in its sharpest form:

> "Why is it that when our cognitive systems engage in visual and auditory information-processing, we
> have visual or auditory experience: the quality of deep blue, the sensation of middle C?"

And his statement of why this is *the* problem, not *a* problem:

> "If any problem qualifies as *the* problem of consciousness, it is this one."

**The easy/hard distinction**, which the chapter will need to state cleanly: the "easy" problems
(explaining discrimination, reportability, access, integration, attention, the deliberate control of
behavior) are easy *in the sense that we know in principle what would count as an explanation* —
functional/mechanistic accounts. The hard problem persists even after every one of those functions is
fully explained: *why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience at all?* This is
the cleanest one-paragraph statement available and is exactly what the brief needs for "the three
degradation tiers in plain language" to have somewhere to sit.

**Note for the Drafter:** this passage is 30 years old (1995) and is the single most-quoted sentence in
philosophy of mind of the last three decades — it will read as familiar to any reader who has touched
this literature before, which is fine (it's canonical for a reason) but means it should not be presented
as if freshly discovered. Use it as the fixed reference point the chapter's argument pivots on, not as a
revelation.

**Flag for the Canon Keeper / Referee:** CANON §8 states the operating rule under consciousness
uncertainty is "err toward inclusion" — this packet did not find, and was not asked to find, literature
on whether that specific rule is a standard move in the field or a framework-original one. Worth a line
in the chapter distinguishing "the hard problem is real and unsolved" (extremely well-supported, SOLID)
from "therefore erring toward inclusion is the correct practical response" (a further, separate claim
that doesn't come from Chalmers and needs its own justification, presumably from the coordination/
boundary-argument material already established in Ch. 6 — the Drafter should not let the hard-problem
citation do work it isn't doing).

## 2. Frankish — illusionism, stated in his own words (primary source, read directly)

**Confidence: SOLID for every quotation below.** Source: Keith Frankish, "Illusionism as a Theory of
Consciousness," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 23(11–12): 11–39 (2016); author's eprint PDF fetched
and read directly this session (also reprinted as the title essay of K. Frankish, ed., *Illusionism as a
Theory of Consciousness*, Imprint Academic, 2017). Eprint page numbers cited below are the eprint's own
internal pagination (1–6 of the extracted range), which will not exactly match the journal's 11–39
pagination — cite the journal pagination in the manuscript and treat this packet's page numbers as
eprint-internal only.

**His own naming decision and why he rejects "eliminativism"** (eprint p. 1, opening section):

> "Theories of consciousness typically address the hard problem. They accept that phenomenal
> consciousness is real and aim to explain how it comes to exist. There is, however, another approach,
> which holds that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion and aims to explain why it *seems* to exist.
> We might call this *eliminativism* about phenomenal consciousness. The term is not ideal, however,
> suggesting as it does that belief in phenomenal consciousness is simply a theoretical error, that
> rejection of phenomenal realism is part of a wider rejection of folk psychology, and that there is no
> role at all for talk of phenomenal properties — claims that are not essential to the approach... I
> propose 'illusionism' as a more accurate and inclusive name."

This is directly useful and important: **Frankish is explicit that "illusionism" is not the same claim
as flat denial that anything is going on** — a distinction the chapter needs if it wants to state his
position fairly rather than as a strawman ("some philosophers think consciousness doesn't exist" is a
misreading Frankish pre-empts in his own second paragraph).

**The core thesis, stated as cleanly as it gets** (eprint pp. 3–4, §1.2 "Illusionism strong and weak" —
this is the passage to quote):

> "Illusionism makes a very strong claim: it claims that phenomenal consciousness is illusory;
> experiences do not really have qualitative, 'what-it's-like' properties, whether physical or
> non-physical."

And the mechanism, from the same section:

> "Illusionists deny that experiences have phenomenal properties and focus on explaining why they seem
> to have them. They typically allow that we are introspectively aware of our sensory states but argue
> that this awareness is partial and distorted, leading us to misrepresent the states as having
> phenomenal properties."

**What illusionism does NOT deny** (his own caveat, footnote 1, eprint p. 1): "When I talk of phenomenal
properties not being real or not existing, I mean that they are not *instantiated* in our world. This is
compatible with the claim that they exist *qua* properties — a claim which illusionists need not deny."
This is a fine distinction the Drafter should probably not try to reproduce in full — it's the kind of
qualification that reads as evasive in prose even though it's philosophically load-bearing — but the
packet flags it so the chapter doesn't overstate illusionism into eliminativism, which is precisely the
move Frankish spends his opening section warding off.

**Defenders named** (footnote 2): Dennett (pre-eminently), Hall, Humphrey, Pereboom, Rey, Tartaglia; also
Place and Smart as historical antecedents who called phenomenal properties "mythological." Useful if the
chapter wants one more name than Frankish/Chalmers for texture — Dennett is the "powerful defender" the
paper itself names first.

## 3. Strawson's rejoinder — verification result

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED, not SOLID — read this section before quoting anything.** This
packet did not read the raw NYRB HTML; it relied on WebFetch's extraction from
nybooks.com/online/2018/03/13/the-consciousness-deniers/ (two separate fetch passes, consistent results
both times). **Recommend a direct primary-text read before the Drafter uses any of this at quotation
marks rather than close paraphrase.**

**Bibliographic facts, verified:** Galen Strawson, "The Consciousness Deniers," *The New York Review of
Books*, online, March 13, 2018.

**The verification result on the brief's specific ask — "the silliest claim ever made"?** **Confirmed,
close to exact, with one important correction to the brief's framing.** The essay's actual opening line
is:

> "What is the silliest claim ever made? The competition is fierce, but I think the answer is easy."

He then identifies the claim: denial of "the existence of consciousness: conscious experience, the
subjective character of experience, the 'what-it-is-like' of experience" — which he names "the Denial."
He does *not* use the exact string "silliest claim ever made" as a single quotable clause the way the
brief's phrasing implies; the quotable unit is the two-sentence opening above, and the payoff line a few
sentences later is: "Next to this denial ... every known religious belief is only a little less sensible
than the belief that grass is green."

**The important correction: Strawson's named targets in this essay are Dennett and historical
eliminativists (Brian Farrell, Paul Feyerabend, Richard Rorty), not Frankish.** Frankish is not named in
this piece per this session's extraction. **This matters for fair characterization**, because Frankish's
own paper (§2 above) is explicit that illusionism is not flat eliminativism — Frankish would say Strawson
is attacking a cruder position than the one he actually holds. The extraction did surface one line that
closes the gap somewhat: Strawson notes that "most Deniers deny that they're Deniers... 'Of course, we
agree that consciousness or experience exists,' they say — but when they say this they mean something
that specifically excludes qualia" — which is a fairly precise redescription of illusionism as Frankish
defines it (allow the functional/introspective state, deny the phenomenal property). **So the honest
version for the chapter is: Strawson's denunciation is aimed at eliminativism as a family, of which
illusionism is arguably a sophisticated member by Strawson's own lights, even though he doesn't name
Frankish and Frankish would resist being folded into "the Denial" as Strawson characterizes it.** Do not
attribute the "silliest claim" line to a direct attack on Frankish by name — that would overstate what
this session verified.

**Recommended use in the chapter:** Strawson's essay is excellent as the anti-illusionist temperature
check the brief wants (it is genuinely one of the more colorful denunciations in recent philosophy of
mind), but it should be introduced as "here is what a serious philosopher on the other side thinks of
this move" rather than as a direct rebuttal addressed to Frankish's paper — because it isn't one.

## 4. Blackburn — quasi-realism, fair characterization

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED** (SEP is a peer-reviewed, citation-controlled tertiary source,
but this packet reached it through WebFetch's extraction rather than a direct read of the full entry —
treat as strong but not SOLID). Source: *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, "Moral Anti-Realism,"
section on quasi-realism.

**The core move, as characterized:** the quasi-realist "endorses an anti-realist metaphysical stance but
who seeks, through philosophical maneuvering, to *earn the right* for moral discourse to enjoy all the
trappings of realist talk." Concretely: Blackburn's underlying view is expressivist/projectivist — moral
judgments express attitudes, not beliefs that track mind-independent facts, in the classical emotivist
lineage ("Stealing is morally wrong" reduces, at bottom, to something like "Stealing: Boo!"). What
quasi-realism adds is an account of how, starting from that base, ordinary moral speakers are entitled to
go on to say things that sound fully realist — "'Stealing is morally wrong' is true," "It's a fact that
cruelty is wrong," "I know slavery is wrong" — without those speakers making a mistake or being covertly
insincere.

**What it preserves, matching exactly what CANON §2.3 Tier 3 needs:** truth-talk, fact-talk,
knowledge-talk, and (per CANON's own description) enough action-guiding force to carry an "ought." This
is precisely the target CANON assigns Tier 3: "you lose 'constrained by objective reality'; you keep
every institutional and practical conclusion."

**What it costs — and this is the live objection the chapter should be honest about, because
POS-02 already flags it as the framework's own least-demonstrated claim:** the standard worry, per SEP,
is "creeping minimalism" — if quasi-realism successfully earns the right to every sentence a realist
would assert, including "it is a mind-independent fact that stealing is wrong," in what sense is it still
*quasi*-realism rather than realism with extra steps? This is associated with James Dreier's critique
(Dreier vs. Blackburn, already named in POS-02's own vulnerability note) — the objection is that
quasi-realism either stops short of full realist entitlement (in which case it's not clear it can bear
the weight CANON's override architecture puts on Tier-3 "oughts") or it doesn't stop short (in which case
the anti-realist starting point was doing no real work and the position collapses into a semantic
relabeling of realism). **This packet did not find, and was not asked to find, a resolution — POS-02
itself already logs this as unresolved and asks for "a demonstration, chapter by chapter" that a Tier-3
reader finds the material action-guiding. This chapter cannot supply that demonstration in 4,000 words;
it should name the cost honestly (per the brief's "and here's what follows anyway" requirement) without
claiming to have closed the Dreier objection.**

**Recommend citing:** Blackburn, *Ruling Passions* (1998) and/or *Spreading the Word* (1984) as the
primary texts if the Drafter wants a page-level citation; this packet did not read either directly this
session (WebSearch exhaustion prevented locating and fetching either book's text) — **flag: if the
Drafter wants a verbatim Blackburn quotation rather than the SEP paraphrase above, that requires a
follow-up primary-source pass this packet did not complete.**

## 5. Free will — the compatibilist default, one clean framing

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED** (SEP entry on compatibilism, via WebFetch extraction).
Per CANON §8: "Compatibilism is assumed as sufficient for moral practice. This affects judgment more than
evaluation" — brief says keep this light.

**The clean framing, per the brief's instruction to consider Strawson's reactive attitudes:** P.F.
Strawson's 1962 paper "Freedom and Resentment" reframes the free-will question away from the abstract
metaphysical one ("could this act have been otherwise, given determinism?") and toward the practice we
actually run: holding people responsible *is* a matter of the reactive attitudes we sustain toward them —
resentment when we're wronged, indignation on others' behalf, guilt when we're the wrongdoer. The
extracted formulation of Strawson's own claim: "what it is to hold a person morally responsible for wrong
conduct *is* nothing more than the propensity towards, or the sustaining of, a moral reactive attitude
like indignation" (Strawson 1962). **This packet did not verify that exact wording against Strawson's
primary text this session** — flag as needing a verbatim check if quoted directly rather than paraphrased
— but the substance (reactive-attitudes compatibilism, 1962, "Freedom and Resentment") is standard and
well-attested enough to use as paraphrase with confidence.

**Why this is the right one clean framing for a light touch:** it sidesteps the hard metaphysics
entirely (determinism/indeterminism, could-have-done-otherwise) and grounds responsibility in something
the reader already does and recognizes doing — feeling resentment, feeling guilt — which fits the
chapter's job (name the open problem, then show the practical architecture survives it) better than a
Dennett-style "real patterns" or "elbow room" argument would, which requires more setup than 4,000 words
and a first-person opening case can afford. **Dennett is a reasonable second name to drop in passing**
(his compatibilism, e.g. *Elbow Room* / *Freedom Evolves*, is the more famous popularization) but this
packet recommends Strawson-père's reactive-attitudes move as the one the chapter actually explains, for
the economy-of-space reason above.

## 6. Moral blindness — three documented reversals, not overlapping Ch. 6's material

Ch. 6's evidence/cases packets already use the following domains for the boundary-erosion argument and
should not be re-run here: Somerset v Stewart / English abolition, the wider abolition-of-slavery
sequence, women's suffrage (1918 Representation of the People Act), the Indian caste system, and
species/animal-welfare boundary material. **All three candidates below are outside those domains** —
this is not the moral-circle-boundary argument (POS-05) at all; it's a narrower claim CANON §8 needs:
"every past moral framework was blind to something its successors could see."

### (a) Judicial torture as ordinary, legal criminal procedure

**Confidence: REPORTED-AND-CROSS-CHECKED** (Wikipedia's "Judicial torture" article, WebFetch extraction;
this is a widely and consistently attested historical fact, not a contested one, so single-outlet
sourcing here is lower-risk than usual).

Judicial torture was not an aberration or an abuse of early modern European law — it was a **formally
authorized part of ordinary criminal procedure** across continental Roman-canon law jurisdictions,
codified in texts like the *Constitutio Criminalis Carolina* (1532, the Holy Roman Empire's criminal
code) and, later, the *Constitutio Criminalis Theresiana* (1768, Austria), which set out approved methods
in detail. It was considered a legitimate, rational means of securing a confession-based conviction —
Roman legal theory specifically held that certain witnesses (e.g., slaves, and by extension the accused
themselves) required coercion to be trusted. This was mainstream, respectable legal doctrine, endorsed
by the same class of jurists and magistrates the era otherwise credited with wisdom and moral seriousness
— not a fringe cruelty practiced by outliers.

**The reversal:** Cesare Beccaria's *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764) is the standard hinge — it
"denounced the use of torture as cruel and contrary to reason" and catalyzed a wave of abolition across
Europe. A 1816 papal bull forbade the practice in Roman Catholic countries; Portugal abolished it in
1828; the Swiss canton of Glarus, in 1851, is cited as the last European jurisdiction to abolish it by
law. **The clean rhetorical point for the chapter:** an entire continent's legal-moral establishment
treated torture as a rational, defensible instrument of justice for centuries, and then, within about a
century of Beccaria, treated it as self-evidently barbaric — with no new empirical discovery about pain
or personhood driving the change, only an argument.

### (b) Lobotomy and the 1949 Nobel Prize — verified details

**Confidence: SOLID on the core facts** (Wikipedia's "Egas Moniz" article, WebFetch extraction, and this
is a well-documented, uncontested set of facts — Nobel Prize records are a matter of public record).

António Egas Moniz won the **1949 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**, shared with Swiss
physiologist **Walter Rudolf Hess**, "for his discovery of the therapeutic value of leucotomy in certain
psychoses" — i.e., for prefrontal leucotomy, what became known as lobotomy. This was not a fringe
endorsement: it is the single most prestigious prize in medicine, awarded by the same Swedish institution
that vets every other laureate, at the height of the procedure's use. Lobotomy went on to be performed on
an estimated tens of thousands of patients (this packet did not verify a precise global count this
session — flag if the Drafter wants a specific number, that needs a follow-up source) before falling out
of favor **after antipsychotic medication (chlorpromazine et al.) became available in the 1950s**, and it
is now regarded, per this session's sourcing, as "an outdated experimental procedure." Prominent later
critics (Elliot Valenstein, Oliver Sacks) attacked both the science and the ethics; there have been
repeated calls — including from families of patients — to have the Nobel Prize itself rescinded, which
the Nobel Foundation has not done. **Note: this packet did not verify the exact current historical
consensus on how effective/harmful lobotomy actually was in net terms — the chapter's use of this case
should rest on the uncontested fact (the highest institutional endorsement medicine has, followed by
total repudiation) rather than on any specific clinical outcome claim, which this packet did not
verify.**

### (c) Third candidate: eugenic sterilization and *Buck v. Bell* (1927) — not previously proposed by
the brief, offered as the "anything else clean" option

**Confidence: SOLID on the core facts** (Wikipedia's "Buck v. Bell" article, WebFetch extraction; the
vote count, quotation, and dates are matters of public judicial and legislative record).

The U.S. Supreme Court upheld Virginia's compulsory sterilization law **8–1** in *Buck v. Bell* (decided
May 2, 1927), with Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes writing for the majority and closing with the line
**"Three generations of imbeciles are enough."** This was not a marginal ruling — it commanded eight of
nine justices, including several of the most respected jurists of the era, and it reflected a genuine
elite consensus: eugenics was mainstream, progressive, scientifically respectable opinion in the 1920s
American establishment, not a fringe position. In the decision's wake, **more than two dozen U.S. states**
enacted or expanded similar sterilization statutes; documented American sterilizations roughly doubled,
from about 6,000 to more than 12,000, by 1947. **The darkest documented downstream fact:** Virginia's
statute, drafted partly by eugenicist Harry H. Laughlin, directly informed Nazi Germany's 1933
sterilization law, and at the postwar Nuremberg trials, counsel for an SS defendant explicitly cited
*Buck v. Bell* in his defense. The ruling has **never been formally overturned** by the Supreme Court —
legal scholars describe it as having been "distinguished out of existence" rather than reversed — even
though eugenic sterilization laws were effectively abandoned in practice by the early 1960s and Virginia
repealed the law's eugenic language in 1974.

**Why this is the strongest of the three for the chapter's specific purpose:** unlike torture (abolished
by argument) or lobotomy (abolished by a better technology arriving), the *Buck v. Bell* case is the
starkest illustration of CANON §8's actual claim — "every past moral framework was blind to something its
successors could see" — because the consensus was not fringe, was reached by the country's most
authoritative legal body, by a wide margin, using language (fitness, imbecility, the good of the race)
that read as scientific and humane at the time, and the doctrine remains, technically, still good law.
**Flag for the Canon Keeper:** this case sits adjacent to POS-05's "severely disabled" category (moral
patients who cannot reciprocate) — worth a quick check that using it here doesn't collide with how Ch. 6
already deploys the disability/boundary material, though this packet's read is that the two chapters are
making different claims (Ch. 6: the *boundary* around who counts erodes; Ch. 7: the *confidence* with
which a now-repudiated view was held was total) and shouldn't actually compete for the same real estate.
