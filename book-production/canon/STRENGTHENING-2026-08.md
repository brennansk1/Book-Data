# Strengthening Pass — August 2026

Eight changes to the framework itself. Four close vulnerabilities the Position Registry already
names; three fill gaps nobody had flagged; one is a missing chapter's worth of content. Each ends
with what it costs and what it still doesn't solve.

---

## 1. Split value from reasons — the fix for POS-04

**The problem, stated exactly.** POS-04 derives impartiality from the coordination layer: agents
uncertain of their future bargaining position converge on norms counting valence impartially. The
Registry's own vulnerability field concedes the failure: *"an agent confident of remaining durably
advantaged has no obvious reason, on this argument alone, to converge."* Its "what would change my
mind" asks for a demonstration that even durably-advantaged agents have reason for uncertainty.

That demonstration isn't coming, and we should stop waiting for it. Entrenched advantage sometimes
really is durable across any horizon the agent cares about. The derivation as written answers the
egoist who is uncertain and has nothing for the egoist who isn't.

**The fix is to notice the framework has been asking one argument to do two jobs.** Two claims are
being run together:

- **Agent-neutral value:** the badness of a pain is a property of that state, not a relation to
  whoever is inspecting it.
- **Agent-neutral reasons:** that badness gives *any* agent reason to act.

The coordination layer cannot establish the first, and has been strained trying. But the first
doesn't need it. **Phenomenal value realism already delivers agent-neutral value directly.** If the
awfulness is constitutive of the experience rather than conferred by an attitude toward it (POS-01),
then it is in the state. It is not indexed to a viewer, because there is no viewer in the analysis —
that was the entire content of refusing the tracking picture in CK-8. The badness of your pain is not
"badness-for-you"; it is badness, located in a state that happens to be yours.

So the axiom was always doing more work than §3 credited it with. What it does *not* do — and this is
the honest half — is get from value to reasons. "That state is bad" and "you must act on it" are
different claims, and the distinction is well-mapped: Parfit's object-given versus state-given
reasons, Nagel's attempt to bridge exactly this gap in *The Possibility of Altruism*.

**Rebuild the derivation along that seam:**
- The axiom plus phenomenal value realism gives **impartial value**. Others' suffering is bad, full
  stop, and the egoist who denies it contradicts his own phenomenology.
- The coordination layer gives **reasons to act on it** — which is what iterated-game reasoning is
  genuinely good at, and what it should have been asked for all along.

**What this buys against the durably advantaged agent.** He no longer gets to say "there is no view
from nowhere." He has to hold something narrower and much harder: that the badness of suffering
varies with whose it is. And his own epistemology refuses him that. On the acquaintance account he
signed up for in Ch. 5, he *meets* the badness of pain whenever he is in pain — and meets it as a
feature of the state, not as a fact about his standing. He is committed to a proposition his own
phenomenology overturns on a schedule.

**Cost, stated plainly.** This is not a refutation. Sidgwick's dualism survives: an agent can
acknowledge that others' suffering is bad and still decline to treat it as reason-giving for him, and
no one in two and a half centuries has closed that. What changes is the shape of his position — from
a respectable metaphysical claim about perspectives to an unstable psychological one. And the rest of
the book does not wait on it: the institutional arguments run on the coordination layer whether or
not the egoist is ever moved.

**Registry effect:** POS-04 rewritten and split. The value half moves under POS-01 and rises to that
position's confidence; the reasons half stays MODERATE with Sidgwick named as the standing limit.

## 2. Answer the marginal-cases problem — the fix for POS-05

**The problem.** The circle argument says arbitrary boundaries are unstable Schelling points. POS-05's
vulnerability field concedes it works among agents who can "threaten, bargain, or shift coalitions,"
and that beings who can *never* reciprocate exert no such pressure. That is the oldest objection to
every contractarian ethics, and the framework currently inherits it whole.

**The fix — instability on the includers' side, not the excluded's.** The current argument locates
instability in pressure from those outside the line. Relocate it. A boundary has to be *maintained*,
and maintenance is done by the people inside it, using a criterion. That criterion is the weak point:

- Draw the line at reciprocity and it excludes infants, the comatose, the deeply disabled — members
  the in-group will not give up.
- Draw it at species and it is admitted arbitrariness, a line with no property behind it.
- Draw it at any capacity — rationality, language, self-modelling — and the capacity turns out to
  come in degrees, distributed unevenly *inside* the group as well as outside it.

Every historical line has failed in one of these three ways. Not because the excluded pushed, but
because **the includers could not hold a criterion that did the work they needed without cutting into
themselves.** A group that wants to use criteria for anything else cannot indefinitely maintain one
it knows to be either self-cutting or arbitrary.

**A second mechanism: cost asymmetry.** Arbitrary exclusions are expensive to keep. They need
ideology, ritual, policed edge cases, an answer ready for every hard case at the margin. The
inclusive line needs none of that — it has no margin to police. Over long horizons, high-maintenance
equilibria lose to low-maintenance ones under drift alone. That is a structural claim about
persistence, and it does not require the excluded party to have any bargaining power at all.

**What this buys.** The circle argument now reaches permanently non-reciprocal beings, which is
precisely where the framework needs it and where POS-05 admitted it did not go. It also explains the
historical record better: exclusions did not typically end because the excluded won a negotiation.
They ended when the justification became unholdable for the people maintaining it.

**Cost.** This is still a claim about *stability*, not about rightness — see §3 below. And it predicts
erosion over long horizons only; it is consistent with a boundary holding for centuries, which is
what caste shows. Say so where caste is discussed rather than treating the counterexample as handled.

**Registry effect:** POS-05 keeps STRONG; vulnerability rewritten; the new mechanism named as the
load-bearing one.

## 3. Stop letting stability stand in for rightness

The framework repeatedly shows that a norm is a stable equilibrium and then proceeds as if it has
shown the norm is correct. Ch. 4 sharpens this ("if morality is just useful, it isn't binding") and
deliberately declines to answer it; Part Two is supposed to. But §3 of CANON quietly commits the same
slide, and so does the circle argument.

**Fix the grammar of the whole book.** Three distinct claims, never again used interchangeably:

1. **Structural** — this norm is a stable equilibrium. Predictive. No normative content.
2. **Prudential** — you would rationally accept this norm under uncertainty about your position.
   Reaches the uncertain; does not reach the entrenched.
3. **Normative** — suffering is bad irrespective of whose it is. From the axiom plus §1 above.

The impartial norms the book defends are supported by all three, and the book's habit has been to
present whichever is handy as though it settled the matter. It doesn't. State which claim is in play
each time. Where only (1) is available, say that only (1) is available.

**Registry effect:** new POS-23, "The Stability/Rightness Distinction," STRONG, with the note that
this is a discipline the book imposes on itself rather than a substantive position.

## 4. Add a sixth override condition: standing

**The gap.** Five substantive conditions and three procedural gates, and not one of them asks
*is this yours to decide*. As written, the architecture is satisfiable by anyone anywhere who judges
a harm catastrophic, attributable, unavoidable, articulable and untainted by self-interest. That is
the profile of every political assassin who ever wrote a manifesto. The conditions filter the
*reasoning*; nothing filters the *self-appointment*.

**The condition.** Before the substance is consulted, the agent must hold standing in at least one of
three forms:

- **Causal** — you are in the chain. Your hand is on it, or your signature, or your silence.
- **Role** — a position that assigns you the responsibility: the auditor, the inspector, the officer
  in command, the physician of record.
- **Last-actor** — no one better placed is available in the time the harm allows.

Note that the framework's own cases already turn on this and the architecture never said so. Buxtun
worked inside the Public Health Service. Cooper ran internal audit. Thompson was flying over the
village. Their standing is exactly why we read them as conscientious rather than self-appointed, and
why the same acts by a stranger with a strong opinion would read differently.

**What it costs.** It narrows the override, and it will sometimes narrow it wrongly — a bystander
with no standing and complete information is now told to escalate rather than act. That is the
intended trade: the architecture exists because agents are unreliable judges of their own cases, and
"I am the right person to do this" is among the least reliable judgments a person makes.

**Registry effect:** POS-13 becomes six conditions; standing is the first consulted because it is
cheapest to check and most often decisive.

## 5. Moral repair — the missing content

The framework covers rules, override, virtue, development, and institutions. It says nothing about
what a person owes **after** doing wrong. For a book with a whole Part on living the system, that is a
hole where an entire chapter's worth of ethics should be.

It is also a hole the book's own machinery is unusually well equipped to fill. Ch. 2 establishes that
forgiveness is load-bearing: TIT FOR TAT wins partly by forgiving, and FRIEDMAN — permanent
retaliation, never letting go of a defection — finishes dead last among the rules that never strike
first. The game theory says a system needs a route back in. The ethics never says what that route is.

**Repair is that route, and it has four parts:**

1. **Acknowledgment** — naming the act accurately, without the softening that makes it survivable to
   say. Most failed apologies fail here.
2. **Restitution** — undoing what can be undone, in the currency actually lost.
3. **Costly change** — a signal that is expensive and hard to fake, per Ch. 4's own account of how
   signals work. A cheap apology is cheap talk, and everyone can read it as such.
4. **Standing withheld** — and this one is the ethical crux: **the wronged party is not obliged to
   accept.** Forgiveness is a coordination benefit, not a debt owed by the person harmed. A framework
   that told victims they owe forgiveness would be using the group's interest in restored cooperation
   to extract something from the one person who paid for the defection.

**Where it goes.** Ch. 13 (relationships) carries the personal case; Ch. 16's institutional material
gains the public one — what an institution owes after it has harmed someone is the same four parts at
scale, and it is where "we have reviewed our procedures" is exposed as failing part three.

**Registry effect:** new POS-24, "Repair as Re-entry," MODERATE.

## 6. Make the three-axis diagnosis a theory rather than a list

POS-11 names three axes — coordination failure, circle failure, epistemic failure — assigns each a
tool, and stops. A typology with no account of interaction is a filing system. The important cases
are all combinations, and the combinations behave differently from their parts:

- **Coordination success weaponised by circle failure.** The most dangerous configuration there is,
  because the standard tool actively backfires: better incentive alignment among perpetrators
  intensifies the harm. Slavery, cartels, and genocide are coordination *victories* for the in-group.
  Mechanism design applied here makes things worse, and the framework must say so before handing
  anyone the toolkit.
- **Epistemic failure as circle maintenance.** This is what ideology is *for*. Racial pseudoscience
  was not a free-standing error that happened to coexist with slavery; it was the maintenance cost of
  §2's boundary, paid in false belief. Attack the epistemics and the boundary destabilises.
- **Coordination failure obscured by epistemic failure.** The modern default — climate, the attention
  economy, antibiotic resistance. The trap is real and the fog is manufactured, often by the parties
  the trap benefits.

**The diagnostic rule this yields:** identify the dominant axis *before* selecting a tool, because the
tools are not interchangeable and the wrong one is not merely useless. This gives Book II a genuine
contribution — a typology with matched interventions and known failure modes — rather than a
restatement of the coordination literature.

**Registry effect:** POS-11 extended with the interaction cases and the diagnostic rule.

## 7. Upgrade the capability floor from welfare threshold to legitimacy precondition

POS-21 currently rests on prioritarian weighting plus a sufficientarian threshold (CK-1). That is a
welfare argument, and welfare arguments are tradeable at the margin — which is exactly what a floor
is supposed not to be.

**A stronger derivation is available from the framework's own machinery.** The system's central
political claim is error-correction primacy (POS-17): institutions earn legitimacy by being
correctable. Correction runs on people being able to exit, to object, and to hold someone
accountable. Below some threshold of capability, a person can do none of those. They cannot leave,
cannot be heard, cannot impose a cost on anyone.

So for them, the system's legitimating claim is **simply false**. The floor is therefore not a
welfare nicety competing with other goods; it is the precondition of the framework's own account of
why its institutions deserve obedience. A society that lets people fall below it has not made a
distributive error — it has forfeited the argument it was using to justify itself.

This also ties Book V and VI back to Book II rather than leaving the floor as an imported commitment
the "method, not policy" rule (POS-22) is otherwise embarrassed by.

**Registry effect:** POS-21 rewritten with the legitimacy derivation as primary and the welfare
derivation as secondary; confidence rises to STRONG.

## 8. Two smaller repairs

**(a) An aggregation priority rule.** POS-07 (prioritarianism), POS-08 (sufficientarian floor) and
POS-09 (separateness constraints) are stated without a rule for when they conflict — the case the
referee will find first. The rule: **the floor is lexically prior up to the floor; prioritarian
weighting governs above it; separateness constraints bound both.** Then bite the bullet out loud:
this means refusing some large aggregate gains that would leave people below the floor, and the book
should name a case where it refuses one rather than letting the reader assume the structure never
costs anything.

**(b) A bounded doctrine for moral uncertainty.** CK-8 installs expected-value reasoning for
uncertainty about *consciousness*. The same logic generalises to uncertainty about moral claims — but
unbounded, it produces fanaticism: any sufficiently large stake dominates any probability, which is
Pascal's mugging wearing a lab coat. The brake is already in the architecture: expected value informs
deliberation but never by itself clears the override gates. Under moral uncertainty you act on the
best-supported view *within* the standing rules; uncertainty is a reason to widen the option set and
buy information, not to license acts the gates would otherwise stop.

**Registry effect:** POS-07/08/09 gain the priority rule; new POS-25, "Bounded Moral Uncertainty,"
MODERATE.

---

## What this pass does not fix

Population ethics still bleeds — the person-affecting default (POS-10) still pays the non-identity
cost, and §8(a)'s priority rule does not touch it. The no-self problem is untouched and remains the
sharpest live threat to a valence-grounded theory. The axiom is still chosen rather than compelled
(POS-03), and §1 above narrows the egoist's position without closing Sidgwick's dualism. And the
framework is still, as CANON §8 says, blind to something its successors will see.

# Part Two — the three gaps the first pass left open

Written after a research pass. Two of them turn out to have live answers in the literature; the third
gets a genuine improvement rather than a solution.

---

## 9. The no-self problem is support, not a threat (CANON §8)

CANON calls this "the sharpest live threat to a valence-grounded theory": if there is no persistent
subject — Buddhist *anātman*, or Parfit's reductionism about personal identity — whose suffering
matters, and across what boundaries does aggregation run? It has been sitting in the Coda as an
unanswered worry.

**It is answerable, and the answer strengthens the framework's weakest joint.**

**Step one: the axiom never needed a subject.** On phenomenal value realism the badness is
constitutive of the state, not conferred by a self who owns it (POS-01, and CK-8's refusal of the
tracking picture). Strip out the enduring self and what remains is still a state with awful felt
character. The badness does not evaporate for want of an owner; it simply stops being *owned*. If
anything the axiom is cleaner under reductionism, because there is no proprietor left to make the
badness relational — which is the very thing §1 of this pass argued it was never supposed to be.

**Step two — and this is the part worth having: no-self is a premise FOR impartiality.** Śāntideva
argues in the eighth chapter of the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* that the nonexistence of an enduring self is
grounds for committing to impartial benevolence: if the boundary between lives is not the deep thing
it appears to be, the reason for privileging suffering that falls inside *my* boundary weakens with
it. Parfit reaches a neighbouring conclusion from analytic premises in *Reasons and Persons* — that
reductionism gives us less reason for special concern about our own futures.

Note where that lands. The framework's weakest argument is against the durably-advantaged egoist
(§1 above), and his position depends on a robust me/them boundary doing real metaphysical work.
No-self attacks exactly that boundary. **The doctrine filed as the sharpest threat turns out to
reinforce the argument that needed reinforcing most.**

**Two honest costs, both printed.**
- *Don't overclaim the parallel.* Scholars of Buddhist philosophy have specifically criticised Parfit
  for claiming the Buddha would have agreed with him. Cite Śāntideva's argument, which is explicit;
  do not conscript "Buddhism" as a bloc, and do not treat the Parfit/Buddhist convergence as settled.
- *It cuts the other way on the prudential half.* If reductionism weakens special concern for one's
  own future, it also weakens the premise the coordination derivation runs on — an agent who cares
  less about his future bargaining position is less moved by uncertainty about it. So reductionism
  strengthens the normative argument and weakens the prudential one. That is a real internal tension
  and the book should print it rather than take the win quietly.

**Registry effect:** new POS-26, "No-Self as Support," MODERATE. CANON §8 keeps the problem listed but
no longer as unanswered.

## 10. Upgrade the population-ethics position from a bitten bullet to a defended one (POS-10)

CANON currently defaults to a person-affecting restriction "with the costs acknowledged" — which is to
say, it picks a bullet and bites it. That is better than silence and worse than it needs to be, since
the position it picks has a developed defence in the literature it does not cite.

**Adopt a *wide* person-affecting view with conditional reasons.** Narrow person-affecting views take
the non-identity problem full in the face: an act cannot be worse if there is no one it is worse
*for*, so a policy that leaves future people badly off but is a precondition of their existing seems
to wrong no one. Wide views are built to avoid this — they compare outcomes across different possible
populations rather than only within a fixed one, and so can hold that bringing about a very good life
is better than bringing about a merely good one. Johann Frick's machinery of wide-scope conditional
reasons (reasons to confer well-being conditional on a person's existing, plus a selection
requirement) is the version to name.

**Why this fits the framework specifically.** The book already reasons in exactly this shape
elsewhere: CK-8's expected-valence doctrine, and §8(b)'s bounded uncertainty, both handle obligations
that are conditional on a fact not yet settled. Conditional reasons are the same structure applied to
existence. Adopting them is not an import; it is consistency.

**Residual cost, named.** Arrhenius's impossibility results still apply: every population axiology
must accept at least one strongly counterintuitive conclusion, and choosing a wide person-affecting
view chooses which one. The book's job is to say which bullet it has taken and why that bullet is
easier to live with than the repugnant conclusion — not to claim it escaped the theorem.

**Registry effect:** POS-10 rewritten from "default, costs acknowledged" to the wide/conditional
version, confidence held at PROVISIONAL, with the specific counterintuitive implication named.

## 11. The axiom is chosen — but not arbitrarily (POS-03)

POS-03's vulnerability is that a rival axiom (perfectionist, preference-satisfaction, Nietzschean
value-creation) "can be chosen with equal formal legitimacy," and that CANON offers no argument that
the valence axiom is uniquely compelled. That remains true and should stay true; manufacturing a
derivation here would be exactly the overreach the book criticises elsewhere.

**But "not uniquely compelled" is not the same as "arbitrary," and the framework can now say why.**
Section 1 of this pass established that the badness of suffering is met in acquaintance rather than
inferred. That gives the valence axiom an evidential base its rivals do not have:

- **Preference satisfaction** is inferred. Nobody encounters the badness of an unsatisfied preference;
  you notice a preference and then reason that its frustration is bad.
- **Perfectionist value** is judged. Excellence is assessed against a standard, and the standard is
  argued for.
- **Valence** is encountered. The awfulness of pain is not a conclusion you reach about your state; it
  is a feature of the state you are having.

So the choice among candidate axioms is not a coin-toss among equals. It is a choice in which exactly
one candidate has first-person evidence behind it and the others have arguments. That is an
asymmetry, and it is the right size of claim: **an evidential asymmetry, not a logical compulsion.**

**Cost.** It leans entirely on POS-01, so an illusionist or an attitudinal theorist denies the base —
in which case the framework degrades to Tier 2 or Tier 3 (POS-02) and the axiom becomes a choice
among rivals again, which the degradation structure already anticipates. And an agent who simply
prefers a different axiom is not thereby irrational. The asymmetry gives the valence axiom the better
claim; it does not give it the only one.

**Registry effect:** POS-03 keeps AXIOM status; the vulnerability field gains the asymmetry argument
as a partial answer rather than a bare concession.
