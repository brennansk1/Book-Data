# Rival Audit — where the framework stands against the major systems

For each tradition: its strongest living form, where it beats us, where we beat it, and what we have
to change. A system that cannot say where it loses is not a system; it is a brochure.

Written 2026-08-27, after the strengthening pass. Registry effects at the end.

---

## 1. Hare's two-level utilitarianism — the originality threat

**Why this one comes first.** Hare splits moral thinking into an *intuitive* level (simple principles
for daily life) and a *critical* level (explicitly utilitarian reasoning that justifies and overrides
them). That is structurally so close to standing rules plus a gated override that a referee will ask,
reasonably, what we have added. We must answer precisely or drop the originality claim.

**Where Hare beats us.** He got there first, and his account of *why* the intuitive level is justified
— that beings with our cognitive limits and self-serving biases do better following good rules than
calculating — is the same argument we give in Ch. 8. We should say so and stop presenting it as ours.

**Where we beat him, and it is not small.** Hare's two levels are a distinction in *kinds of
thinking*. Ours is a *procedure*. He has no bright-line trigger, no mandatory delay, no documented
post-hoc review, no standing requirement (CK-12). His archangel simply thinks better; his prole simply
thinks less well. Nothing in his architecture treats the agent as an unreliable evaluator of his own
case — which is the entire content of ours.

**And here is the sharp end.** Williams's central objection to Hare is that the bifurcated moral
psychology is *incoherent*: it works only for agents who fail to reflect properly on their own
decision-making, since a prole who understands the critical level can no longer be a prole. That
objection is fatal to Hare and **slides off us**, because our gates are public and procedural rather
than psychological. Every agent can know the entire architecture — the conditions, the gates, the
review — and it still works, because the rule's grip does not come from the agent's failure to think
about it. It comes from a procedure a third party could check.

That is the cleanest statement of our distinctive contribution we have: *the same objection that
breaks the nearest rival passes through us without touching anything.*

**Also:** Hare's archangel/prole split is a cognitive caste system. Ours is one architecture for
everyone; role-relativity (POS-15) tracks standing and capture risk, not intellectual rank.

**Change required:** Ch. 8 and Ch. 10 must name Hare, credit the priority, and state the
procedural-versus-psychological difference plus the Williams asymmetry. Without this the originality
claim is unearned.

## 2. Bernard Williams — the deepest threat

Three separate attacks, and they need separate answers.

**(a) Internal reasons.** Williams holds that a reason must connect to something in the agent's
subjective motivational set; external reasons claims are false. This aims directly at §1 of the
strengthening pass — if he is right, "the badness is there whether or not you care" gives the egoist
nothing.

**Our answer, and it costs us less than it looks.** CK-9 already split value from reasons. We claim
agent-neutral *value* — a metaphysical claim about states, not a claim about anyone's reasons. On
*reasons* we can simply accept Williams's internalism, because the coordination layer is an account of
how the relevant motivations are **built and sustained**: iterated games, institutions, cultivated
virtue, repair. We never needed external reasons. We need agent-neutral value plus motivations that
institutions actually construct — and constructing motivations is what Books II and V are about.
Accepting internalism costs us the fantasy of a knockdown argument against the egoist, which CK-9
already gave up.

**(b) Integrity, and "one thought too many."** Williams's man who calculates whether to save his wife
has had one thought too many; an impartial system alienates agents from the projects and attachments
that make a life theirs.

**We are better placed here than any consequentialism, and should say so.** The standing rules exist
precisely so that you do not calculate in the moment. In Williams's case the override never triggers:
no catastrophic irreversible harm attributable to the rule, no last-resort condition met, and — after
CK-12 — the question of standing does not even arise. You save your wife because that is what a person
with the right firmware does. The architecture was built to prevent the thought Williams objects to.

**(c) The critique of systematising ethics as such.** The deepest one: that the ambition to reduce
ethical life to a theory is itself the error.

**Partial concession, and we should make it openly.** We are not a theory of the good life and should
stop implying we are. We are a method for coordination, error-correction, and decision under known
bias. Where Williams says ethical life exceeds any system, we agree — and point out that our own
architecture encodes the agreement: rules over calculation, virtue over deliberation, an override that
is rare and gated rather than a decision procedure that runs constantly. **A system that says "mostly
don't use the system" is not the target Williams built his objection for.**

**Change required:** Williams gets the longest engagement in the rivals material, and (c) is a
concession, not a rebuttal.

## 3. Scanlon's contractualism — the strongest rival on structure

**Strongest form.** Principles no one could reasonably reject; moral reasoning restricted to
individuals' reasons; **no interpersonal aggregation** — a mass of small complaints cannot outweigh
one grave complaint. This captures the separateness of persons cleanly and without machinery.

**Where Scanlon beats us.** He gets separateness for free, as a structural feature. We get it by
bolting constraints (POS-09) onto an aggregative base and then needing a priority rule (§8a) to
adjudicate. His is more elegant, and elegance is evidence.

**Where we beat him.** Aggregation-blindness has costs he has never fully paid: the numbers problem
(saying "save the many" requires contested machinery), and the implausibility of never letting large
numbers of small harms count for anything. We can say plainly that a policy harming a million people
slightly is worse than one harming one person slightly, and he cannot without strain.

**The honest position: we occupy a deliberate middle** — bounded aggregation. Prioritarian weighting
above a lexically prior floor, with separateness constraints bounding both. We should present that as
a chosen position with costs on both sides, not as obviously correct.

**A convergence worth naming.** Our fourth override condition — articulable to a skeptical third party
who does not share your interests — *is* justifiability-to-each in procedural clothing. We should
credit Scanlon rather than let a reviewer notice it first.

**Change required:** a Scanlon engagement exists nowhere in the current outline. Add it to the rivals
material; add the credit in Ch. 10.

## 4. Dancy's particularism — the direct attack on the architecture

**Strongest form.** Reasons are holistic: a feature that counts in favour here counts against there.
So there are no true general moral principles, and moral knowledge is a matter of judgment and
perception, not rule-application.

**We are unusually well placed, and the reason is instructive.** Particularism is a thesis about the
*metaphysics of reasons*. Our rules do not claim to be true generalisations about reasons. They are
**devices for agents with known biases** — justified instrumentally by bias-resistance, cognitive
economy, predictability, and the fact that a rule-follower can be trusted while a calculator cannot.

So Dancy can be **entirely right** and our architecture is untouched. If anything, holism strengthens
the case for rules: the more context-sensitive the true moral landscape is, the less any agent can be
trusted to read it correctly in the moment under pressure, and the more valuable a commitment device
becomes.

**What it does cost us.** We cannot claim our rules track moral reality. They track *reliability*.
That is a smaller claim than the book sometimes implies, and it should be stated in Ch. 8.

## 5. Korsgaard — the constructivist we are closest to

**Strongest form.** Normativity is grounded in the reflective structure of agency; obligation comes
from the agent's own practical identity and the regress terminates in the value of humanity.

**Where she beats us.** Her regress argument is more developed than ours, and she has an answer to
"why be moral" that does not route through institutions. Ours routes through institutions and does not
close (CK-9).

**Where we differ, and it should be stated as a difference rather than a defeat.** Both regresses have
to stop somewhere. Hers stops at autonomy and the value of rational agency; ours stops at valence, met
in acquaintance (CK-19). **Her terminus excludes anything that cannot reflect. Ours does not.** That is
the practical fork: infants, animals, the deeply cognitively disabled are inside our circle by
construction and inside hers only by extension arguments she has to supply.

**Change required:** the framework must stop describing itself as "constructivist" in any loose sense
(already banned as a label, CANON §9) and mark the Korsgaard difference explicitly.

## 6. Nietzsche — the rival the book has been ducking

**Strongest form.** The valence axiom — suffering matters, and matters negatively — is precisely the
premise he attacks. Suffering can be constitutive of depth, achievement, and value; a morality
organised around minimising it produces diminished people and calls the diminishment virtue.

**The answer we actually have, and it is better than dismissal.** The axiom says suffering *matters*.
It does not say suffering must always be prevented, and it never did. Goods pluralism (POS-06) already
holds that autonomy, competence, connection, understanding and meaning are conditions of sustained
positive valence — which means a life organised solely around the avoidance of pain is, by our own
value theory, a bad life. The framework can distinguish suffering that is a by-product of things worth
doing from suffering as such, and it should, out loud.

**What we concede.** He is right that the axiom is a choice, and CK-19 says so; our reply is the
evidential asymmetry, not a proof. And he is right that systems like ours have a characteristic failure
mode — comfort mistaken for flourishing. That belongs in the Coda as a named blindness.

## 7. Care ethics, Confucian role ethics, and the reciprocity problem

**Care ethics (Held, Noddings)** is right that dependency relations are primary, asymmetric, and
non-contractual, and that a framework built on iterated games will systematically misdescribe them.
Our answer is not to absorb care as a virtue list but to concede the *scope limit*: the coordination
layer explains a great deal and does not explain the relationship between a parent and an infant. The
circle argument (CK-10) and repair (CK-13) carry that territory instead.

**Confucian role ethics (Ames & Rosemont)** holds the self is constituted by roles rather than prior to
them. There is a real convergence we can now claim: CK-12's **standing** condition is a role-based
concept — whether an act is yours to perform depends on the position you occupy. We arrived at it from
the opposite direction (guarding against self-appointment) and landed on relational ground.

**Change required:** both traditions move from "donors" to genuine interlocutors. The donor/opponent
asymmetry — Western systems get adversarial chapters, non-Western ones get mined for parts — reads as
condescension whether or not it is meant that way.

## 8. What we claim, stated so a referee can test it

Not "the best moral system." Four claims, each falsifiable:

1. **Procedural gating of the override.** The nearest rival (Hare) splits *kinds of thinking*; we
   specify a *procedure* — trigger, delay, review, standing — that treats the agent as an unreliable
   judge of his own case. Williams's coherence objection kills Hare's version and does not touch ours.
2. **The three-axis diagnosis with matched tools and known failure modes** (CK-14), including the
   warning that mechanism design makes circle failures *worse*.
3. **The split derivation** (CK-9): agent-neutral value from the axiom, reasons from the coordination
   layer — which lets us accept Williams's internalism without collapse.
4. **The includers' instability argument** (CK-10) for moral-circle expansion, which reaches
   permanently non-reciprocal beings where contractarian arguments do not.

Everything else in the book is synthesis, and we should say so. Four contributions is more than most
systems have.
