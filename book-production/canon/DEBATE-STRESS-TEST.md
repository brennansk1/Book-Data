# Debate stress test — the grounding challenge

Prepared against the strongest form of the theistic grounding objection, as pressed by debaters like
Andrew Wilson: *what are rights actually grounded in?* — with the follow-on claim that a secular
ethics inevitably collapses into preference or emotion, and that an unchanging standard requires God.

Steelman first, because a caricature is useless preparation. **The objection is serious.** Every
justification chain has to stop. If yours stops at something you chose, then when you say "torturing
children is wrong" you are reporting a policy, not a fact, and the person who chose differently has
said something with exactly the same standing. The theist's claim is that a standard external to all
human choosing is the only thing that makes moral disagreement a disagreement about *anything*. That
is a real problem for a great deal of secular ethics, and much of it does collapse when pushed.

This document is about whether ours does. In one place it nearly did.

---

## The exposure we have to fix

CANON §3 and POS-03 say the axiom is **chosen, not derived**. On the page that is scrupulous. In a
live exchange it is a gift to the other side, because "I choose to start from suffering" is
indistinguishable, at conversational speed, from "this is my preference" — which is his entire thesis
about us.

**The fix is not to retreat from the honesty. It is to say the true thing in the right order.**

We are not choosing a value the way one chooses a flavour. Per CK-9 and CK-19: the badness of
suffering is **constitutive of the state**, not conferred by anyone's attitude toward it, and it is
**met in acquaintance** rather than inferred or elected. What is chosen is the decision to *build on*
it — the same sense in which a physicist chooses to build on observation.

**The line, and it should be practised:** *I don't choose that burning hurts. I meet it. What I chose
was to take that seriously instead of explaining it away.*

**Canon change required:** POS-03 keeps AXIOM status and the "not uniquely compelled" concession, but
the framework must stop leading with "chosen." Lead with the acquaintance claim; the choice is what
comes after.

---

## The regress, and why it is symmetric

He will run the regress: what grounds that? And what grounds *that*? His terminates at God.

**The reply is not to out-regress him. It is to point out that his chain stops too.** Ask what makes
God's nature good. Two answers exist and both cost him:

- *It just is — goodness is identical to God's nature.* Then he has a brute terminus he cannot
  justify from outside, which is structurally the same move we make. He has not escaped the regress;
  he has relocated it and named it.
- *There is a standard by which God's nature qualifies.* Then the standard is prior to God and God is
  not the ground.

This is Euthyphro, and the point is not that it refutes him — sophisticated theists have answers, and
we should say so rather than pretend the dilemma is fatal. **The point is that everyone stops
somewhere, so "you have a brute starting point" is not an objection to us specifically.**

Then the real comparison, which is the strongest single move available to us:

> We both stop somewhere. The difference is what you have to do to check my stopping point versus
> yours. Mine you can verify in about four seconds by putting your hand on a hot stove. Yours requires
> accepting testimony about a being nobody in this room can inspect.

That is not a proof. It is an **epistemic asymmetry**, and it is the same asymmetry CK-19 identifies
among rival axioms. It puts the burden where it belongs: not on whether a starting point exists, but
on whether it can be checked.

---

## "Was the Holocaust objectively wrong on your view?"

He will ask a version of this, and the answer must be immediate, unhedged, and **yes**.

The framework can say it and mean it, which many secular positions cannot. On phenomenal value realism
the badness of that suffering is a fact about those states — not about my attitude toward them, not
about my culture's consensus, not about anyone's preference. Had every person alive approved, it would
have been exactly as bad. **We are moral realists about this.** The dial does not move with opinion.

**A distinction that has to be made explicit in canon, because this format exposes it.** The Registry
rates POS-01 at MODERATE confidence. That is confidence in a *metaethical theory* — and it must never
be confused with confidence in a *verdict*. "I am moderately confident the Holocaust was wrong" would
be a catastrophic sentence and a false one. The verdict carries maximal confidence; the calibration
lives in the account of *why*.

**Say it in that order:** the verdict, flatly. Then, if asked, the theory and its confidence level.
Never the reverse.

---

## The evolutionary debunking move, turned around

He may argue that our moral responses are evolved survival heuristics and therefore not truth-tracking.

We already have the answer (CK-8): refuse the tracking picture. There are not two things here — an
evolved attitude and an independent fact it suspiciously tracks. The felt awfulness *is* the badness.
Evolution explains why creatures have states with that character; it does not explain the character
away, any more than explaining why we evolved colour vision explains away the seenness of red.

And note where the argument lands if it works: **it debunks his intuitions too.** Whatever cognitive
equipment he uses to recognise God's goodness came from the same evolutionary history. A debunking
argument that proves too much proves nothing.

---

## "You're borrowing from the Christian worldview"

The presuppositional move. Our answer is empirical and checkable: a person in pain in a society that
never encountered Christianity meets the same badness. The claim that suffering matters appears
independently across traditions with no shared theological source — the Buddhist first noble truth is
the obvious case, and CK-17's use of Śāntideva is a live example of the framework taking an argument
from a non-theistic tradition on its merits.

Acquaintance with your own pain is not a loan from anyone's worldview.

---

## Where he is right, and we concede on the spot

Conceding fast is a strength in this format, and there are two places we must.

**One: we cannot get from value to obligation for an agent who simply does not care.** CK-9 says so
already. Against a genuinely indifferent egoist we have an unstable position to point at, not a
knockdown.

**But the concession is symmetric and should be handed straight back.** Divine command yields
obligation only for someone who already accepts the authority, and "God will punish you if you don't"
is a prudential reason, not a moral one — it has the same shape as a threat. If his answer is instead
that we are obligated because God is good and made us for a purpose, then he is relying on a claim
about goodness that returns us to Euthyphro. **Neither of us closes the gap. One of us says so.**

**Two: the is-ought gap.** We do not cross it. We start with a normative claim and admit it. So does
he — "God's commands are binding" is not derived from any description of God. Symmetric again.

---

## Format discipline

The framework's real liability in a live hostile exchange is not weakness. It is **complexity**. Six
override conditions, three procedural gates, three diagnostic axes, degradation tiers. Under
cross-examination, elaboration reads as evasion, and a long answer to a short question loses the room
regardless of its content.

Three rules:

1. **Never answer a grounding question with architecture.** He asks what grounds rights; the answer is
   the badness of suffering, met directly. The gates and axes are answers to *how do you decide*, not
   *why does it matter*, and offering them at the wrong moment sounds like a man reaching for a manual.
2. **Verdict before theory, always.**
3. **Concessions immediately and without softening.** "That's right, and here's what it costs you too"
   is the strongest available move and the one an opponent expecting evasion is least prepared for.

**The thirty-second version, which should exist and be memorised:**

> Suffering is bad. Not because I disapprove of it — because of what it *is* to be in it, which you
> can check right now without consulting me or anyone else. Everything after that is engineering:
> we're bad at judging our own cases, so we build rules and procedures the way you'd build a bridge,
> and we publish what would prove us wrong. You start somewhere too. The difference is that my
> starting point is one you can put your hand on.

# Part Two — the remaining traps, and the counter-case

## The traps, with answers

**"If only suffering matters, what's wrong with killing someone painlessly in their sleep?"**

The sharpest trap available against a valence-grounded view, and it must not be met with improvisation.

Two answers, and give the first:

1. *The states that never happen.* Killing does not merely avoid pain; it destroys the being who would
   have had decades of experience. The badness is in everything that does not now occur. The person
   exists at the moment of the act, so the reasons in play are not conditional-on-existence reasons
   about merely possible people (POS-10) — they are reasons about someone who is there.
2. *And the rule is load-bearing.* Even in a case constructed so nobody ever knows, a society in which
   the prohibition on killing holds only when discovery is unlikely has no prohibition on killing. This
   is the standing-rules argument doing exactly the work it exists for.

Do not say "it's wrong because of the suffering of the bereaved." That answer is available and it is
bad — it makes killing a hermit permissible, and everyone in the room will see it.

**"Where do rights come from on your view?"**

*Rights are what we build when a protection has to hold even when violating it would pay.* They are
hardened coordination equilibria — deliberately expensive to override, because a protection that bends
whenever someone calculates a benefit provides no protection at all. That is why they are written down,
entrenched, and made costly to amend.

Then concede the real cost before he finds it: on our account rights are contingent on the arrangements
that sustain them, in a way they are not on his. Our reply is that this describes every actual rights
regime — rights fail everywhere institutions fail — and that a metaphysical guarantee that has never
once stopped a determined state is not obviously the more practical article.

**"Is a human worth more than a rat?"**

Careful — the graded answer is correct and sounds monstrous compressed. Lead with the verdict:
*Yes.* Then the reason: a human being has vastly greater capacity for suffering and for the goods that
make a life go well, and moral weight tracks that capacity (CK-8's expected-valence doctrine). Then the
part that is worth saying out loud: *and this is a framework on which a rat's pain is not nothing,
which is a conclusion I'd rather defend than the alternative.*

**"Is someone's pleasure in cruelty a good?"**

No. Two reasons: the victim's suffering is bad in a way the sadist's enjoyment does not offset under
prioritarian weighting; and per the amended POS-06, positive welfare is not a single quantity that a
sadist can top up — the goods that constitute a life going well (connection, understanding) are the
ones cruelty destroys in the person practising it.

**"So a society that tortures for entertainment is just different, not worse?"**

Worse. Flatly. Not different, not culturally situated, not a matter of our norms. The suffering is real
and its badness is not indexed to anyone's opinion, including that society's.

## The counter-case — go on offense here

A purely defensive performance loses even when every answer is right. One line of attack is available
and it is the framework's actual comparative advantage:

**Divine command has no error-correction procedure.**

Not "religious people have been wrong" — that is cheap and he has answers. The structural claim: *when
an interpretation of the standard is mistaken, what is the mechanism by which anyone finds out?* An
unchanging standard mediated by fallible interpreters gives you unchanging confidence, not unchanging
correctness, and the two are easy to confuse from the inside. Every tradition has reversed positions it
once held with certainty, and each reversal happened *somehow* — the question is whether the mechanism
is part of the system or arrives from outside it.

Ours is part of the system: positions carry confidence ratings, named vulnerabilities, and published
conditions under which we would abandon them. The Registry exists so that being wrong is survivable and
detectable.

**The framing to use:** *You have a standard that cannot change and interpreters who cannot be checked.
I have a standard you can check and a procedure for discovering I'm wrong. One of those is built for
beings who make mistakes.*

**Do not overreach.** He can reply that scripture and tradition contain correction mechanisms, and that
is a fair answer deserving a real one. The honest version of our claim is comparative, not absolute:
error-correction is the framework's *primary design goal* and in his system it is a secondary feature.

## The single most important instruction

**Do not try to win the metaethics on stage.** Sidgwick's dualism is unclosed, the axiom is not uniquely
compelled, and neither of those is a defeat in a two-hour exchange — they are defeats only if concealed
and then exposed. The framework's advantage in this format is that it has already written down its own
weak points, so nothing an opponent produces can be a revelation.

Say what is true, concede what is conceded, and hand back the symmetry every time: he has a brute
starting point too, he cannot derive obligation from value either, and his interpreters can be wrong
with no procedure for noticing.
