# Glossary

One definition per term, used everywhere in production. Plain English, one short paragraph max. Confidence and vulnerability ratings for the positions these terms name live in `POSITIONS.md`; this file is for the term only.

---

**Capability floor.** A guaranteed minimum level of real freedoms and functionings — being adequately nourished, housed, educated, and healthy, and able to participate in one's community (Sen, Nussbaum) — that redistribution is meant to secure before other distributive concerns apply. In the framework it acts as a floor below which gains elsewhere don't count for as much. See *sufficientarianism*.

**Circle failure.** One of the three axes of the diagnosis: successful coordination achieved *against* excluded parties, rather than a failure of coordination at all. Slavery, genocide, and cartels are circle failures — coordination victories for the in-group that better incentive alignment would make worse, not better. Addressed with rights, constitutional constraint, and circle-expansion arguments — not mechanism design, which intensifies the harm here.

**Commons.** A resource many people can use but that no one owns and no one can be excluded from, so that individually rational use can add up to collective ruin — overfishing, overgrazing — even when no user is greedy or ill-intentioned. The framework treats well-governed commons (per Ostrom) as the historical norm rather than the exception; ungoverned collapse is the special case, not the default.

**Coordination failure.** One of the three axes of the diagnosis: individually rational choices that produce a collectively catastrophic outcome, even though everyone involved would prefer a different result. Commons tragedies, arms races, and races to the bottom are coordination failures. Addressed with mechanism design.

**Costly signal.** An action or commitment that is credible precisely because it would be irrational to fake — it costs something real, and only an agent whose disposition is genuine would pay that cost. Keeping a promise when it's inconvenient is a costly signal of integrity; the willingness to bear the cost is what makes the promise believable to others.

**Degradation tiers.** The book's answer to metaethical uncertainty: a three-tier structure — phenomenal realism true (Tier 1), illusionism true (Tier 2), strong anti-realism true (Tier 3) — in which the framework's institutional and practical conclusions survive, weakened but intact, at every tier. The claim is that the book's practical content is invariant across the live space of metaethical positions, not that any one metaethical position is proven.

**Epistemic failure.** One of the three axes of the diagnosis: suffering caused by false belief, independent of coordination or circle failure. Addressed with the epistemic commons, free expression, forecasting institutions, and institutional truth-seeking.

**Error-correction primacy.** The design principle that institutions should be judged by how well and how fast they detect and reverse their own mistakes, not by whether they get things right the first time or by raw efficiency. Justifies separation of powers, federalism, subsidiarity, and antifragility as institutional goods in themselves.

**Illusionism.** The metaphysical view (Frankish, Dennett) that there are no phenomenal properties of the kind Tier 1 requires — what feels like an intrinsic, irreducible quality of experience is a representational illusion generated by the brain. Tier 2 of the degradation structure: the framework survives under illusionism by relocating value to representational content that is intrinsically avoidance-generating, with weaker metaphysics but identical normative output.

**Iterated game.** A game played repeatedly between the same parties, or between parties who expect to meet again, as opposed to a one-shot interaction. Repetition is what makes cooperation a stable strategy rather than a one-time act of goodwill, because it creates the *shadow of the future*.

**Mechanism design.** The engineering discipline of building rules and institutions so that individually rational behavior within them produces good collective outcomes, rather than relying on participants' goodwill. The primary tool for *coordination failure*; it makes *circle failure* worse, not better, because it improves incentive alignment among perpetrators too.

**Mode A / Mode B.** Internal labels, not book vocabulary — the book must find English for them (Ch. 8 brief). Mode A names the default decision procedure: following pre-committed rules and cultivated virtues. Mode B names the override: explicit case-by-case reasoning, available only after the three procedural gates and subject to the five substantive conditions. CANON §6.2 already supplies a partial book-facing synonym for Mode B — "the override" (see below); no equivalent synonym yet exists in canon for Mode A.

**Nash equilibrium.** A state of a strategic interaction in which no participant can improve their own outcome by unilaterally changing their choice, given what everyone else is doing — even if the resulting outcome is bad for everyone. Coordination failures are typically stable Nash equilibria: individually rational, collectively regretted, and self-sustaining without outside intervention.

**Person-affecting restriction.** The default position on population ethics: an outcome can only be worse, or better, if it is worse or better *for someone* — for some particular person who exists or will exist — rather than by comparison to a merely possible different population. Blocks Parfit's repugnant conclusion, at the cost of generating its own instabilities across cases involving different possible people.

**Phenomenal value realism.** The book's metaethical position (Tier 1 of the degradation tiers): the badness of suffering is not a further fact added on top of the experience of suffering, but constitutive of what that experience is — to be in pain just is to be in a state whose felt character is bad. Moral epistemology, on this view, is partly a matter of introspective acquaintance with that felt badness rather than inference from evolved evaluative attitudes.

**Prioritarianism.** The view that a given increase in wellbeing matters more, morally, the worse off the person receiving it already is. Distinct from egalitarianism, which cares about the *gap* between people — prioritarianism cares only about how badly off the recipient is. Used to block the utility monster: a monster's marginal gains are weighted down because a unit of its pleasure no longer counts equally against a unit of someone worse off's.

**Quasi-realism.** Blackburn's metaethical position: moral discourse behaves exactly like realist discourse — we say things are really right or wrong, argue about them, treat some views as mistaken — without mind-independent moral facts underlying it. The realist-sounding talk is earned by the internal logic of the practice, not by tracking an external moral reality. Tier 3 of the degradation structure: what the framework falls back on if strong anti-realism is true, combined with the coordination theory.

**Schelling point.** A solution parties converge on without communicating, because it stands out as focal — obvious, salient, or uniquely non-arbitrary — compared to the available alternatives. The circle-expansion argument turns on the claim that "all valence" is the only non-arbitrary, and therefore stable, Schelling point for where the moral circle's boundary sits.

**Shadow of the future.** The expectation that today's interaction with someone will be followed by more interactions with them, which changes what's individually rational to do today. A long shadow of the future — high probability of meeting again — makes cooperation self-enforcing without requiring participants to be good.

**Stag hunt.** A coordination game in which everyone is best off if everyone cooperates (hunting the stag together), but any individual is safer defecting to a smaller sure payoff (a hare) unless confident enough others will cooperate too. Distinct from a prisoner's dilemma: mutual cooperation, once trusted, is stable. The problem is establishing the trust, not resisting the temptation to defect on a cooperator.

**Subsidiarity.** The default that decisions should be made at the lowest level of governance competent to handle them, because local knowledge is destroyed by centralization. Genuine cross-jurisdictional externalities are the recognized exception that pushes a decision upward.

**Sufficientarianism.** The view that what matters most, morally, is that everyone reach an adequate threshold — a floor — of wellbeing or capability, with secondary weight given to further gains above that floor or to gains below it that don't reach it. In the framework this threshold is lexically prior to aggregate welfare maximisation: no amount of gain above the floor outweighs a shortfall below it.

**The gates.** The three procedural checks that must be passed, in order, before the five override conditions (see *the override*) are even consulted: a bright-line trigger check, a mandatory deliberation period, and mandatory documented post-hoc review regardless of outcome. Designed to filter false positives without relying on the agent's in-the-moment self-assessment.

**The override.** The book-facing name (CANON §6.2; internal label "Mode B") for the case-by-case exception to rule-following. Available only after the three procedural gates are passed, and subject to five further conditions: catastrophic irreversible harm from rule-following, harm attributable to the rule, last resort, articulability to a skeptical third party, and absence of self-interest.

**Valence.** The positive or negative felt quality of a conscious experience — how good or bad it feels from the inside. Suffering is negative valence; joy is positive valence. It is what the axiom is about ("the suffering of a conscious being matters") and what phenomenal value realism claims is where badness actually lives.
