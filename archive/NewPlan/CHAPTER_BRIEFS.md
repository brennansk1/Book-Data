# Chapter Briefs

Each brief is the Drafter's contract. Fields:

- **Argument** — one sentence, sayable aloud. This is the chapter.
- **Opening case** — the concrete scene. Case Hunter supplies candidates; Showrunner picks at Gate 1.
- **Interlocutor** — who objects, and their strongest version.
- **Establishes** — what later chapters depend on.
- **Must not** — the specific failure this chapter is prone to.
- **Sources** — Researcher's starting set.
- **Words** — target.

Total ~81,000 words.

---

## Prologue — "The Thing Nobody Chose" · 2,000 w

**Argument:** There are situations where everyone involved is behaving reasonably, everyone hates the result, and it happens anyway — and no moral theory built around good and bad people can explain them.

**Opening case:** Small, real, recent, non-political. Candidates to hunt: an arms race in a professional field (residency application inflation, college admissions test prep, restaurant delivery-app commissions); a fishery collapse with named fishermen; a workplace where everyone stays late because everyone stays late. **Must not be a philosophical thought experiment and must not be climate change or nuclear war** — too large, too politically pre-loaded, and the reader has defenses up.

**Interlocutor:** none yet. The prologue asks; it doesn't argue.

**Establishes:** the book's central question — *why does suffering persist when nobody wants it?* — and the promise that the answer is structural.

**Must not:** preview the book's structure. No "in Part One we will…" Ends on the question.

**Words:** 2,000. **Audio note:** rendered in the part-opening voice, slower.

# PART ONE — THE TRAP

## Ch. 1 — "Everyone Hates This" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** A large share of the world's avoidable suffering comes from situations where individually reasonable choices produce collectively terrible outcomes, and these situations have a precise mathematical shape.

**Opening case:** A commons that actually collapsed, with named people. Newfoundland cod (1992) is strong — a real moratorium, 30,000 people out of work, and fishermen who could see it coming and could not stop.

**Interlocutor:** The cynic: "this is just people being greedy." Answer: the structure produces the outcome even among agents who are not greedy, and the proof is that participants often see the collapse coming and still cannot stop.

**Establishes:** commons tragedy, arms race, race to the bottom, stag hunt as *structures*. The reader should finish able to recognise the shape in their own life.

**Must not:** present a taxonomy. Two structures explained properly beat five listed. No payoff matrices.

**Sources:** Hardin (1968) *and* the standard correction that Hardin's unmanaged commons is not the historical norm; Newfoundland cod literature; Schelling, *Micromotives and Macrobehavior*.

---

## Ch. 2 — "The Shadow of the Future" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** Cooperation doesn't require anyone to be good — it requires the expectation of meeting again, and that expectation can be engineered.

**Opening case:** Axelrod's tournament, told as a story with named entrants. Anatol Rapoport submitting a four-line program that won. The second tournament, where everyone knew and still couldn't beat it.

**Interlocutor:** "So morality is just self-interest with extra steps." Answer honestly: the game theory explains why cooperative dispositions are *stable*, not what makes them *right* — that's Part Two. Flagging the gap here builds trust.

**Establishes:** the four properties (nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear); the shadow of the future; integrity as costly signal.

**Must not:** reduce love and friendship to strategy in this chapter. That's Ch. 13 and it needs more room to avoid sounding monstrous.

**Sources:** Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*; Rapoport; the Christmas truce of 1914 as an illustration of iteration producing cooperation between enemies.

---

## Ch. 3 — "Eight Rules from a Spanish Water Court" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** Communities have been solving these problems for centuries without either markets or states, and the conditions under which they succeed are known.

**Opening case:** The Tribunal de las Aguas in Valencia — a water court that has met on Thursday mornings outside the cathedral for roughly a thousand years, run by farmers, no written records, no lawyers. Then Ostrom arriving to study it.

**Interlocutor:** The economist: "this doesn't scale." Concede substantially — it doesn't scale simply, which is why nesting matters and why some problems need states.

**Establishes:** mechanism design as an engineering discipline; that the market/state dichotomy is false; that solutions must be matched to problem structure.

**Must not:** list all eight of Ostrom's principles. Pick three, explain them properly through Valencia, cite the rest to the notes.

**Sources:** Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*; the Valencia and Murcia huerta literature; a documented Ostrom-style failure, for honesty.

---

## Ch. 4 — "Morality as Technology" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** The rules, reputations, punishments and honour codes that look like arbitrary cultural residue are mostly solutions to coordination problems, invented repeatedly because the problems recur.

**Opening case:** A specific honour norm whose function is legible in hindsight — Appiah's material on duelling's collapse, or the medieval Icelandic legal system, or merchant law among the Maghribi traders.

**Interlocutor:** "If morality is just useful, it isn't binding." This is the hinge into Part Two. Do not resolve it — sharpen it, and say plainly that the rest of the book depends on answering it.

**Establishes:** the functional account of moral institutions; and the *need* for Part Two, by showing that function alone doesn't give you obligation.

**Must not:** overclaim. Some moral content is not coordination technology — that's Axis 2 and Ch. 6. Foreshadow rather than resolve.

**Sources:** Appiah, *The Honor Code*; Greif on the Maghribi traders; Boehm on egalitarian enforcement in small-scale societies.

# PART TWO — THE GROUND

## Ch. 5 — "The Dentist's Drill" · 5,000 w

**Argument:** The badness of suffering isn't a judgment we add to the sensation — it's part of what the sensation is, and that's the one thing solid enough to build on.

**Opening case:** Something physical, specific and non-abstract. A named person's account of unanaesthetised procedure, or the case history of a patient with pain asymbolia — who reports the sensation but not its badness, and whose existence is the sharpest available evidence about what "badness" is doing in the phenomenology.

**Interlocutor:** Mackie, on queerness. Then the illusionist. Both get real hearings.

**Establishes:** phenomenal value realism (CANON §2.1); the degradation tiers (CANON §2.3) stated in plain language.

**Must not:** claim more than the tier structure allows. Must not use the thermometer analogy. Must not say "Constructivist Realism." Must not present three independent grounding layers.

**Sources:** Sinhababu, *Humean Nature*; Kahane on pain and dislike; Mackie, *Ethics*; pain asymbolia case literature (Grahek).

**Audio note:** hardest chapter to keep listenable. Every abstract move needs a physical anchor within three sentences.

---

## Ch. 6 — "Whose Suffering" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** Every line anyone has drawn around the circle of moral concern has eroded, and that's not sentiment — it's because arbitrary lines are unstable equilibria.

**Opening case:** A historically specific boundary being redrawn, told through one person. Somerset v Stewart (1772). Or the first veterinary anaesthesia debates. Or Peter Singer's own account of the moment the animal case became undeniable to him.

**Interlocutor:** The indexical egoist — "your pain is bad-for-you, mine is bad-for-me, there is no view from nowhere." Give this its strongest form; it's the objection the prior draft ducked.

**Establishes:** the split derivation (axiom supplies the currency, iterated game supplies impartiality); the unstable-Schelling-point argument.

**Must not:** shift the burden onto the egoist. That's the failure being corrected. Argue it.

**Sources:** Sidgwick on the dualism of practical reason; Parfit, *On What Matters* vol. 1; Binmore, *Natural Justice*; Singer, *The Expanding Circle*.

---

## Ch. 7 — "What I Don't Know" · 4,000 w

**Argument:** The framework rests on an unsolved problem in the philosophy of mind, and here is exactly how much of it survives if I'm wrong.

**Opening case:** First person. A specific occasion of the author changing his mind, with the discomfort intact.

**Interlocutor:** the reader, who at this point should be suspicious.

**Establishes:** the three tiers as a robustness claim; consciousness, free will, and moral blindness as live open problems; the book's confidence conventions.

**Must not:** be a hedging chapter. Earned uncertainty is a strength; unearned humility is a bore. Every "I don't know" is followed by "and here's what follows anyway."

**Sources:** Chalmers on the hard problem; Frankish on illusionism; Blackburn on quasi-realism.

**Audio note:** the most first-person chapter in the book. This one must be genuinely the Showrunner's voice.

# PART THREE — THE ARCHITECTURE

## Ch. 8 — "The Fence in the Field" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** For brains like ours, committing to rules beats deciding case by case — not because rules are wiser, but because a rule-follower can be trusted and a calculator can't.

**Opening case:** Someone who calculated and got it wrong in a way that is sympathetic rather than villainous. A doctor, a regulator, a parent.

**Interlocutor:** the act-consequentialist: "surely you should do the thing with the best outcome." Answer with the trust argument and the prediction argument, not with intuition-thumping.

**Establishes:** Mode A; Chesterton's fence as an evidentiary claim; rules as commitment devices.

**Must not:** use the terms "Mode A" and "Mode B." Find English for them. The labels are framework-document residue and they're bad in audio.

**Sources:** Chesterton, *The Thing* (1929); Schelling on commitment; Tetlock on expert prediction; Parfit on indirect consequentialism.

---

## Ch. 9 — "The Man at the Door" · 4,000 w

**Argument:** There has to be an exception to any rule, and the trouble is that everyone who has ever done something terrible believed they were in it.

**Opening case:** Not Kant's murderer-at-the-door. Something real: a documented case where someone broke a rule and was right, paired with one where someone broke a rule for what felt like the same reasons and was catastrophically wrong.

**Interlocutor:** the absolutist, who says the exception destroys the rule. Take it seriously — it's close to correct.

**Establishes:** the problem Ch. 10 solves. Ends unresolved, on the question *how would I know?*

**Must not:** resolve it. This chapter's job is to make the reader feel the difficulty.

---

## Ch. 10 — "Three Gates" · 5,000 w

**⭐ The book's most original contribution. Best-written chapter. Drafted second, right after the Prologue, to set the technical register.**

**Argument:** If you can't trust your own judgment about when you're entitled to break a rule, the fix isn't a better test — it's a procedure that runs before you're allowed to apply the test.

**Opening case:** An institutional failure where every individual applied a defensible-sounding exception. A cover-up with a documented paper trail.

**Interlocutor:** two. (a) "This is bureaucracy applied to conscience." (b) The whistleblower: "your gates would have stopped me." Both are strong. (b) is the one that forces the false-negative material.

**Establishes:** the substantive conditions and the three procedural gates, **entirely in prose**; the false-negative side; role-relative thresholds; the sealed-documentation fix.

**Must not:** contain a list. This is the hard test of the no-lists rule and the chapter that proves the method. If the conditions cannot be made to work as prose, the argument isn't clear enough yet.

**Sources:** Milgram; Kahneman on hot-cold empathy gaps; documented whistleblower cases; the moral-mazes literature (Jackall).

---

## Ch. 11 — "Firmware" · 4,000 w

**Argument:** Character is the part of ethics that runs when you don't have time to think, and it can be deliberately built.

**Opening case:** Someone acting well under time pressure, where deliberation was impossible and the response came from training.

**Interlocutor:** the virtue ethicist who says the framework has this backwards — character is prior to rules, not downstream firmware.

**Establishes:** virtues as installed response patterns; humility as keystone; the developmental path from instinct to reflection to habit.

**Must not:** catalogue twelve virtues. Introduce four or five where they do work. The rest go to the notes.

**Sources:** Aristotle, *NE* II; Doris on situationism and the honest reply to it; Annas, *Intelligent Virtue*.

# PART FOUR — THE LIFE

## Ch. 12 — "Tuesday Morning" · 4,000 w

**Argument:** Here is what the whole apparatus actually looks like on an ordinary day, which is mostly: not very much, on purpose.

**Opening case:** A single real day, hour by hour, with three or four small moral moments — none of them dramatic.

**Interlocutor:** "this is exhausting." Answer: it isn't, because Mode A means most decisions aren't decisions.

**Must not:** be a flowchart in prose. It's a day.

---

## Ch. 13 — "Love Is an Iterated Game (And That's Not an Insult)" · 4,500 w

**Argument:** Describing intimacy in game-theoretic terms doesn't reduce it — it explains why the things that feel most unlike strategy, like unconditional commitment, are exactly what a good strategy would produce.

**Opening case:** A long marriage, specific, with a real difficulty in it.

**Interlocutor:** the person who finds this account cold. This is the chapter most likely to lose readers; the objection deserves the most generous hearing in the book.

**Establishes:** care and dependency as the limit of reciprocity — relationships with infants and the dying are not iterated games and the framework must say so plainly.

**Must not:** be smug. No "and that's not a reduction!" beats.

**Sources:** Frank, *Passions Within Reason*; Held or Noddings on care ethics as a genuine corrective.

---

## Ch. 14 — "Building Purpose Without a Script" · 4,000 w

**Argument:** Meaning isn't discovered and it isn't arbitrary — it's constructed under constraints, the way a good building is.

**Opening case:** Someone who built a life around something after losing an inherited framework. Real, named.

**Interlocutor:** the religious reader, given a genuinely respectful hearing. This chapter determines whether a large audience finishes the book.

**Must not:** score points against religion. The framework's case doesn't require it and the prior draft's "ruins" framing was needlessly antagonising.

---

## Ch. 15 — "What the Stoics Got Right" · 4,000 w

**Argument:** Suffering and death are the two things no framework escapes, and the honest position is narrower and more useful than either the Stoic or the modern one.

**Opening case:** A specific death, handled with restraint.

**Must not:** be consoling in a way the argument doesn't license. Grief is not a problem to be dissolved.

**Handling note:** if suicide is discussed at all, it is brief, non-technical, and directs the reader to real support. No methods, no framing that could function as permission. Showrunner writes this passage personally.

# PART FIVE — THE SCALE

## Ch. 16 — "Institutions That Can Be Wrong" · 5,000 w

**Argument:** The point of political design isn't to get the right answer — it's to be able to find out you were wrong and reverse it.

**Opening case:** An institution that caught its own error, or conspicuously failed to.

**Interlocutor:** someone who wants government to be *good*, not merely correctable.

**Establishes:** error-correction primacy; subsidiarity; democracy as removal mechanism rather than oracle; the three-axis diagnosis mapped onto institutional tools.

**Must not:** contain policy conclusions. Method only. Name where Volume II goes further.

---

## Ch. 17 — "Prices Are Sentences" · 5,000 w

**Argument:** Prices carry information no one person has, which is why markets work — and the same fact tells you exactly where they fail.

**Opening case:** A concrete price signal doing something no planner could have done, or a documented planning failure.

**Interlocutor:** two, deliberately symmetric — a socialist and a libertarian, each getting a real objection and a real concession.

**Establishes:** the knowledge problem; the four failure classes; Ostrom as the third option; the capability floor.

**⭐ Must contain the divergence demonstration:** two readers apply the method faithfully, hold different defensible empirical priors, and reach different policy conclusions — followed by what evidence would settle it. **This is the chapter that proves the book is a method rather than a conclusion wearing one.** Nothing else in the book does as much for its credibility with a reader who disagrees politically.

**Must not:** endorse a policy bundle.

---

## Coda — "What This Doesn't Solve" · 3,000 w

**Argument:** Here are the places I think this is weakest, including one where I suspect my own priors did the work.

**Contents:** population ethics; the no-self problem (Buddhist anātman and Parfit's reductionism together — the sharpest live threat to a valence-grounded theory); consciousness; the near-certainty of a blindness we can't currently see; and at least one named place where the author's politics probably shaped the argument.

**Must not:** end on reassurance. The last note is honest, not comforting. This is the chapter that earns the most trust, and it earns it by conceding.

**Audio note:** part-opening voice. Slowest passage in the book.

**Words:** 3,000. Showrunner drafts personally.
