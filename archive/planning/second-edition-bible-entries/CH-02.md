### CH-02: The Map and the Territory — Epistemology as Moral Duty

Core argument: given that suffering matters (Chapter 1, provisionally; fully defended in Chapter 3), seeing reality clearly is not an optional intellectual virtue but a moral obligation, because actions grounded in false beliefs produce unnecessary suffering. Human cognition resists this duty at every level: Kahneman's dual-process model shows a fast, biased System 1 (anchoring, availability, representativeness, and the master bias WYSIATI) that a lazy System 2 mostly rubber-stamps; Simler and Hanson argue self-deception is an evolved strategic feature rather than a bug, serving reputation management over truth; Haidt's elephant-and-rider model shows moral reasoning arriving as post hoc justification for verdicts already reached. Galef's scout-versus-soldier distinction supplies the required posture — hold beliefs as a corrigible map, not a fortress — and Bayesian updating supplies the formal method: calibrated probabilities, revised in proportion to evidence, defended against the charge of subjective priors via its convergence property (bad priors wash out; bad certainties do not). The identity trap, in which belief fuses with self-image and becomes uncorrectable, is named as the deepest obstacle and is answered institutionally by the book's own Position Registry. The planning fallacy is treated as a moral hazard, not a mere management nuisance, since the gap between confident forecasts and honest ones is paid by whoever relied on the forecast.

Opening case: Ignaz Semmelweis and the 1847 childbed-fever mortality gap between Vienna General Hospital's two obstetrical clinics, his cadaverous-particles/handwashing discovery, and his colleagues' rejection of unambiguous data because the implication (that their own hands were killing patients) was unbearable to professional identity — returned to at the close. Integrated cases: the German judges' dice-anchoring study; the Linda problem; Kahneman's own curriculum-project planning fallacy and the 81%-entrepreneur-overconfidence statistic; the premortem technique (Klein). Quotes (Galef, *The Scout Mindset*; Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*; Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*) are all quotes already verified in the prior chapter draft. Featured objection: truth-seeking vs. tact — steel-manned via Taylor and Brown's positive-illusions research (mild self-deception as psychologically adaptive) and answered by distinguishing epistemic duty (never lie to yourself) from the separate, downstream question of tactful communication, governed by the Mode A/B architecture (Chapters 9–10).
