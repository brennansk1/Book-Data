# Chapter 20: Democracy and Its Discontents — Rational Irrationality and Institutional Fixes

---

> *"Democracy is the worst form of government, except for all the others that have been tried."*
> — attributed to Winston Churchill (House of Commons, 1947)

---

## The Question We Have to Face

We have argued that error-correction is the primary virtue of political systems (Chapter 18) and that rights protect the error-correction mechanism from majoritarian attack (Chapter 19). This points toward democracy as the right framework — democracy, after all, is the political system most obviously structured around peaceful error-correction through elections.

But there is a problem. A large body of research shows that voters are systematically irrational about political questions — in ways that do not self-correct through normal market-like mechanisms. Plato worried about it. Madison worried about it. Modern political science has documented it exhaustively. If the voters are not rational, how can democratic outcomes be good? If democratic outcomes are often not good, how can democracy be the best error-correction mechanism?

Our answer: democracy is the best available error-correction system *despite* its many flaws, and the right response is not to discard it but to *design it better* — through specific institutional mechanisms that compensate for the predictable failures of voter rationality.

---

## Caplan's Diagnosis: Rational Irrationality

Bryan Caplan, in *The Myth of the Rational Voter* (2007), advanced a thesis that has become central to how serious political economists think about democracy. His claim, drawing on decades of survey research, is that voters are not merely ignorant about political questions — they are *systematically biased* in ways that conventional economic theory could not predict.

The conventional model assumes that voter errors are random. If voters are ignorant, the argument goes, their errors will cancel out in aggregate, and the "wisdom of crowds" will produce outcomes close to what well-informed voters would choose. This would be true if voter errors were like coin flips — unpredictable in direction. But they are not. Caplan identifies four systematic biases that affect voters across demographic groups and political affiliations:

**1. Anti-market bias.** Voters systematically underestimate the benefits of market mechanisms (prices, profits, trade) and overestimate their costs (inequality, exploitation). When economists and ordinary citizens are asked the same questions about economic cause and effect, economists across the political spectrum are more pro-market than ordinary citizens across the same political spectrum. This is not because economists are ideologically captured — the pattern holds even for economists who are personally left-wing. It is because economists have studied the mechanisms and ordinary voters have not.

**2. Anti-foreign bias.** Voters systematically overestimate the economic harm caused by interactions with foreign countries (trade, immigration, outsourcing) and underestimate the benefits. This is a cognitive tribalism built into human psychology — the tendency to see "us" as victims of "them" when we are actually benefiting from the exchange.

**3. Make-work bias.** Voters systematically confuse "jobs" with "welfare," and underestimate the benefits of productivity-enhancing changes that displace workers. A policy that creates inefficient jobs is often more popular than one that creates prosperity through efficiency, because the jobs are visible and the prosperity is diffuse.

**4. Pessimistic bias.** Voters systematically believe that conditions are worse than they are and getting worse. This is documented across countries, across time periods, and regardless of actual conditions. When asked about the direction of the country, a majority almost always says "wrong direction," even during periods of objective improvement.

Caplan's empirical evidence comes largely from the Survey of Americans and Economists on the Economy (SAEE), which compared responses to identical questions from economists and from the general public. The comparison revealed that on most questions where economists have a clear consensus, ordinary voters systematically disagree — and disagree in predictable directions that align with the four biases above.

The key theoretical move is **rational irrationality**. If being right about politics costs you nothing personally (your individual vote has negligible influence on outcomes), and if holding irrational beliefs feels good (they align with your tribal identity and emotional preferences), then rational irrationality is predictable — people will consume irrational political beliefs the way they consume comfort food, because the costs of error are externalized and the benefits are internalized.

This is devastating for simple populist democracy. If voters' incentives actually push them toward error, just counting votes will not produce good policy.

---

## The Implications

If Caplan is right (and the framework thinks the empirical case is strong), then several things follow:

**1. Simple majoritarianism is not enough.** A government that does whatever the majority wants will often do things the majority itself would not want if it had better information. This is not an elitist claim — it is an empirical observation about the gap between voter beliefs and the actual consequences of the policies voters support.

**2. Democracy still beats the alternatives.** Caplan himself is not an anti-democrat. Dictators face no error-correction mechanism at all, and their biases are unchecked. Democracy is flawed but self-correcting; autocracy is flawed and non-self-correcting. The comparison favors democracy, even with rational irrationality.

**3. Democratic institutions need to be designed around voter irrationality, not around an idealized image of the rational voter.** If voters have predictable biases, we can build institutions that compensate for those biases — that slow down decisions, that delegate technical questions to experts, that introduce friction where snap judgments would otherwise prevail, that use prediction markets to counterbalance popular misperceptions.

**4. The framework's commitment to democracy is conditional, not absolute.** We support democracy because it is the best available error-correction mechanism, not because the vote is always right. This means we can criticize specific democratic decisions without becoming anti-democratic. It also means we can advocate for specific reforms without committing to the idea that the current system is sacred.

## What Democracy Is Good At

Before discussing fixes, we should note what democracy actually does well — because any proposed reform must preserve these functions.

**1. Peaceful leadership transition.** This is democracy's single most important achievement. In democracies, bad leaders can be replaced without revolution or civil war. In autocracies, leadership succession is either hereditary (which produces bad leaders on average) or violent (which is catastrophic). The ability to replace leaders peacefully is not a small thing; it is the foundation of political stability.

**2. Legitimacy.** Democratic decisions, even ones individual citizens disagree with, carry a kind of legitimacy that authoritarian decisions do not. Citizens who lose an election usually accept the outcome and prepare to try again next time. This acceptance is what keeps the system stable between elections. Authoritarian decisions generate resentment without outlet, which either festers or explodes.

**3. Feedback to leaders.** Even when voters are wrong about the details, they are often right about the direction. A leader who is producing widely disliked outcomes will be punished at the ballot box, and this creates pressure to change course. The information content of democratic elections is low on specific policies but high on "is the current direction working?"

**4. Protection against the worst outcomes.** The leaders democracy produces are usually not the best available, but they are also rarely the worst. Catastrophically bad leaders (the Hitlers, the Stalins, the Pol Pots) arise most often in non-democratic or newly-democratic systems where institutional constraints are weak. Mature democracies with strong institutions produce mediocrity more often than disaster, and mediocrity is better than disaster.

**5. Incorporation of diverse perspectives.** Democracies must, over time, accommodate the interests and views of diverse constituencies. This is slow and frustrating, but it means that no single faction can easily impose its preferences on everyone else. This pluralism produces policies that are usually less extreme than any single faction would choose.

These are not trivial achievements. Any democratic reform that would undermine them is a reform the framework rejects, even if it would produce better policy in the short run.

---

## What Democracy Is Bad At

Alongside these strengths, democracies have predictable weaknesses that correspond to Caplan's analysis.

**1. Technical questions.** Voters are systematically bad at questions requiring technical expertise (economics, epidemiology, climate science, foreign policy). The "wisdom of crowds" works for questions where the crowd has roughly accurate intuitions; it fails for questions where the crowd has systematic biases.

**2. Long-term thinking.** Elections happen every 2-4 years. Politicians who focus on long-term problems (infrastructure, fiscal sustainability, climate, pandemic preparedness) often lose to politicians who focus on short-term wins. The feedback loop is short, and policies that pay off after the next election are systematically underrepresented.

**3. Concentrated vs. diffuse interests.** Policies that impose small costs on many people to benefit a few concentrated interests are easier to pass than policies with the opposite structure. The few with concentrated interests organize, lobby, and vote on the issue; the many with diffuse interests do not. This is why agricultural subsidies, tariffs, and occupational licensing tend to accumulate over time.

**4. Anti-foreign and anti-market biases.** When the expert consensus disagrees with popular intuition, popular intuition usually wins. Trade protection, immigration restriction, price controls, and other policies that economists almost universally oppose remain popular because the intuitions supporting them are stronger than the expert analysis rejecting them.

**5. Tribalism and polarization.** Democratic systems can be captured by tribal dynamics, especially with modern media and sorting. When political identity becomes the dominant identity, cross-partisan compromise becomes impossible, and the system either stalemates or lurches between extremes.

**6. Information degradation.** Democratic discourse depends on a functioning epistemic commons — a shared set of facts from which debate can proceed. When that commons degrades (through disinformation, motivated journalism, or attention economies that reward outrage), democratic deliberation degrades with it.

These weaknesses are not reasons to abandon democracy. They are reasons to *design around* them — to build institutions that do the things democracy is bad at through other mechanisms, while preserving the things democracy is good at.

## Institutional Fixes

What fixes are available? The framework endorses several categories of reform, drawn from comparative political science, political economy, and the mechanism design tradition of Chapter 8.

### Category 1: Insulating Technical Decisions

Some decisions require technical expertise that voters cannot be expected to acquire. For these, *democratic authorization of expert decision-making* is appropriate. Voters elect representatives who establish institutions (central banks, regulatory agencies, judicial bodies) that make technical decisions within bounds set democratically.

The classic example is central bank independence. Monetary policy is technically complex. Voters are systematically biased about inflation (they prefer lax money that feels like prosperity in the short run and crushing inflation in the long run). Politicians are tempted to satisfy this preference. An independent central bank, removed from direct electoral pressure, can make technically better monetary policy decisions — and the evidence strongly supports that central bank independence correlates with better inflation outcomes.

The same logic applies to judicial review, scientific advisory bodies, independent agencies, and various technical regulators. The key is that the delegation is *democratically authorized* — voters (through their representatives) decide to create the institution, set its mandate, and retain the ability to change it. This is delegation, not abdication.

The risk: insulation can become unaccountability. An independent central bank can serve private interests rather than public ones. A technocratic agency can become a self-serving bureaucracy. The safeguard is periodic democratic review and the retention of ultimate democratic authority over whether the institution continues to exist.

### Category 2: Futarchy and Prediction Markets

Robin Hanson proposed a more radical reform: *vote on values, bet on beliefs*. This is sometimes called **futarchy**. The idea is that democratic processes decide what outcomes we want to achieve (what we value), while prediction markets decide which policies will achieve those outcomes (what is likely to work).

This separates the two things democracy does badly into components. Voters are reasonably good at expressing values — they can say what they want. They are bad at predicting which policies will deliver those values. By delegating the prediction task to markets (where people bet real money on outcomes), we use the information-aggregation strength of markets on the empirical question while keeping the democratic legitimacy of the values question.

The theoretical promise is considerable. Futarchy exploits the key insight from mechanism design (Chapter 8): people who must stake real resources on their predictions are more honest and better calibrated than people who merely opine. Political pundits face no penalty for being wrong; prediction market participants do. The result is that markets, when they function well, aggregate dispersed information more efficiently than any deliberative or polling mechanism. If we can harness that epistemic power for policy evaluation while retaining democratic authority over the values question — over *what we are trying to achieve* — we get the best of both worlds.

But the failure modes are serious, and intellectual honesty requires naming them. First, **conditional prediction markets confuse correlation with causation**. A futarchic market asks: "What would GDP growth be if we adopt Policy A versus Policy B?" But the market prices reflect traders' beliefs about all the conditions under which each policy would be adopted, not just the causal effect of the policy itself. If Policy A is more likely to be adopted during economic booms, the market will attribute the boom conditions to the policy. Disentangling the causal effect requires counterfactual markets of considerable technical sophistication, and the field has not fully solved this problem. Second, **market manipulation** becomes a political strategy. If policy outcomes are determined by market prices, anyone with enough capital can move the market and thereby determine policy. The defense — that manipulators lose money to more informed traders — works in thick markets but fails in thin ones, and many policy-relevant markets would be thin. Third, **long-term outcomes resist market evaluation**. A market can forecast next quarter's GDP with some reliability. It cannot forecast the effects of an education policy that pays off over twenty years. The further out the horizon, the thinner the market and the less reliable the signal. Most of the policies that matter most — infrastructure, education, climate, institutional reform — are precisely the ones whose payoffs are too distant for markets to evaluate well.

Futarchy is not currently implemented as a governing system anywhere, and these challenges explain why. But the theoretical appeal is significant, and the framework endorses serious experimentation with futarchy-like mechanisms at subnational scales — municipal budgeting, agency evaluation, regulatory review — where the stakes are lower and the feedback loops shorter.

More modestly, *ordinary prediction markets can inform policy debates* even when they don't determine outcomes. A market that forecasts the economic effects of a proposed policy gives voters and officials information they would otherwise lack. Most current democratic processes do not use prediction markets in this way. They should.

### Category 2.5: Quadratic Voting

A distinct but complementary reform comes from the mechanism design tradition: **quadratic voting**, developed most fully by Glen Weyl and endorsed in various forms by Vitalik Buterin and others working at the intersection of economics and institutional design. The core idea addresses a problem that standard one-person-one-vote democracy cannot solve: the *intensity* problem. In a binary vote, the person who barely prefers one option counts exactly as much as the person whose life depends on it. The result is that intense minorities are systematically overridden by indifferent majorities — or, conversely, that intense minorities capture the process through lobbying and logrolling because the formal voting mechanism gives them no way to express their intensity legitimately.

Quadratic voting works as follows. Each citizen receives an equal allocation of "voice credits." To cast votes on any issue, a citizen spends voice credits, but the cost increases quadratically: one vote costs one credit, two votes cost four credits, three votes cost nine, and so on. This means that expressing moderate preference is cheap, but expressing intense preference is expensive. A voter who cares deeply about one issue and little about others can concentrate credits on that issue — but at a cost that prevents any single voter from dominating.

The mathematical elegance is that quadratic pricing is the *unique* cost function that produces socially optimal outcomes under standard welfare assumptions. Linear pricing (one credit per vote) allows the wealthy in credits to dominate. Exponential pricing suppresses intensity too aggressively. Quadratic pricing balances the two: it allows intensity expression while preserving the fundamental equality of the initial credit allocation.

The practical implications are substantial. Quadratic voting could be applied to referenda, to legislative voting, to budget prioritization, to corporate governance. It has been tested in small-scale experiments — the Colorado state legislature used a version internally, and several blockchain governance systems have implemented it — with promising though preliminary results. The framework endorses quadratic voting as a concrete institutional reform worthy of expanded experimentation, particularly for multi-issue decisions where preference intensity matters and where the current system either ignores intensity (producing majority tyranny) or accommodates it through backroom deals (producing opacity and corruption).

### Category 3: Deliberative Mechanisms

Another approach is to improve the quality of deliberation within the democratic process. Citizens' assemblies, deliberative polls, and similar mechanisms bring randomly selected citizens together, give them access to expertise, and ask them to deliberate on specific questions. The results are usually more moderate, more informed, and more consensus-oriented than typical partisan political debate.

Ireland has used citizens' assemblies to address contentious constitutional questions (same-sex marriage, abortion). The results have been notable: deliberative assemblies have produced recommendations that subsequent referenda have ratified, suggesting that when ordinary citizens are given time and information, they can reach reasonable conclusions on even highly charged issues.

The framework endorses expanding these mechanisms. They are not a replacement for mass democracy, but they can handle specific questions better than mass democracy alone. They also provide a counterweight to the Caplan biases — deliberation tends to reduce the impact of quick intuitive biases by forcing engagement with evidence and competing perspectives.

### Category 4: Structural Reforms

Some democratic problems are addressed by structural reforms that change how elections work:

- **Ranked-choice voting** reduces the penalty for third-party voting, allowing genuine competition and reducing the lesser-of-two-evils dynamic.
- **Proportional representation** allows minority viewpoints to have voice in the legislature without requiring geographic concentration.
- **Independent redistricting commissions** prevent gerrymandering that locks in partisan advantages.
- **Automatic voter registration** increases participation, which usually (though not always) produces more representative outcomes.
- **Campaign finance reform** reduces the influence of concentrated wealth on electoral outcomes.
- **Term limits** on legislators reduce incumbency advantages and introduce turnover.

Each of these has tradeoffs. Ranked-choice voting is complex. Proportional representation can produce unstable coalitions. Independent redistricting requires enforcement. Voter registration can be abused. Campaign finance reform can chill speech. Term limits can reduce institutional memory. The framework does not endorse any single reform as universally correct; it endorses the *ongoing work of structural improvement*, evaluated empirically rather than ideologically.

### Category 5: Epistemic Infrastructure

Democracy depends on an informed citizenry, and an informed citizenry depends on functioning epistemic infrastructure: a free press, accessible expertise, shared factual ground, and institutions that combat disinformation. The framework treats this infrastructure as a public good that requires active maintenance.

What does active maintenance look like?

- **Public funding for journalism**, especially local journalism, which has been devastated by the collapse of advertising-based business models.
- **Transparency requirements** for algorithms that shape political information flow.
- **Media literacy education** in schools, so that citizens can evaluate information sources critically.
- **Fact-checking institutions** with credibility across political tribes (not just one).
- **Prediction markets and forecasting aggregators** that provide probabilistic estimates rather than confident punditry.

These interventions are not censorship. They are the construction of infrastructure that improves the quality of democratic discourse without restricting what anyone can say. The framework holds that such interventions are essential — a democracy whose citizens cannot access reliable information is a democracy that cannot function as an error-correction mechanism.

## The Limits of Reform

We should be honest about what reforms can and cannot do.

**Reforms can improve democratic outcomes.** The evidence is clear: some democratic designs work better than others, and the differences compound over time. A well-designed democracy produces systematically better outcomes than a poorly-designed one.

**Reforms cannot make democracy infallible.** No matter how well-designed the system, voters will sometimes make choices that informed observers find bad. This is a feature of democracy, not a bug that can be engineered away. The alternative is not "better democracy"; it is "not democracy." And as we have argued, not democracy is worse over the long run.

**Reforms cannot substitute for political culture.** The most elegant institutional design will fail if the political culture does not support it. Citizens who do not value truth, norms, and compromise cannot be forced into good democracy by institutional mechanisms. The mechanisms can slow the decay but cannot prevent it if the underlying culture fails.

**Reforms must themselves be democratically authorized.** Any reform that bypasses democracy in the name of "fixing" democracy faces a legitimacy problem that usually defeats it. Reforms must be voted in, and they must be voted in by people who understand what they are voting for. This means that serious democratic reform is always a long-term project of education and persuasion, not a technocratic imposition.

## Democracy Within a Plural System

One more consideration. The framework does not treat democracy as the whole of politics. Democracy is one component of a well-designed political system, along with rights (Chapter 19), separation of powers (Chapter 18), federalism, civil society, markets, and the whole apparatus of mechanism design (Chapter 8). Democracy's role is to provide error-correction and legitimacy at the level of ultimate authority. It is not supposed to decide everything.

Rights, for example, are counter-majoritarian — they are precisely the things that democratic majorities cannot override. Independent courts, independent central banks, professional civil services, and scientific advisory bodies are all insulated (to various degrees) from direct democratic control. This is not a betrayal of democracy; it is part of the design. Democracy is the ultimate source of legitimacy, but it is not the operational mechanism for every decision.

The framework's position: **democracy at the top, specialized institutions underneath, rights as constraints, federalism as a layered structure, civil society and markets outside the state entirely**. This complex architecture is harder to describe than simple majoritarianism, but it is what actually produces good outcomes in practice. Any framework that tries to reduce politics to a single principle — whether the principle is democracy, or markets, or expertise — misses what actually works.

## The Counsel

What does all this mean for the reader — for the ordinary citizen living inside a flawed but functional democracy?

**1. Participate, even knowing your individual vote is close to inconsequential.** The reason is not that your vote is pivotal (it isn't) but that participation is the maintenance practice of democratic citizenship. Systems depend on habits, and habits depend on participation.

**2. Inform yourself, while recognizing your biases.** You are Caplan's voter. You have the anti-market, anti-foreign, make-work, and pessimistic biases, calibrated to your tribal affiliation. Knowing this does not eliminate the biases, but it lets you compensate. Read across the political spectrum. Consult prediction markets when they exist. Trust expert consensus on technical questions unless you have specific reasons to doubt it.

**3. Hold your political identity lightly.** We addressed this in Chapter 2 and again in Chapter 15. The identity trap is especially dangerous in politics. If you cannot specify what evidence would change your political views, you are not thinking politically — you are signaling tribal loyalty.

**4. Support the infrastructure.** Subscribe to journalism. Participate in civic organizations. Attend local meetings. Donate to institutions that maintain the epistemic commons. This is boring work, and it is also the actual maintenance of democratic capacity.

**5. Accept that you will sometimes lose.** Democratic politics means losing elections, losing arguments, losing policies you care about. The losers who accept their losses and prepare for the next round are what keep the system stable. The losers who refuse to accept their losses are how democracies die.

**6. Defend the framework itself.** More important than any specific policy is the continued functioning of the democratic error-correction mechanism. A policy you like that is imposed through anti-democratic means is a policy that undermines your ability to defend other policies you like in the future. The framework is the thing that lets you play the game at all; defend it even when it produces outcomes you dislike.

## The Epistocracy Challenge

The strongest contemporary argument against democracy comes not from authoritarians but from Jason Brennan's *Against Democracy* (2016). Brennan argues that if voters are systematically incompetent — as Caplan's evidence suggests — then allowing them to make binding decisions about others' lives is morally analogous to allowing incompetent people to perform surgery. His proposed alternative: **epistocracy**, rule by the knowledgeable, where voting rights or voting weight are conditioned on demonstrated competence.

This is the most dangerous version of the anti-democratic argument because it uses the framework's *own* epistemic commitments against it. If epistemic duty is real (Chapter 2), and if voters systematically violate it (Caplan's evidence), why should the framework defend their right to impose their ignorance on others?

The framework's response: epistocracy fails on three grounds. **First**, the knowledge tests would be set by someone — and whoever sets them has the power to determine who votes, which is the power to determine outcomes, which defeats the purpose. This is regulatory capture applied to the franchise. **Second**, the historical track record of knowledge-restricted suffrage (property requirements, literacy tests, poll taxes) is uniformly terrible — the restrictions have always been used to exclude the already-disadvantaged rather than to improve policy quality. **Third**, democracy's value is not primarily epistemic — it is not defended because voters are right, but because peaceful power transfer, legitimacy, accountability, and error-correction require that losers accept the process. Epistocracy destroys acceptance by telling losers that they are too stupid to participate.

The framework therefore defends democracy while acknowledging that Brennan's diagnosis of voter incompetence is largely correct. The response is institutional reform (insulation, prediction markets, deliberative mechanisms) rather than franchise restriction.

## Summary

1. **Voters are systematically biased** (Caplan's four biases: anti-market, anti-foreign, make-work, pessimistic). Errors do not cancel out in aggregate.

2. **Democracy is the best error-correction mechanism despite these flaws**, not because voters are right. Autocracy does worse over the long run.

3. **Democracy excels at peaceful leadership transition, legitimacy, feedback to leaders, protection against the worst, and incorporation of diverse perspectives.**

4. **Democracy fails at technical questions, long-term thinking, diffuse vs. concentrated interests, certain biases, tribalism, and information degradation.**

5. **Institutional fixes** include insulating technical decisions, futarchy and prediction markets, deliberative mechanisms, structural electoral reforms, and epistemic infrastructure.

6. **Reform has limits.** It can improve democracy but cannot make it infallible, cannot substitute for political culture, and must be democratically authorized.

7. **Democracy is one component of a plural system**, not the whole of politics. Rights, expert institutions, federalism, and civil society all contribute.

8. **The ordinary citizen** should participate, inform themselves, hold identity lightly, support the infrastructure, accept losses, and defend the framework.
