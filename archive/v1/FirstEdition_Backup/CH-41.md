# Chapter 41: Against Moral Relativism

> *"It is one of the commonest of mistakes to consider that the limit of our power of perception is also the limit of all there is to perceive."*
> — C. W. Leadbeater

## The Rival

Moral relativism is the view that moral claims are true or false only relative to some framework — typically a culture, a historical period, or an individual. On this view, there is no view-from-nowhere from which moral claims can be evaluated. "Slavery is wrong" is not simply true; it is true-relative-to-modern-Western-culture and false-relative-to-19th-century-Southern-American-culture. Both cultures have their own moral truths; neither has access to a truth that transcends its framework.

Moral relativism comes in several flavors:

- **Descriptive relativism**: different cultures have different moral beliefs. (True, and uncontroversial.)
- **Metaethical relativism**: moral truth itself is relative — there is no culture-independent moral fact. (Contested.)
- **Normative relativism**: we should not judge other cultures by our own standards, because their standards are as valid as ours. (Contested and often self-defeating.)

The framework is committed to some form of moral realism — not the naive version, but the view that valence is objectively real and that moral claims can be evaluated (at least in part) against it. This puts us in opposition to metaethical and normative relativism.

This chapter argues that relativism fails as a metaethical position, that it fails even more severely as a normative position, but that it captures some legitimate insights that must be preserved in any account of moral epistemology.

---

## Steel-Manning Moral Relativism

Relativism has real appeal, and its defenders are not fools. What makes relativism attractive?

**1. Anthropological evidence of moral diversity.** Different cultures have genuinely different moral practices -- on polygamy, infanticide, property, gender roles, retribution. The diversity is real and runs deep.

**2. The history of moral imperialism.** When powerful cultures have imposed their moral views on others, the results have often been catastrophic. Colonial powers justified brutal exploitation in the name of civilizing "backward" peoples. Missionaries destroyed traditional practices that had sustained communities for centuries. The confidence that one's own culture had the correct morality and others had wrong ones has produced immense suffering. This is a practical argument for humility about one's own moral claims.

**3. The difficulty of moral justification.** When you try to justify moral claims, you end up appealing to other claims that are themselves contested. Every attempt to establish a neutral foundation seems to smuggle in assumptions from a particular culture or tradition. This difficulty is real and should not be minimized.

**4. The evolutionary contingency of moral intuitions.** Our moral intuitions are products of evolution, shaped for reproductive success in particular environments. There is no reason to think they track "moral truth" in any deep sense. They are tools for social life, not receivers for objective moral signals.

**5. The failure of universal moral theories.** Both religious and secular attempts to articulate moral principles that all rational beings should accept have failed to produce convergence. Different traditions persist. Different philosophers reach different conclusions. The lack of convergence suggests that the premise — that universal moral truth exists and can be discovered — may be wrong.

**6. Respect for cultural autonomy.** Each culture has developed its own way of life, its own values, its own solutions to the problems of living together. Outside judgment imposes one culture's solutions on another, which is disrespectful to the autonomy of the second culture. Relativism expresses a kind of humility and respect.

These are serious considerations. Any fair treatment of relativism must engage with them.

## Why Metaethical Relativism Fails

Despite these considerations, metaethical relativism — the view that there is no culture-independent moral truth — fails on several grounds.

### The Self-Refutation Problem

If moral claims are true only relative to a framework, then the claim "moral claims are true only relative to a framework" is itself true only relative to a framework. Either:

**(a) The claim is true absolutely.** But then there is at least one absolute moral truth (relativism), which contradicts the claim itself. Self-refutation.

**(b) The claim is true only relatively.** Then it is true for the relativist's culture but not for the non-relativist's. Non-relativists are entitled to reject relativism as simply not applying to them. The relativist has no standing to argue against the non-relativist.

The relativist might respond: "I'm not making a moral claim; I'm making a metaethical one, and metaethics is outside the relativist's scope." But this is an awkward move. The claim that "moral claims are not objectively true" is, at best, an epistemic claim with strong moral implications (don't judge other cultures, don't try to impose your morality, etc.). The distinction between metaethical and ethical claims doesn't quite do the work that's needed here.

The self-refutation problem is old (it goes back to the ancient Greek sophists) and well-known. Contemporary relativists have responses, but the problem remains a persistent obstacle to strict relativism.

### The Moral Progress Problem

If moral claims are relative to frameworks, then moral progress is unintelligible. "Moral progress" requires that the new framework is better than the old one, but relativism denies any framework-independent standard for "better." On strict relativism, the abolition of slavery was not moral progress -- it was moral change, from one framework to another. Neither is objectively better.

Most people, relativists included, find this hard to accept. The abolition of slavery was a real improvement, not a change in fashion. Women's rights represent genuine progress. Opposition to cruelty is correct, not merely locally fashionable. If relativism is inconsistent with these clear intuitions, that is evidence against relativism.

### The Cross-Cultural Critique Problem

If moral claims are relative to cultures, then cross-cultural critique is incoherent. A relativist cannot consistently say "genocide is wrong" and mean anything more than "genocide is wrong relative to my culture's framework." But this means they cannot coherently criticize cultures in which genocide is accepted. They can report that they disapprove, but they cannot say the other culture is *wrong*.

Most relativists are unwilling to follow this through. When confronted with genocide, slavery, widespread cruelty, or similar practices in other cultures, they want to criticize them — not merely report their disapproval. But their relativism denies them the standing to do so.

The responses are predictable:
- **"I'm only criticizing from within my framework."** This reduces criticism to expression of preference, which is too weak for the moral work we want it to do.
- **"The other culture has internal resources for self-critique."** This is sometimes true, sometimes not. And even when true, it doesn't justify the relativist's own critique; it points to the possibility of critique from within the other culture, which doesn't help the relativist.
- **"Certain minimal standards apply everywhere."** This is smuggling in absolute moral claims after denying they exist. A relativist who is willing to identify minimal universal standards (no genocide, no slavery) has abandoned strict relativism.

The framework's position: **moral criticism across cultures is legitimate, and any view that denies this is wrong**. The abolitionists who criticized slavery were not merely expressing preferences; they were identifying a real moral wrong. A framework that cannot accommodate this observation has failed at something important.

### The Convergence Problem

Relativists emphasize moral diversity, which is real. But they often understate the degree of cross-cultural moral convergence, which is also real. Almost every culture prohibits:

- Arbitrary murder of community members
- Theft from community members
- Lying that damages community members
- Betrayal of trust
- Cruelty for its own sake
- Failure to care for one's children

These are not universal in every detail, and the specific scope (who counts as a "community member") varies. But the structure is remarkably consistent. This consistency is not what we would expect if moral claims were arbitrary constructions of culture. It is what we would expect if there were underlying features of human social life — the realities of coordination, the facts of welfare, the structure of reciprocity — that constrain what functional moral codes can look like.

The framework's position: **the convergence is better explained by moral realism (in our thin version) than by relativism**. Not because every culture perceives moral facts identically, but because functional moral codes are constrained by the realities they must address, and the realities are similar across cultures.

### The Internal Diversity Problem

Relativism tends to treat cultures as unified frameworks, each with its own morality. But cultures are not unified. Within any culture, there is moral disagreement, debate, and progress. Women in patriarchal cultures have argued against patriarchy. Enslaved people have argued against slavery. Religious reformers have argued against corrupted tradition. Internal critique is a constant feature of moral life.

If morality is relative to culture, whose version is "the culture's morality"? The dominant version? The dissenting version? The version that will prevail in a generation? The version that prevailed a generation ago? Relativism has no principled answer, because the framework assumes a unity that does not actually exist.

This internal diversity also suggests that individuals are capable of stepping outside their culture's dominant morality and evaluating it. If that is possible, then the evaluation must be drawing on resources that are not simply internal to the culture. Those resources — whatever they are — provide the foothold for critique that relativism denies exists.

## Why Normative Relativism Fails

Descriptive claims about moral diversity are one thing; normative claims about how we should respond are another. Normative relativism — the view that we should not judge other cultures — has even more severe problems than metaethical relativism.

**1. It is self-applying in strange ways.** If we should not judge other cultures, should we judge cultures that judge other cultures? Should we judge our own culture? The principle seems to require non-judgment, but the principle itself is a judgment, and refusing to judge requires judging that judgment is wrong.

**2. It conflicts with basic moral intuitions.** Genocide, slavery, and severe oppression are wrong wherever they occur. The refusal to say so is not humility; it is failure of moral courage. Historical "non-judgment" of practices like apartheid or Nazi Germany's treatment of Jews would not have been wise restraint; it would have been moral collapse.

**3. It disempowers people within oppressive cultures.** A dissident in an authoritarian state appealing for international support is not helped by "we cannot judge your culture by our standards." They need allies who will say that their oppression is wrong, not relativists who will say that their oppression is valid-relative-to-their-culture.

**4. It confuses humility about specific judgments with denial of judgment altogether.** We should be humble about specific cases where we may lack understanding. We should not assume our first reaction is the right one. We should take seriously the possibility that a practice we don't understand serves functions we don't see. All of these are legitimate forms of epistemic humility. None of them require the stronger claim that judgment is forbidden.

**5. It is often deployed selectively.** Relativists typically criticize their own culture freely while refusing to criticize others -- holding their own society to high standards while exempting others from scrutiny. This is not humility; it is selective permissiveness that follows from no principled position.

The framework's position: **we should be humble about specific judgments but firm about the legitimacy of judgment itself**. The difference between "we should think carefully before concluding that another culture is wrong about X" and "we should never conclude that another culture is wrong" is enormous. The first is correct; the second is morally disastrous.

## The Distinction Relativism Misses

Underlying the relativist position is a failure to distinguish between different kinds of moral questions.

**Questions where cultural variation is legitimate**: many practices differ across cultures, and no universal standard determines the correct answer. Should meals be eaten with forks, chopsticks, or hands? Should marriages be arranged or romantic? Should children live with their parents or in communal groups? Should property be individual or familial? These are questions of custom and style where different answers can all work.

**Questions where universal standards apply**: other practices concern things that no cultural variation should touch. Is it acceptable to torture children for entertainment? Is it acceptable to practice slavery? Is it acceptable to murder dissenters? These questions have answers that transcend cultural variation, and a culture that gives the wrong answer is wrong, regardless of tradition.

The framework's claim: **there is a distinction between these two categories, and the first category is larger than strict universalists admit while the second category is larger than relativists admit**. Both extreme positions fail because they try to apply a single approach to all moral questions. The correct approach distinguishes between questions that admit of cultural variation and questions that don't.

What determines which category a question falls into? The framework's answer: **questions that involve preventing severe harm to conscious beings are in the universal category. Questions that involve styles of flourishing or coordination solutions that work differently in different contexts are in the cultural-variation category**.

This is not a bright line. There are hard cases in the middle. But the existence of hard cases doesn't mean the distinction is empty. Genocide is in the universal category. Spoon vs. chopsticks is in the cultural variation category. Most cases are clearly one or the other.

## What Relativism Gets Right

Despite its failures as a metaethical or normative position, relativism captures important insights that the framework should absorb:

**1. Humility about specific judgments.** When encountering an unfamiliar practice, the first reaction is often wrong. The practice may serve functions we don't see, address problems we don't face, or reflect wisdom we lack. We should investigate before condemning.

**2. Awareness of our own cultural biases.** Every moral position we hold is shaped by the culture we grew up in. This shaping is partly legitimate (tradition often encodes genuine wisdom) and partly distorting (tradition also encodes our culture's prejudices). We should be alert to both.

**3. Respect for cultural autonomy.** Cultures have legitimate interests in governing themselves according to their own traditions, and outside interference has historically produced disasters. The Chesterton principle (Chapter 9) applies to cultures as well as to individuals — don't dismantle what you don't understand.

**4. Recognition that moral knowledge is difficult.** We do not have easy access to moral truth. Our judgments can be wrong, sometimes systematically. This should produce caution in applying universal claims to specific cases.

**5. The plurality of good lives.** There are many ways to live well. Different cultures have discovered different good lives, and the world is richer for the diversity. The framework's welfare pluralism (Chapter 3) incorporates this — the value vector is multidimensional, and different cultures may weight the dimensions differently without any being wrong.

**6. The danger of moral imperialism.** Even when your moral judgments are correct, the project of imposing them on others can be catastrophic. Being right about ethics does not automatically justify coercion, sanctions, or invasion. The question of what to do when you see moral wrongs in other cultures requires its own careful analysis, and it does not reduce to "since we're right, we can do whatever we want."

The framework absorbs these insights into its broader position. The result is a form of moral realism that is modest, epistemically careful, and respectful of cultural diversity while still able to make and defend substantive moral judgments when necessary.

## The Framework's Alternative

The framework's position on cross-cultural moral evaluation:

**1. Moral claims can be evaluated against objective features of reality** — specifically, valence (does the practice produce or reduce suffering?) and coordination (does the practice solve or create coordination problems?). These are not culture-relative.

**2. Most moral practices have multiple functions**, and understanding them requires careful study rather than quick judgment. Before concluding that a practice is wrong, investigate what problem it is addressing and what would happen without it.

**3. Some practices are wrong regardless of culture**. These are practices that clearly cause severe suffering without corresponding benefit, or that clearly violate the welfare of conscious beings in ways the culture's own thoughtful members would recognize. These judgments can be made across cultures without imperialism.

**4. Cultural variation is legitimate and valuable** in areas where multiple solutions can work. Diversity in these areas enriches human experience.

**5. Cross-cultural critique should come with epistemic humility.** Criticism is legitimate, but it should be offered with awareness of one's own limitations and the possibility of being wrong.

**6. Imposition of moral views is a separate question.** Being right does not justify coercion. Even correct moral judgments usually should be expressed through persuasion, example, and dialogue rather than through force.

**7. Progress is possible.** Cultures can and do move toward better moral positions, as ours has (and still must). Progress is measured by reduction in suffering, expansion of flourishing, improvements in coordination, and refinements of understanding. These are real even if they are slow and contested.

This position differs from both strict universalism and strict relativism. It is not middle-of-the-road splitting; it is principled engagement with the specific content of moral questions. Where universal standards apply, we apply them. Where cultural variation is legitimate, we respect it. The work is in distinguishing the cases, and this requires attention, empathy, and sustained investigation.

## The Hard Cases

To test the framework's approach, consider some hard cases:

**Female genital mutilation.** Practiced in various cultures, defended by some as traditional. The framework's analysis: it causes severe and lasting physical harm, reduces welfare on multiple dimensions, and the "benefits" claimed for it are either empty or can be achieved through other means. It is wrong. Saying so is not cultural imperialism; it is recognizing a clear harm. The harder question is how to address it, which requires sensitivity to local context.

**Arranged marriage.** Practiced in many cultures, often stably. The framework's analysis: arranged marriage can be voluntary and successful, depending on how it is practiced. It is not intrinsically wrong. Forced marriage (where consent is not given or is coerced) is wrong. The category "arranged marriage" covers both, and blanket condemnation is a mistake.

**Polygamy.** Legal in some cultures, illegal in others. The framework's analysis: polygamy is not intrinsically wrong, but specific forms of it can be — particularly forms that involve significant inequality between spouses or exploitation. The question is not "polygamy yes/no" but "what conditions make polygamous arrangements work well or badly?"

**Honor killings.** Practiced in some cultures as a response to perceived family dishonor, often targeting women. The framework's analysis: this is wrong. The welfare costs (to the victims) are severe and the "benefits" (family honor) are not benefits in any real sense — they are about social appearances and face-saving, not about genuine welfare. Cultural defense of honor killings is defense of a practice that cannot be justified under the framework's criteria.

**Communal ownership.** Some cultures have less individualistic property arrangements than the modern West. The framework's analysis: this is in the category of legitimate cultural variation. Communal arrangements can work well (Ostrom's case studies from Chapter 8). Individualistic arrangements can work well. Neither is universally correct; the choice depends on specific circumstances.

These examples show that the framework's approach produces specific answers. Some practices in some cultures are wrong. Some are legitimate variation. The determination depends on the specific content of the practice and its effects, not on a blanket rule that either all traditions are valid (relativism) or all must conform to Western norms (crude universalism).

## Summary

1. **Moral relativism** comes in descriptive (true), metaethical (contested), and normative (problematic) forms.

2. **Metaethical relativism fails** on self-refutation, moral progress, cross-cultural critique, convergence, and internal diversity grounds.

3. **Normative relativism fails even more severely** — it is self-applying strangely, conflicts with basic intuitions, disempowers dissidents in oppressive cultures, confuses humility with denial of judgment, and is typically deployed selectively.

4. **The distinction relativism misses** is between questions that admit of cultural variation (many) and questions that have universal answers (some). Both extreme positions err by trying to apply a single rule to all cases.

5. **Relativism gets real things right**: humility about specific judgments, awareness of cultural bias, respect for cultural autonomy, recognition of the difficulty of moral knowledge, plurality of good lives, and the dangers of moral imperialism.

6. **The framework's alternative** is modest moral realism that distinguishes between universal and variable questions, applies criticism with epistemic humility, separates judgment from coercion, and recognizes the possibility of moral progress.

7. **Hard cases** test the framework by requiring specific judgments. The framework produces defensible answers through careful engagement with the specific content of practices.
