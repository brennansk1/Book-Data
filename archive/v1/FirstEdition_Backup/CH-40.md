# Chapter 40: Against Pure Deontology

---

> *"Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."*
> — Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*

---

## The Rival

If utilitarianism says only consequences matter, deontology says duties matter independent of consequences. Some actions are forbidden regardless of what good they might produce. Some actions are required regardless of what bad they might produce. The right and wrong of an action is determined by its conformity to moral rules, not by its results.

The greatest exponent is Immanuel Kant, whose *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) provides three formulations of the fundamental moral law:

**1. The Universal Law Formulation:**

> *"I ought never to act except in such a way that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law."*
> — Kant, *Groundwork*, AK 4:402

**2. The Humanity Formulation:**

> *"So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means."*
> — Kant, *Groundwork*, AK 4:429

**3. The Kingdom of Ends Formulation**: act as if you were through your maxim a lawmaking member of a kingdom of ends.

These are not identical but are meant to express the same underlying principle. Kant's view: morality consists in rational agents legislating for themselves and all other rational agents, applying principles that can be universalized and that respect the autonomy of each person. The foundational commitment of the whole *Groundwork* is that a good will — willing from duty — is the only thing with unconditional worth:

> *"The good will is not good because of what it effects or accomplishes... it is good through its willing alone, that is, it is good in itself."*
> — Kant, *Groundwork*, AK 4:394

This is a serious moral tradition with distinguished contemporary defenders (Christine Korsgaard, Onora O'Neill, Barbara Herman, Allen Wood). It is not merely "rules for rules' sake" — it has deep philosophical justification and practical power. The framework must engage with it seriously, as we did with utilitarianism.

---

## Steel-Manning Kantian Ethics

What does Kant get right?

**1. Morality is about more than consequences.** Some actions are wrong independently of their outcomes. A utilitarian who would lie to save 100 lives might be doing the calculation correctly, but most people think there is something wrong with lying even when it produces good results. Kant captures this — lying is a violation of respect for persons, regardless of what it achieves.

**2. Persons are not merely receptacles of welfare.** Utilitarianism aggregates welfare across persons in ways that dissolve individual boundaries. Kant insists that each person has intrinsic dignity that cannot be traded off against collective welfare: "rational nature exists as an end in itself. The human being necessarily represents his own existence this way" (*Groundwork*, AK 4:429). Persons are self-legislating agents whose autonomy commands respect, not containers of welfare to be emptied and refilled as the aggregate demands. The framework's commitment to individual rights (Chapter 19) shares this insight.

**3. Moral reasoning should be universalizable.** If an action is right for you in circumstance X, it should be right for anyone in circumstance X. This captures the intuition that morality is not about special pleading for your own case. The framework's emphasis on consistency (from Chapter 2) owes something to this.

**4. Motives matter, not just actions.** Kant distinguished between doing the right thing and doing the right thing *because* it is right. A person who refrains from lying only because they fear getting caught has not acted morally; a person who refrains because they recognize lying as wrong has. This seems right — we intuitively value moral motivation, not just moral compliance.

**5. Moral knowledge is available through reason.** You do not need religious revelation to know that lying is wrong, that promises should be kept, that persons should be respected. Careful reflection on what rational agents would will reveals moral truth. This makes morality accessible to all rational beings regardless of their specific traditions.

**6. Autonomy is central.** Kant treats the autonomy of rational agents as the source of moral authority. You do not obey morality because someone commands it (Chapter 37); you obey because as a rational being you recognize the moral law as your own. This grounds morality in the agent rather than in external authority.

**7. Some actions are absolutely prohibited.** Pure utilitarianism allows any action if the consequences are good enough. This produces the monstrous conclusions we examined in Chapter 38 — torture, transplant surgery, sacrificing the few for the many. Kant rules out such actions categorically, which aligns with widely shared moral intuitions.

These are serious strengths. The framework has absorbed several of them. The question is where we differ from pure Kantianism and why.

## The Classic Objections to Kant

Kant's framework faces several serious objections:

### The Problem of Conflicting Duties

Kant's moral rules, taken strictly, can conflict with each other. The classic example: someone asks you where your friend is, and you know they intend to harm your friend. Kant's prohibition on lying is absolute — you cannot lie even to a murderer at the door. But refusing to lie might mean betraying your friend to their attacker, violating your duty of care.

Kant himself, in a famous essay titled "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropic Concerns," held that you cannot lie even in this case. This conclusion strikes almost everyone as clearly wrong. You should lie to the murderer. The question is whether Kant's framework can accommodate this intuition.

Responses Kantians offer:
- **Kant was wrong about this specific case.** Some Kantians distinguish between Kant's explicit views and the best version of his theory. The theory, correctly applied, might allow exceptions.
- **You can refuse to answer.** Remaining silent is not lying, and Kant can endorse it. But this only works if the murderer lets you remain silent; if you must choose between speaking truthfully and lying, the option has been removed.
- **The perfect/imperfect duty distinction.** Kant distinguished perfect duties (which are always binding, like don't lie) from imperfect duties (which leave room for judgment, like help others when you can). Conflicts between perfect duties are rare because perfect duties are narrowly specified. But conflicts between perfect and imperfect duties can be managed.

The framework's response: **the case shows that absolute rules fail in edge cases, and the framework's dual-process architecture (Chapters 9-10) handles this better**. Mode A says don't lie by default. Mode B allows override when the conditions are met. The Nazi-at-the-door case clearly meets the Mode B conditions (catastrophic harm from rule-following, directly attributable, no alternatives, publicly articulable, self-interest absent). Kant's absolutism fails this case; the framework's architecture doesn't.

### The Emptiness Objection

Hegel accused Kant of "empty formalism." The Categorical Imperative tells you to act only on universalizable maxims, but almost any maxim can be universalized if you describe it carefully. "Lie whenever convenient" cannot be universalized (it would undermine the practice of truth-telling), but "Lie when necessary to prevent murder" can be. The test alone does not determine which maxims are right; you need substantive judgments about which descriptions are relevant.

Kantians have responded with refinements -- the Humanity Formulation, distinctions between maxim types, attention to motives. But the objection persists. The framework's position: **the Categorical Imperative is a useful heuristic but not a complete decision procedure**. It works well for clear cases but leaves substantial judgment required for hard ones, which suggests that the framework alone is incomplete.

### The Content Problem

A related objection: where do Kant's specific duties come from? Kant thought he could derive them from pure rational reflection, but most readers find his derivations unconvincing. The prohibition on lying, for example, depends on reasoning about what a rational agent could coherently will — but the argument depends on specific descriptions of what counts as "lying" and what counts as "universalizing" that are not themselves derived from pure reason.

In practice, Kantian duties track widely shared moral intuitions rather than being genuinely derived from reason alone. This is not necessarily bad — intuitions have some evidential weight — but it undermines Kant's claim that his system provides a uniquely rational foundation that other systems lack.

The framework's response: **we are honest about this**. The framework's moral conclusions draw on multiple sources: the valence axiom (which is an explicit choice), game-theoretic analysis, empirical evidence about what produces flourishing, and moral intuitions (which are data but not foundations). We do not claim to derive everything from pure reason. Kant's claim that he did is implausible, and his framework is stronger if reframed as "articulating and systematizing moral intuitions that are available to rational reflection."

### The Motive-Only Problem

Kant's insistence that motives are what matter (the "Good Will" is the only thing that is good without qualification) has an odd implication: **if you do the right thing for the wrong reasons, you have acted wrongly**. Someone who tells the truth because it serves their interests, not because they recognize truth-telling as right, is not acting morally on Kant's account.

This seems wrong in the opposite direction. We care about outcomes and actions, not just motives. A doctor who saves a patient for financial reasons still saved the patient; the outcome matters. A firefighter who runs into a burning building out of habit rather than explicit moral reflection still saved lives.

Responses Kantians offer:
- Kant is not denying the value of outcomes; he is saying that only the good will is *unconditionally* good. Outcomes can be good in other senses.
- The good will matters for *moral* evaluation, not for all evaluation.

Fair enough, but the emphasis on motive over outcome produces a theory that seems to care about the wrong thing — how people feel about their actions rather than what their actions do. The framework disagrees: **motives and outcomes both matter**, and a theory that privileges one over the other is incomplete.

### The Demand for Impartiality

Kant's universalization requirement demands that you treat relevantly similar cases similarly. If it is wrong for someone else to do X in circumstance Y, it is wrong for you. This seems right as a general principle.

But applied strictly, it conflicts with reasonable partial obligations. You have stronger duties to your own children than to other children. You owe more to your friends than to strangers. You have special obligations to your country, your community, your promises. Pure universalization seems to deny these partial obligations or to force them into awkward shapes.

Kantians have addressed this through various moves — distinguishing different kinds of duties, allowing special relationships as morally relevant features, introducing "agent-relative" permissions. These moves work to some extent but pull the theory away from its strict universalist core.

The framework's response: **partial obligations are real, and they are not a deviation from morality; they are part of how moral life works**. The framework includes them explicitly (Chapter 14 on relationships) without claiming they are derived from universalization.

## What the Framework Takes from Kant

Despite these objections, the framework owes significant debts to Kantian ethics:

**1. The dignity of persons.** The framework's rights chapter (Chapter 19) builds on the Kantian insight that persons cannot be treated merely as means. Human dignity is not a quantifiable welfare property; it is a structural feature of moral personhood.

**2. Rules as Mode A defaults.** Our argument for rule-based default moral reasoning (Chapter 9) is Kantian in spirit, even if the justification is different. Kant says rules because rational beings legislate for themselves; the framework says rules because rule-following is bias-resistant, coordinable, and predictable. The conclusions overlap substantially.

**3. Universalizability as a check.** We use "what if everyone did this?" as a test for moral reasoning, even though we don't treat it as the only test. Kant was right that universalization catches certain failures; we absorb the test while not making it foundational.

**4. The concept of moral autonomy.** The idea that moral agents legislate for themselves — that morality is not imposed from outside but recognized as one's own — is preserved in the framework's insistence that moral commitments must be internalized through reflection, not merely obeyed (Chapter 12).

**5. The categorical prohibition on treating persons as mere means.** While we do not treat this as an absolute rule in the Kantian sense, we treat it as a very strong default that should be overridden only in the most extreme cases. The transplant case, for instance, is forbidden in the framework, just as it is forbidden for Kant.

**6. Moral motivation as important.** The framework cares about character (Chapter 11) as well as action. A person who does right things for the wrong reasons is missing something, even if the right things get done.

## Where the Framework Differs

The differences are significant:

**1. Rules are not absolute.** The framework allows Mode B overrides under strict conditions. Kant (in his strict version) does not. This is the biggest disagreement, and the framework believes it produces better answers in hard cases.

**2. Consequences count.** Not as the only consideration, but as a real consideration. The framework is not willing to follow the Categorical Imperative off a cliff when the consequences are catastrophic. A moral theory that cannot accommodate "the cost of this rule application is too high" will fail in extreme cases.

**3. The foundation is different.** Kant grounds morality in practical reason and the autonomy of rational agents. The framework grounds it in valence and coordination. These can converge on similar specific conclusions but the foundations are distinct.

**4. Intuitions are data, not derivations.** The framework is explicit that moral intuitions provide evidence but are not themselves the basis of moral conclusions. Kant claims his results are derived from reason alone, which the framework finds implausible.

**5. Welfare matters.** Kant treats welfare as largely secondary to the good will. The framework treats welfare (understood as the complex value vector) as central. You cannot have a defensible moral theory that is not oriented toward welfare.

**6. Partial obligations are real.** The framework allows stronger duties to people in specific relationships. Kant's pure universalizability struggles with this.

**7. The motive-only view is rejected.** The framework cares about what people do, not just why they did it.

---

## Contemporary Kantian Responses

Serious contemporary Kantians (Korsgaard, O'Neill, Herman) have developed more sophisticated versions of Kant's theory that address many of these objections. Their versions:

- Allow more room for consequence-sensitivity while preserving rule-based structure.
- Accommodate partial obligations through specific relationships.
- Recognize that motive is one factor among several.
- Provide more content to universalization through substantive claims about rational agents.

These developments are genuine improvements on Kant's original formulation. The framework is closer to sophisticated contemporary Kantianism than to 18th-century Kant. In many specific cases, a sophisticated Kantian and a framework advocate would give the same answer, reached through similar reasoning, with different terminology.

As with utilitarianism, convergence from multiple traditions on similar answers is evidence that the answers are tracking something real. The framework's distinctive contribution is integrating insights from multiple traditions while being explicit about the foundations and method.

## The Verdict

The framework rejects pure Kantianism while absorbing much of what it gets right:

**What we reject**:
- Absolute rules that cannot be overridden under any circumstances
- Derivation of morality from pure reason alone
- Primary emphasis on motive over outcome
- Strict universalizability that denies partial obligations
- The claim that consequences are largely irrelevant to moral evaluation

**What we absorb**:
- Dignity of persons as a non-negotiable feature of moral thinking
- Rules as the normal structure of moral practice
- Universalizability as a useful (though not sufficient) test
- Moral autonomy as the basis of moral authority
- Strong prohibitions on treating persons as mere means
- The importance of moral motivation

The framework's dual-process architecture is, in one sense, an attempt to combine the strengths of Kantian and utilitarian thinking. Mode A (rules) captures the Kantian insight that moral life works through rule-based defaults. Mode B (calculation) captures the consequentialist insight that outcomes matter in extreme cases. The combination preserves what each tradition gets right while avoiding what each tradition gets wrong.

This is, again, not a dismissal of Kant. Kant is one of the two or three most important moral philosophers in history, and the framework owes him more than to any thinker except perhaps Aristotle (whose virtue ethics we address in Chapter 42). The disagreement is specific and substantive, and the framework's position is better than pure Kantianism, but the debt is real.

---

## Summary

1. **Kant's strengths** include the dignity of persons, universalizability, moral autonomy, categorical prohibitions, and the recognition that motive matters.

2. **The classic objections** (conflicting duties, emptiness, content, motive-only, partiality) point at real problems with strict Kantianism.

3. **The framework's responses** involve the dual-process architecture (rules as default, calculation as override), treating intuitions as data rather than derivations, caring about consequences alongside motives, and allowing partial obligations.

4. **The framework absorbs** Kantian insights about dignity, rules, universalizability, autonomy, and prohibitions on treating persons as mere means.

5. **The framework differs** from Kant on the absoluteness of rules, the role of consequences, the foundation in valence rather than pure reason, the status of intuitions, and partial obligations.

6. **Contemporary Kantians** have developed sophisticated versions that are closer to the framework's position than Kant's original formulation. Convergence from multiple traditions is evidence for the framework.

7. **The verdict** is that pure Kantianism is rejected but most of its substance is preserved. The disagreement is specific but the debt is significant.
