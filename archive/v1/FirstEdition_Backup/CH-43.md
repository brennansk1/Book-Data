## The Tradition

Virtue ethics is older than utilitarianism or deontology. Its canonical source is Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, but the tradition includes the Stoics, Confucius, Aquinas, and contemporary thinkers like Alasdair MacIntyre, Philippa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse, and Michael Slote.

The central claim of virtue ethics: **the primary question of ethics is not "what should I do?" but "what kind of person should I be?"** Virtues (honesty, courage, justice, temperance, practical wisdom, and others) are character traits that constitute a good life. Right action is what a virtuous person would do. Moral theory should focus on developing these character traits rather than on formulating rules or calculating consequences.

This is a serious tradition with deep roots and strong contemporary defenders. It captures something important that both rule-based and consequence-based ethics can miss: the recognition that *who we are* matters morally, not just what we do or what results we produce.

The framework has absorbed significant elements of virtue ethics — Chapter 11 developed the twelve virtues of rationality, and Chapter 12 addressed moral development as the formation of character. In that sense, the framework is partially virtue-ethical. But we also diverge from pure virtue ethics in important ways, particularly from its Aristotelian and MacIntyrean forms, which depend on teleological claims about human nature that the framework rejects.

This chapter identifies where the framework agrees with virtue ethics, where it disagrees, and why the synthesis it offers is an improvement on the pure virtue-ethical position.

## Steel-Manning Aristotelian Virtue Ethics

Aristotle's argument, compressed: **humans have a natural function (ergon), and flourishing (eudaimonia) consists in fulfilling that function well**. The *Nicomachean Ethics* opens with the claim that all human activity aims at some good:

> *"Every art and every inquiry, and similarly every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good; and for this reason the good has rightly been declared to be that at which all things aim."*
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, I.1 (1094a)

The function of a knife is to cut; a good knife cuts well. The function of a doctor is to heal; a good doctor heals well. The function of a human is to reason well and act in accordance with reason, within the context of a polis (political community). Virtues are the character traits that enable humans to fulfill this function. The result is Aristotle's definition of the human good:

> *"The Good of man is the active exercise of his soul's faculties in conformity with excellence or virtue, or if there be several human excellences or virtues, in conformity with the best and most perfect among them."*
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, I.7 (1098a)

From this foundational claim, Aristotle derives:

**1. Virtues are discovered, not invented.** They are features of human flourishing given our nature, not arbitrary choices. Courage, honesty, justice, temperance, and practical wisdom (phronesis) are the virtues because they are what humans need to flourish, not because they happen to be valued in specific cultures.

**2. Virtue is habituation.** We become virtuous by practicing virtuous acts, not by intellectual acceptance of moral principles. Character is formed through repeated action that eventually becomes second nature.

**3. Practical wisdom is central.** Unlike rule-based ethics, virtue ethics does not provide algorithms. Instead, it requires practical wisdom — the capacity to perceive what the particular situation calls for. The practically wise person (phronimos) knows how to act rightly in specific circumstances, not because they are applying a rule but because they have developed the perception and judgment that virtue requires.

**4. The virtues form a unity.** You cannot have courage without practical wisdom (or your "courage" is just recklessness). You cannot have justice without temperance. The virtues reinforce and require each other. A person who is honest but lacks other virtues is not fully virtuous.

**5. Eudaimonia is the goal.** Not pleasure, not preference satisfaction, not rule-following — the full flourishing of the human being as the kind of creature they are. Eudaimonia is "activity of the soul in accordance with perfect virtue" (*NE* I.7), and it must be assessed over a complete lifetime — as Aristotle memorably puts it, "one swallow does not make a spring" (I.7, 1098a). This is more objective than utilitarian welfare (because it is grounded in human nature) and more concrete than Kantian principles (because it points at a specific kind of life).

**6. Ethics is political.** Virtues are developed and exercised in communities. A person in isolation cannot be fully virtuous, because virtues like justice and friendship require others. The polis is the natural context of human flourishing, and the good state is one in which citizens can develop and exercise their virtues.

This is a rich and compelling vision. It captures insights that other moral traditions miss, particularly about character, community, and the role of practical wisdom in navigating complex situations.

## MacIntyre's Contemporary Revival

Alasdair MacIntyre, in *After Virtue* (1981), mounted the most influential contemporary defense of virtue ethics, and we engaged with his critique in Chapter 11. Briefly:

MacIntyre argued that modern moral philosophy has lost the teleological framework that made classical virtue ethics coherent. Without a shared conception of the human good — without a telos — modern moral discourse becomes incoherent, reducing to emotivism (Stevenson's view that moral claims are just expressions of preference). The way forward, MacIntyre argued, is to recover virtue ethics embedded in practices (activities with internal standards of excellence), narratives (stories that give unity to a life), and traditions (ongoing communities of inquiry).

MacIntyre's version is interesting because it does not require a controversial metaphysical telos. Instead, virtues are identified through participation in specific practices — activities like medicine, science, farming, teaching, craftsmanship. Each practice has internal goods that are achieved by excelling in the practice, and the virtues are the character traits that enable this excellence. Justice, courage, honesty, and practical wisdom are virtues in almost every practice because almost every practice requires them.

This is a clever move. It grounds virtues in observable features of human activities rather than in contested metaphysical claims about human nature. MacIntyre gets much of the force of classical virtue ethics while avoiding some of its most contested premises.

But MacIntyre's position still has commitments that the framework cannot fully accept, particularly the claim that modern moral philosophy is incoherent and that only return to tradition can save it. Chapter 11 addressed this; the framework agrees with part of MacIntyre's critique while disagreeing with his prescription.

## What the Framework Takes from Virtue Ethics

The framework has absorbed significant elements from the virtue tradition:

**1. Character matters, not just acts.** A person who does the right thing for the wrong reasons, or who has bad character but happens to behave well, is missing something important. The framework's twelve virtues (Chapter 11) reflect this insight.

**2. Habituation is the mechanism of moral development.** You become honest by practicing honesty. You become courageous by doing courageous things. Moral development is not primarily intellectual; it is primarily practical. Chapter 12 developed this view.

**3. Practical wisdom matters.** Rules and principles are insufficient for specific cases. A person navigating real moral situations needs judgment that cannot be fully reduced to algorithms. The framework's dual-process architecture acknowledges this — Mode A uses rules, but Mode B requires judgment, and virtues are what make the judgment reliable.

**4. The unity of virtues.** Having one virtue without others produces distorted outcomes. An honest person without tact can be cruel. A courageous person without wisdom can be reckless. The framework's virtues work as a system, not as a checklist.

**5. Virtues are developed in communities.** Moral formation happens through participation in communities of practice, not through solitary reflection. The framework acknowledges this (Chapter 12) and incorporates it into the theory of moral development.

**6. Flourishing is more than hedonic tone.** The framework's complex value vector (Chapter 3) includes multiple dimensions beyond simple pleasure and pain, reflecting the Aristotelian insight that human flourishing is rich and multifaceted, not reducible to utility calculations.

**7. Practices have internal goods.** MacIntyre's insight about practices informs how the framework thinks about meaningful work (Chapter 30) and the structure of institutions (Chapter 18). Activities with internal standards of excellence produce genuine goods that cannot be replaced by purely external rewards.

These are significant debts. The framework is substantially virtue-ethical in its treatment of character, development, and practical wisdom.

## Where the Framework Differs

Despite these agreements, the framework diverges from pure virtue ethics on several specific points:

### Divergence 1: Telos

Aristotelian virtue ethics depends on the claim that humans have a specific telos — a natural function that defines what flourishing consists in for humans. The framework rejects this claim in its strong form.

The problem: what is the human telos? Aristotle said it was reasoning well in a political community. But different answers produce different virtue theories -- reproduction yields one set, knowledge another, pleasure another, service to God another. Aristotle's answer is one among many, and none can be derived from biology or physics alone.

The framework's approach: rather than grounding virtues in a contested claim about human telos, ground them in more defensible foundations. Valence (Chapter 3) grounds the basic concern for welfare. Coordination (Book II) grounds the need for character traits that enable cooperation. Epistemic duty (Chapter 2) grounds the need for character traits that enable truth-seeking. These foundations do not require a specific metaphysical telos, yet they generate most of the virtues that the Aristotelian tradition identifies.

This is what we meant earlier by saying the framework's approach is "virtue ethics without telos." We keep the virtues; we reject the specific metaphysical story about why they are the virtues. The result is defensible against critics who reject Aristotelian metaphysics while preserving most of the substantive content.

### Divergence 2: Decision Procedures

Virtue ethics is famously weak on decision procedures. When you face a specific dilemma, what do you do? The virtue-ethical answer is: "what would the virtuous person do?" But this is not much help if you are trying to figure out what the virtuous person would do, and the theory does not give you much guidance on how to figure it out.

Defenders respond that this is a feature, not a bug -- real situations require practical wisdom that cannot be captured in algorithms. There is truth to this. But people in hard situations need more guidance than "use practical wisdom." They need a framework for thinking through the situation, even if that framework does not determine the answer.

The framework's dual-process architecture provides this framework. Mode A gives rules for the normal cases. Mode B gives a structured decision procedure (with the five conditions from Chapter 10) for the override cases. Virtues are essential to both — they enable reliable Mode A compliance and reliable Mode B judgment — but the architecture provides guidance that pure virtue ethics lacks.

### Divergence 3: The Problem of the Phronimos

Aristotle identifies the practically wise person (phronimos) as the standard of right action: what the phronimos would do is what you should do. But this is circular unless you can identify the phronimos without already knowing what right action is. If you know right action, you can identify the phronimos (the person who consistently does it); if you know the phronimos, you know right action (what they do). But you cannot know both without knowing one first.

Virtue ethicists have various responses to this circle. Some say we identify the phronimos through their track record and reputation, which avoids the circle without fully breaking it. Some say the phronimos is recognized intuitively, which makes the standard vague. Some say the phronimos is an ideal that we can approximate through moral development, which pushes the question back to how moral development works.

The framework's response: **we do not need the phronimos as a standard**. We have independent criteria for right action (the framework's principles from Books I-III) that we can use to evaluate specific choices. These criteria do not replace practical wisdom, but they provide something for practical wisdom to be wise about. A person navigating a hard situation is not merely asking "what would the virtuous person do?" — they are also using the specific tools the framework provides (valence analysis, coordination considerations, rights, etc.) to think through the situation.

### Divergence 4: Cultural Embeddedness

Virtue ethics, especially in MacIntyre's version, emphasizes that virtues are developed in specific traditions and communities. There is truth to this — moral formation does happen in particular contexts, and decontextualized moral reasoning misses something important. But the emphasis can become an argument against universal moral claims, which the framework rejects.

MacIntyre's version sometimes slides toward the view that moral knowledge is tradition-dependent — that members of different traditions have genuinely different moralities and cannot judge each other. This is close to the moral relativism we rejected in Chapter 40. To the extent that virtue ethics depends on this view, we reject it.

The framework's position: **virtues are developed in traditions, but the traditions can be evaluated against criteria that are not themselves tradition-dependent**. Some traditions produce better character than others. Traditions that produce courageous, honest, just people are better than traditions that produce cowardly, deceptive, unjust people. This evaluation is not imperialism; it is recognition that character formation has consequences, and some consequences are better than others by standards that are not merely local.

### Divergence 5: The Unity of Virtues Is Overclaimed

Aristotle and some contemporary virtue ethicists argue that the virtues form a strict unity -- you cannot have one without all the others. This is too strong. In practice, people have some virtues and lack others. A courageous person may lack patience. An honest person may lack compassion. These are not fake virtues; they are real virtues that happen to exist without some of the others.

The framework's position: **the virtues reinforce each other and work best as a system, but they can exist partially and in isolation**. Someone who is working on developing their courage without yet having all the other virtues is still making moral progress, even though their progress is incomplete. The strict unity claim sets a standard that few or no actual people meet, which makes it poorly suited as a practical guide.

### Divergence 6: The Role of Rules

Virtue ethics tends to de-emphasize rules in favor of character and judgment. This is valuable as a corrective to rule-fetishism, but it can overshoot. Rules have real value — they are bias-resistant, coordination-enabling, and cognitively efficient (Chapter 9). A purely virtue-based approach misses these benefits.

The framework's dual-process architecture combines rules and virtues: virtues are the character traits that make reliable rule-following possible, and rules are the practical structure through which virtuous agents act most of the time. This combination is stronger than either pure virtue ethics or pure rule-based ethics alone.

## What Virtue Ethics Gets Right (Restated)

Despite these divergences, the framework is substantially indebted to virtue ethics. It is worth stating the debts clearly:

**1. The primacy of character.** Moral philosophy that focuses on acts without addressing character is incomplete. The framework agrees.

**2. The importance of habituation.** Moral development is primarily practical, not primarily intellectual. The framework agrees.

**3. Practical wisdom as irreducible.** No algorithm can replace judgment in specific cases. The framework agrees.

**4. Community and practice as formative contexts.** Moral formation happens in communities, through participation in practices. The framework agrees.

**5. Multidimensional flourishing.** Welfare is not reducible to a single metric. The framework's value vector reflects this.

**6. The internal goods of activities.** MacIntyre's insight about practices is important for understanding meaningful work and institutional structure. The framework uses it.

**7. The role of emotion in moral perception.** Virtues involve feeling rightly, not just acting rightly. A person who gives to charity while resenting every dollar is missing something that a person who gives gladly is not. The framework's account of moral development (Chapter 12) incorporates this.

These are large debts. The framework is what you might call "character-informed consequentialism plus rules" — but the "character-informed" part is doing a lot of work, and it comes mostly from the virtue tradition.

## The Contemporary Convergence

Contemporary virtue ethicists (Hursthouse, Slote, Swanton, and others) have developed versions of virtue ethics that are more flexible than pure Aristotelianism and more compatible with other moral traditions. They emphasize:

- Virtues as character traits that promote human flourishing, without necessarily committing to a strong telos.
- Right action as what the virtuous person would do, supplemented by explicit rules and principles where virtues alone are underdetermining.
- The role of moral emotions and perceptions, not just abstract principles.
- The developmental and community contexts in which virtues are formed.

This contemporary virtue ethics is closer to the framework than classical Aristotelianism. If you asked a contemporary virtue ethicist what their view implies in specific cases, their answers would often align with the framework's. The disagreements are about which considerations are primary and how to structure the theoretical apparatus, not about what to do in specific situations.

## The Verdict

The framework is significantly virtue-ethical in its treatment of character, habituation, practical wisdom, and community. But it is not pure virtue ethics. It differs on:

- **The telos question**: rejecting strong metaphysical claims about human nature while preserving the role of virtues.
- **Decision procedures**: providing the dual-process architecture as guidance where pure virtue ethics leaves agents unaided.
- **The phronimos standard**: providing independent criteria for right action rather than circularly defining them through the virtuous agent.
- **Cultural embeddedness**: rejecting the slide toward relativism while acknowledging the role of traditions in moral formation.
- **The unity of virtues**: allowing partial virtues rather than insisting on all-or-nothing.
- **The role of rules**: combining rule-based and virtue-based approaches rather than privileging either.

The result is a position that takes seriously what virtue ethics gets right (character, development, practical wisdom, community) while avoiding its weaknesses (circularity, weak decision guidance, dependence on contested metaphysics, potential slide toward relativism).

As with Rawls and with utilitarianism, the framework's engagement with virtue ethics is substantive rather than dismissive. The tradition has real insights, and we have absorbed them. The disagreements are specific and defensible. A person who accepts virtue ethics as a starting point and then considers the framework's specific arguments would find much to agree with, and the remaining disagreements would be about particular points rather than about the whole approach.

## Summary

1. **Virtue ethics** focuses on character traits (virtues) that constitute a good life, drawing on Aristotle, MacIntyre, and contemporary thinkers.

2. **Aristotle's strengths** include grounding virtues in human flourishing, the role of habituation, practical wisdom, the unity of virtues, eudaimonia as the goal, and the political context of ethics.

3. **The framework absorbs** the primacy of character, the importance of habituation, practical wisdom as irreducible, community and practice as formative contexts, multidimensional flourishing, and the moral role of emotions.

4. **The framework differs** on the telos (we reject strong metaphysical claims about human nature), decision procedures (we provide the dual-process architecture), the phronimos standard (we provide independent criteria), cultural embeddedness (we reject the slide toward relativism), the unity of virtues (we allow partial virtues), and the role of rules (we combine rules with virtues).

5. **Contemporary virtue ethicists** have developed positions closer to the framework than pure Aristotelianism. Convergence from multiple directions strengthens confidence in the framework.

6. **The verdict**: the framework is significantly virtue-ethical but not purely so. The debts are real and the disagreements are specific rather than fundamental.
