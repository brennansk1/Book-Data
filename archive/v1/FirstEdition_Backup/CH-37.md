# Chapter 37: Against Divine Command Theory

---

> *"Is what is pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?"*

## Why This Book Exists

Through seven books and thirty-five chapters, we have built a moral system from the ground up — grounded it in consciousness and game theory, diagnosed the coordination failures that produce most suffering, designed a decision architecture, and applied it to personal life, politics, economics, and the frontier problems of the twenty-first century. The positive case is now on the table.

But a moral system that cannot withstand scrutiny from its rivals is not yet ready to be believed. Every tradition we are competing with has had centuries of refinement, brilliant defenders, and real-world results. If Constructivist Realism is better than these alternatives, it must be able to demonstrate that superiority in direct engagement — not by caricaturing opponents, but by confronting the strongest versions of their arguments and showing, honestly, where they fall short and where we do better.

That is what Book VIII does. Ten chapters, ten rival systems, each engaged on its own terms. The method in every chapter is the same: steel-man the position, identify its genuine strengths, and then show where the framework we have built provides a better account. Where we find that a rival system has insights we lack, we absorb them. This is not a victory lap. It is a stress test — and the framework's willingness to submit to it is itself an argument for its seriousness.

We begin with the oldest and most widespread competitor.

---

## The Rival

Divine Command Theory (DCT) is the claim that morality is grounded in God's commands. What makes an action right is that God commands it; what makes an action wrong is that God forbids it. Without God, there would be no morality -- just human preferences with no normative force beyond themselves.

This is the dominant moral framework for most of human history and for billions of people today. It is defended by serious philosophers and theologians, including Robert Adams, William Lane Craig, and a tradition running through Aquinas, Augustine, and the medieval scholastics. Any moral framework that aspires to be "the best available" must engage with DCT seriously.

This chapter steel-mans DCT, identifies its strongest arguments, and then shows why the framework we are building is a better foundation for moral reasoning. The conclusion is not that DCT is unintelligible or that its adherents are foolish. The conclusion is that DCT, even in its most sophisticated versions, fails to provide what it promises.

---

## Steel-Manning Divine Command Theory

The strongest version of DCT is not "God said so, end of discussion." That is the caricature, and attacking it is cheap. The strongest version is more subtle.

**Modified DCT** (Robert Adams and others): Divine commands ground moral *obligation*. Moral *value* can be grounded in God's nature rather than in specific commands. What makes something good is that it resembles God's essentially loving and just nature; what makes something obligatory is that God commands it. Adams's key move, developed across "A Modified Divine Command Theory of Ethical Wrongness" (1973) and *Finite and Infinite Goods* (1999), is to anchor divine commands in God's character: God cannot command cruelty for its own sake, because doing so would contradict His essential nature — it is, as Adams argues, "unthinkable for him to do so because of his nature." This avoids some of the classical problems (like the Euthyphro dilemma) by locating different moral categories at different levels of dependence on God. The Euthyphro presents a false choice on Adams's view — God's commands flow necessarily from His omnibenevolence, not from arbitrary will, so they are neither arbitrary nor independent of God.

**Theistic metaethics more broadly**: even if the specific DCT formulation is problematic, theism provides resources for moral realism that naturalism struggles with. If God exists and is essentially good, then moral facts are the facts about what a maximally loving, wise, and knowing being would approve of. This is realism (moral facts are objective) without the "queerness" problem that Mackie identified — the moral facts are grounded in a kind of entity (a mind) that we already accept as possible.

**The argument from moral knowledge**: We seem to have moral knowledge. People can recognize cruelty as wrong, kindness as good, justice as valuable. How is this knowledge possible? If morality is grounded in God and God has given humans a moral sense (conscience), then moral knowledge is explicable — we were designed to perceive it. On naturalism, the reliability of our moral intuitions is harder to explain.

**The argument from moral motivation**: Why should we do what morality requires, especially when it costs us? On DCT, the answer is clear: we ought to obey God because God is the source of our being and our flourishing, and because disobedience has consequences (in this life or the next). On naturalism, the motivation is less clear. "You ought to reduce suffering" is abstract; "God commands you to love your neighbor and you will answer to him for how you treat them" is concrete and motivating.

**The historical argument**: Most successful moral traditions in human history have been religious. They have produced extraordinary feats of virtue, sacrifice, and institutional achievement. The abolitionists, the civil rights movement, charitable organizations, hospitals, universities — many of the moral achievements of Western history emerged from religious contexts. Theism is not merely defensible; it is the tradition within which most of the moral work of civilization has happened. Secular alternatives are recent and their track record is more mixed.

**The argument from ultimate meaning**: If there is no God, then the universe is ultimately indifferent to human welfare. The framework's attempts to ground meaning in local conscious experience (Chapter 15) feel thin compared to the theistic alternative, in which meaning is woven into the fabric of existence by a being whose love ensures that it is not finally in vain.

These are serious arguments. A dismissive response is unworthy of them. We must engage them on their own terms before explaining why the framework does not accept them.

## The Euthyphro Problem, Still Unsolved

The classical attack on DCT is Plato's *Euthyphro*. Socrates asks: "Is what is pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?" The question creates a dilemma for DCT:

**Horn 1: Things are good because God commands them.** If so, then God's commands are arbitrary. God could, in principle, have commanded anything — including cruelty, torture, or murder — and it would have been good. This makes morality arbitrary at its foundation, which is exactly what DCT was supposed to avoid. "God commanded X" cannot be a moral reason if there is no independent fact about whether X is good apart from God's command.

**Horn 2: God commands things because they are good.** If so, then goodness is independent of God's commands. God recognizes what is good and commands accordingly, which means the standards of goodness exist independently of God. But then DCT is false — morality is not grounded in God's commands but in the standards that even God must follow.

Modern DCT responses typically try to escape both horns through the Modified DCT move mentioned above: ground value in God's *nature* (not in arbitrary commands) and ground obligation in commands that flow from that nature. On this view, God is essentially good, so God cannot command cruelty, but the goodness does come from God rather than from an independent standard.

The framework's response: **Adams's move is the most sophisticated available, but it relocates the problem rather than solving it**.

Adams's strategy in *Finite and Infinite Goods* is essentially Platonic: God plays the role of Plato's Form of the Good. Finite goods are good insofar as they resemble or participate in God's nature, and God's nature just *is* the Good. Obligations then flow from the commands of this essentially good being. This is elegant and avoids crude voluntarism. But consider: what makes God's nature "good"? Is there an independent standard of goodness that God's nature happens to meet? If so, goodness is independent of God, and we are on horn 2. Or is God's nature good simply because it is God's nature? If so, we have returned to arbitrariness — God could have had any nature and it would have been the standard, which makes "God's nature is good" meaningless as a claim with content.

The Modified DCT theorist might respond: "God's nature is constitutively good — there is no separate standard, but there is no arbitrariness either, because the nature is essentially what it is." This is sophisticated, but it has a cost. It reduces "God is good" from an informative claim to a definitional one. Saying "God is good" now tells us nothing about God's properties; it just tells us that whatever God's nature is, we are calling it good. This drains the claim of the moral content it was supposed to have.

More importantly, the Modified DCT position depends on specific claims about God's nature -- that God is essentially loving, just, wise. These claims are themselves contested. Different religious traditions attribute different natures to God, and within a single tradition, theologians disagree about God's essential properties. If you need to know God's nature before you can know what is good, and God's nature is itself a matter of dispute, DCT provides no more certainty than the alternatives it was supposed to replace.

## The Verification Problem

Even if DCT were conceptually sound, there is a practical problem: **how do you know what God commands?**

The options are:
- **Scripture.** But which scripture? The texts claim different and sometimes incompatible things. How do you choose among them?
- **Tradition.** Tradition has endorsed different moral positions at different times. Slavery was morally endorsed by Christian tradition for centuries; now it is condemned. Same scripture, opposite positions. Which tradition is authoritative?
- **Conscience.** But conscience, on naturalistic examination, is an evolved capacity shaped by selection for gene fitness (Chapter 4). If conscience is unreliable on naturalism, DCT proponents cannot appeal to it without explaining why divine authorship would make it reliable in ways we can verify.
- **Religious authorities.** But which ones? Authorities disagree with each other, and religious institutions have their own incentives (power, money, political influence) that compromise their reliability as moral guides.
- **Direct revelation.** Claimed revelations contradict each other and cannot be verified by third parties. Without a verification mechanism, any appeal to revelation is an appeal to private experience that others cannot check.

Each option faces the same underlying problem: **there is no reliable procedure for determining what God actually commands that produces convergent answers**. Different people using the same procedures reach different conclusions, and the disagreement is not resolved by more prayer, more scripture, or more tradition -- it persists and often deepens.

Compare the framework's epistemology from Chapter 2. We propose methods — Bayesian updating, scout mindset, peer review of evidence, deference to expert consensus on technical questions — that, while imperfect, tend to produce convergent answers over time when applied by people of goodwill. The framework's moral conclusions can be debated, but the methods for debating them are public and checkable. DCT's methods lack this property. Two sincere DCT proponents asking "what does God command?" can arrive at opposite answers and have no principled way to resolve the dispute.

This is not a minor technical problem. It is fatal to DCT's practical application. A moral framework whose foundational questions cannot be answered reliably by anyone cannot function as moral guidance for anyone.

## The Moral Progress Problem

DCT has a specific difficulty with moral progress. Consider: every major religious tradition has had to update its moral positions over the centuries. Slavery was endorsed, then condemned. The moral status of women has been revised. Attitudes toward dissent, heresy, and religious minorities have changed. Attitudes toward sexuality continue to evolve.

These changes are usually framed as "deeper understanding of God's will" rather than as "our previous views were wrong." But this framing is suspicious. The new views are almost always *in response to* moral arguments from outside the religious tradition — arguments from secular ethics, empirical evidence, and the moral intuitions of people who were being harmed by the old views. The religious tradition absorbs these arguments, relabels them as "what God really meant," and declares that the new position was always the correct interpretation.

The framework's diagnosis: **moral progress is real, but it happens in response to reasoning that DCT cannot itself generate**. If DCT were the source of moral knowledge, we would expect progress to come from deeper religious understanding -- more prayer, more scripture, more tradition. Instead, it comes from secular argument and moral intuition that pressure religious tradition to update. This is evidence that the moral insight is coming from somewhere other than the claimed source.

The DCT theorist might respond: "God works through reason and through the consciences of all people, including nonbelievers. So the moral progress driven by nonreligious arguments is still ultimately God's work." This is unfalsifiable, which is part of the problem. Any moral progress from any source can be relabeled as God's work. But then DCT has no distinctive content — it is no longer saying that specific religious texts or traditions are the authority. It is saying that everything good is God's and everything bad is humanity's failure, which is a theological claim that makes no contact with specific moral questions.

## The Existence Question

We have so far bracketed the question of whether God exists. DCT's coherence does not depend on God's existence; it depends on *if* God exists, then morality is grounded in his commands. But practically, DCT's force depends on God's existing. If there is no God, DCT is irrelevant as a moral framework.

The framework's position on God's existence: **it is an empirical question that should be settled by evidence, and the evidence is weak**.

This is not the place for a full argument about theism. The philosophical literature is vast and contested. But a few points:

**1. The classical arguments for God (cosmological, ontological, teleological) have been extensively discussed and have not produced consensus even among philosophers of religion.** The arguments have defenders, but they also have serious problems that make them uncompelling to those who do not already believe.

**2. The problem of evil is a serious obstacle.** The amount and kinds of suffering in the world (especially the suffering of innocents, children, and non-human animals) is difficult to reconcile with an all-powerful, all-knowing, perfectly good God. Theodicies exist, but they require significant auxiliary assumptions and often feel strained.

**3. Religious experience is real but interpretively ambiguous.** People have experiences they interpret as encounters with the divine. These experiences are psychologically real and often transformative. But they are also common across incompatible religious traditions (which cannot all be accurate reports of the same reality), and they have naturalistic explanations in terms of neuroscience and psychology. They are not reliable evidence for the specific metaphysical claims theism makes.

**4. The track record of divine intervention claims is poor.** Claims of miracles, prophecy, answered prayer, and direct intervention have been investigated many times and have not produced convincing evidence of divine action that goes beyond what would be expected from chance, psychology, and selection effects.

This does not refute theism. Theism might be true, and the arguments for it might be stronger than we currently appreciate. What it does do is make theism an uncertain foundation for moral knowledge. Building a moral framework on a contested empirical claim is risky — if the claim turns out to be false, the framework collapses. The framework in this book avoids this risk by grounding morality in features of reality that do not depend on contested empirical claims.

## What DCT Gets Right

We should acknowledge what DCT and religious moral traditions get right. The critique above should not be mistaken for a dismissal.

**1. Religious traditions have developed sophisticated moral wisdom.** Centuries of engagement with moral questions, embedded in communities of practice and transmitted across generations, have produced insights that modern secular ethics often reinvents. The framework's position that moral wisdom accumulates in traditions (Chapter 11) owes something to the religious example.

**2. The motivational question is serious.** Why should we do what morality requires, especially when it costs us? Secular frameworks often have weak answers. Religious frameworks have strong ones — love of God, fear of judgment, hope for transcendence, membership in a community of the faithful. The framework's answer (consistency with the valence axiom and with our own reflective identities) is honest but less emotionally compelling. This is a genuine cost of secularism that should not be papered over.

**3. Religious communities have been extraordinary vehicles for moral formation.** The practices, liturgies, and community structures of religious traditions do moral formation in ways that secular alternatives often fail to match. A framework that ignores this is missing something important about how moral lives are actually built.

**4. The intuitions of most people align with religious moral traditions in important ways.** Care for the poor, protection of the vulnerable, hospitality to strangers, honesty, faithfulness — these are moral universals that religious traditions codify and secular ethics often struggles to ground as firmly. When the framework reaches conclusions similar to religious traditions on many specific questions, this is not coincidence; it reflects convergence on real moral features.

**5. Some religious insights about human limits and finitude are genuinely important.** The recognition that we are not our own creators, that we will die, that we are not omniscient, that we need community and humility — these insights are preserved better by religious traditions than by some forms of secular ethics that treat humans as autonomous reasoners with no essential limits.

The framework takes these strengths seriously. Where it diverges from religious traditions is not in rejecting the moral content but in rejecting the grounding. We believe the moral content (most of it) is correct — but we think it can be grounded in valence, coordination, and reflective reason, without requiring the specific theological claims that DCT depends on.

## Our Alternative

The framework's alternative, summarized:

**1. Morality is grounded in valence.** Conscious suffering and joy are objective features of the universe, and they matter morally. This is the axiom. It does not require God, because suffering is not less real if God does not exist. (If God does exist, the axiom is still true — suffering matters.)

**2. Moral knowledge comes from reflection on valence, plus game-theoretic analysis, plus empirical evidence.** These are methods that converge when applied carefully, and they are available to anyone regardless of their religious commitments.

**3. Moral authority comes from the quality of the reasoning, not from any external command.** An argument is good if it is valid and its premises are true. It does not become more true because God endorses it, and it does not become less true because no one has authority to enforce it.

**4. Moral motivation comes from our nature as conscious beings who care about our own welfare and (through empathy and reflection) the welfare of others.** This is weaker than theistic motivation in the abstract, but it is available to all humans regardless of their beliefs.

**5. Moral progress is possible** through the error-correction mechanisms of the framework — the willingness to update positions when arguments are strong and evidence is compelling.

This alternative is compatible with personal religious belief. A religious person can accept the framework's analysis and still believe that the specific moral features the framework identifies are what God had in mind, that prayer and worship are good things for reasons the framework can accommodate, and that their religious community provides moral formation in ways the framework endorses. The framework does not require atheism; it requires that moral reasoning not rest on claims whose verification depends on religious authority.

---

## Why This Matters

The stakes are not merely theoretical. Different moral frameworks produce different conclusions on hard questions, and the choice of framework affects real lives.

Divine Command Theory, when it operates independently of empirical checks, produces conclusions that most contemporary moral observers find horrifying. The Bible endorses slavery; the Quran endorses specific punishments that include stoning and amputation; various traditions have endorsed killing heretics, suppressing women, punishing gay people. DCT proponents typically deal with these difficulties by reinterpreting the relevant texts. But the reinterpretation is a response to moral pressure from outside the religious framework, not a deliverance of the framework itself. A strict DCT, applied without external moral constraints, would endorse conclusions its own contemporary proponents reject.

The framework's alternative does not have this problem. Its conclusions can be wrong — we have been honest about this throughout — but the error-correction mechanism operates within the framework itself, not by importing arguments from outside it.

This is the practical case for the framework over DCT: **it updates more reliably because its epistemology is public and checkable, and it arrives at contemporary moral intuitions through direct reasoning rather than through selective reinterpretation of texts that on their face say something different**.

---

## Closing

This chapter has not argued that religious people are wrong to be religious, or that religious moral traditions have nothing to teach. It has argued that DCT, as a metaethical framework, fails to provide the grounding it claims to provide, and that the framework in this book offers a better foundation.

The reader who is a committed theist will probably not be persuaded by this chapter alone. That is expected. Metaethical commitments run deep, and one chapter is not going to overturn them. What the chapter hopes to do is:

- Demonstrate that the framework takes DCT seriously rather than dismissing it.
- Identify the specific points at which DCT falls short.
- Show that the framework's alternative grounds the moral insights DCT correctly identifies without relying on contested theological claims.

For readers who are already skeptical of DCT, the chapter clarifies what we are offering instead. For readers who accept DCT, the chapter offers an engagement rather than a dismissal, and invites further reflection on whether the framework's alternative might do the work DCT was supposed to do without its specific difficulties.

The next chapter addresses the opposite extreme: nihilism and moral anti-realism. Having defended morality against those who would ground it in God, we must now defend it against those who would deny that morality exists at all.

## Summary

1. **Divine Command Theory** grounds morality in God's commands and is the dominant framework for most of human history. It must be taken seriously.

2. **The Euthyphro problem** — whether things are good because God commands them (arbitrariness) or God commands them because they are good (independence) — remains unsolved in sophisticated versions of DCT.

3. **The verification problem** is fatal: there is no reliable procedure for determining what God commands that produces convergent answers across sincere inquirers.

4. **Moral progress** comes from arguments outside specific religious traditions, which the traditions then absorb and relabel. This suggests the moral insight is not coming from the claimed source.

5. **God's existence is an empirical question** that is contested and cannot serve as a reliable foundation for moral knowledge even if theism is true.

6. **DCT and religious traditions get real things right**: moral wisdom accumulation, motivational strength, community formation, alignment with universal intuitions, insights about human finitude.

7. **The framework's alternative** grounds morality in valence, coordination, and reflective reason — a foundation compatible with personal religious belief but not dependent on specific theological claims.

8. **The practical case** for the framework is that its epistemology is public and checkable, allowing reliable error-correction without requiring selective textual reinterpretation.

The next chapter turns to the opposite challenge: nihilism.
