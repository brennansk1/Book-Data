# Chapter 13: The Daily Algorithm — Moment-to-Moment Moral Decision-Making

---

> *"The unexamined life is not worth living, but the over-examined life never gets lived at all."*
> — a synthesis of Socrates and honest experience

---

## From Theory to Tuesday Morning

The previous twelve chapters built a moral system. This chapter addresses what it *feels like* to use it on an ordinary Tuesday morning, when you are running late, your coffee is lukewarm, and a colleague has just asked you a question you don't want to answer honestly.

Theory is easy. Application is hard. A moral framework that works beautifully in seminars but collapses under real decisions made by tired, distracted, emotional agents is not a useful moral framework. Book III gave us the architecture (Mode A / Mode B, virtues, development). This chapter asks: *how does the architecture actually run in the course of a normal day?*

The answer, drawn from everything that came before, can be compressed into a five-step loop:

1. **Observe.** Gather the facts before acting. What is actually happening?
2. **Orient.** Check for biases. What might you be missing? What is your elephant doing?
3. **Decide.** Apply Mode A (rule) by default; check if Mode B (override) conditions are met.
4. **Act.** Execute with competence. Half-hearted action is a failure of virtue.
5. **Review.** Update your map based on the outcome. What did you learn?

Call this the **Daily Algorithm**. It is a practical loop designed for the cognitive conditions you will actually face: limited time, bounded attention, imperfect information, and the elephant in the background steering you toward convenient conclusions.

---

## Step 1: Observe

You cannot act rightly in a world you see wrongly. This was the whole argument of Chapter 2, and it is the first step of the algorithm because every moral error downstream compounds from failures of observation.

Observation sounds trivial. It is not. The main enemy is **WYSIATI** — What You See Is All There Is (Kahneman, Chapter 2). Your mind constructs confident narratives from whatever evidence it has, usually a small fraction of the relevant evidence, and treats that narrative as complete.

> *"Nothing in life is as important as you think it is, while you are thinking about it."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

This is Kahneman's "focusing illusion," and it operates constantly. By the time you are "deciding what to do," you have already committed to a particular interpretation of the situation — colored by whatever features happened to grab your attention — and the interpretation is usually what is wrong.

The discipline of observation is the discipline of *slowing down enough to gather the facts*. Before you decide what to do, ask:

- **What do I actually know?** Not what do I assume, infer, or suspect — what do I have direct evidence for?
- **What am I missing?** Whose perspective am I not seeing? What information might I not have?
- **What's the base rate?** How often do situations like this actually turn out the way I'm assuming they will?
- **Is my first impression consistent with the evidence, or am I pattern-matching to something familiar?**

This is not paralysis by analysis. For most decisions, observation takes seconds, not minutes. Most of the time, you already know enough to proceed. But the *habit* of pausing to check is what prevents the WYSIATI trap from locking you into decisions based on inadequate information.

**Example:** You come home and find your teenager's room a disaster. Your immediate impression: lazy and disrespectful. Before you confront them, observe: when did you last see them? What happened today? Have they been sick, stressed, grieving? Is this a pattern or an aberration? The observation step does not tell you the right answer — it prevents you from acting on the first interpretation that came to mind, generated by an elephant that wants a simple story and a dramatic response.

**Example:** A colleague makes a comment that sounds insulting. Before you respond defensively, observe: what's the full context? Were they joking? Are they stressed about something unrelated? Is this consistent with their normal behavior, or an aberration? The pause before reacting is often the difference between a productive conversation and a damaged relationship.

The observation step is where scout mindset is most directly applied. The scout is the person who looks before concluding. The soldier is the person who starts with a conclusion and then sees only what confirms it. You want to be a scout for about five seconds longer than feels necessary.

## Step 2: Orient

Once you have the facts — or as many as you are going to get before you must act — the next step is to orient yourself: What kind of situation is this? What biases am I likely to have? What is my elephant doing?

Orientation is the discipline of asking, *before I decide, is there anything systematically wrong with how I am approaching this situation?*

Common failures at this step:

- **Motivated reasoning.** You want a particular outcome, and your reasoning is shaping itself to justify that outcome. Ask: would I still hold this view if I had the opposite interest?
- **Affect substitution.** You are reacting to how you *feel* about someone or something, not to the actual situation. Ask: would I reach the same conclusion if the person I am judging were someone I liked more?
- **Tribal signaling.** You are taking a position because it aligns with your group's current consensus, not because you have examined it. Ask: would I hold this view if none of my friends held it?
- **Sunk cost.** You are continuing a course of action because you have already invested in it, not because continuing is the best choice from here. Ask: would I start this now, given where I am?
- **Present bias.** You are giving too much weight to immediate consequences and too little to long-term ones. Ask: how will I feel about this decision in a year?
- **Availability.** You are estimating probabilities based on vivid recent examples rather than base rates. Ask: what has actually happened in most cases like this?

You do not need to run through this entire list for every decision. But you do need the habit of *noticing* when you are in a state that makes one of these failures likely. Emotion is a signal — not a signal to ignore, but a signal to pay extra attention to your reasoning.

This step is also where you ask: **is this a Moloch trap?** (Chapter 6) Is the situation structured so that rational individual choices lead to collectively bad outcomes? If so, the right move may not maximize your local payoff — it may support the cooperative equilibrium. Most of your decisions are iterated games, and your best move is usually the one that preserves your position as a reliable cooperator.

**Example:** A salesperson is pressuring you to decide quickly on a significant purchase. Orient: this situation is *designed* to trigger decision biases — scarcity, social pressure, loss aversion. Recognizing this is most of the work. The appropriate response is to slow down, not speed up, precisely because your elephant is being manipulated.

**Example:** You are in a meeting where everyone else is agreeing with a proposal that seems wrong to you. Orient: conformity pressure, social cost of dissent, and the possibility that you are missing something the others see. The fact that you feel pressure to go along is itself data, but it is not dispositive — sometimes conformity pressure is tracking genuine consensus, sometimes it is preventing groupthink from being corrected. The orient step is where you ask which is happening.

## Step 3: Decide

Now the actual decision. By default, apply Mode A: *is there a rule that covers this situation?* If yes, follow the rule.

The rules you follow are ones you worked out in advance, during moments of reflection, when you had time to think clearly. You are not constructing new rules mid-decision; you are executing pre-committed dispositions. This is the whole point of having rules — they do the hard thinking once, so that in the moment you can just act.

Core rules to have already worked out:
- Don't lie.
- Don't take what isn't yours.
- Keep your promises.
- Don't initiate violence.
- Respond proportionally to mistreatment, then forgive.
- Don't cause unnecessary suffering.
- Help when you can, especially those in serious need.
- Be honest about your uncertainty.
- Meet your commitments before pursuing your preferences.

Most decisions are covered by this short list. When they are, Mode A is the answer: apply the rule.

**Check for Mode B conditions only if Mode A is producing a clearly catastrophic outcome.** The five conditions from Chapter 10:

1. Is following the rule going to cause catastrophic irreversible harm?
2. Is the harm directly attributable to rule-following?
3. Have you exhausted alternatives that preserve the rule?
4. Can you articulate the override to a skeptical third party?
5. Is your self-interest absent or corrected for?

If even one of these fails, you are not in Mode B — you are in ordinary decision-making, and the rule applies. Most claimed Mode B cases fail at condition 4 (articulability) or condition 5 (self-interest). If you cannot explain your reasoning to a skeptical outsider without embarrassment, you are rationalizing, not overriding.

**When rules conflict:** This is the other case where Mode B enters. If two rules genuinely conflict (not "one rule is slightly inconvenient" but "both rules apply and they demand opposite actions"), you need to decide which takes precedence. The general heuristic: **the rule whose violation would cause greater harm takes precedence**, adjusted for certainty. Lying to a Nazi about refugees (violating the honesty rule) prevents greater harm than telling the truth would (violating the protect-innocents rule). The greater-harm rule wins, but only after you have verified that the conflict is genuine and not manufactured.

**When no rule applies:** Novel situations arise. Technology creates moral questions the traditional rules were not designed for. Here you must reason from first principles — from the foundations of Books I and II. *What reduces suffering? What supports cooperation? What would a reasonable person articulate as the right action here?* This is slower and more error-prone than applying rules, but sometimes unavoidable. Treat the resulting conclusion as provisional until you have reflected on it outside the moment of decision.

The decision step is usually fast. The rule is obvious, the elephant has been checked, and the action is clear. Moral agony is the exception. Most of daily moral life is simply doing the thing your character already directs you to do.

## Step 4: Act

Once the decision is made, execute with competence. Many well-intentioned moral agents fail here — not because they don't know the right thing to do, but because they do it badly.

> *"Goodness without power is impotent. Competence is a moral duty."*
> — from the Framework's virtue list, *tsuyoku naritai*

A half-hearted apology is usually worse than no apology. A clumsy intervention in a crisis can cause more harm than none. A truth told with malice is worse than a truth told with care. The *how* of an action matters almost as much as the *what*, and developing the competence to execute actions well is a moral obligation, not a nice-to-have.

This means:

- **Do the work.** If the right thing to do requires skill you don't have, your job is to develop the skill, not to skip the action. Don't know how to have a hard conversation? Learn. Don't know how to handle conflict? Learn. Don't know how to apologize properly? Learn. The virtue of *tsuyoku naritai* — I want to become stronger — applies to moral execution just as much as to intellectual growth.

- **Don't wait for perfect conditions.** Competence is about acting *well* under the conditions that exist, not about finding perfect conditions. You will never have all the information, all the time, all the energy, all the support you want. The moral agent acts anyway, with the resources they have, and takes the results seriously.

- **Commit fully.** Once you have decided, act. Do not half-commit while keeping an escape route open — this produces worse outcomes than either full commitment or no action at all. Half-measures satisfy no one and often cause the damage of action without the benefits.

- **Notice how you feel during execution.** Emotional resistance during action is often a signal. Sometimes it means you are acting against your own values and should stop; sometimes it means you are doing something hard but right and should continue. The discipline is to notice the signal, distinguish its source, and adjust accordingly — not to suppress the emotion or be ruled by it.

**Example:** You have decided to apologize to someone you wronged. Execution matters. A sincere apology that takes responsibility, does not make excuses, and offers repair is a different act from a muttered "sorry" that hedges the admission and implies the other person overreacted. Same decision, very different moral quality of action. The virtue of precision and the virtue of perfectionism both apply here.

**Example:** You have decided to report a colleague's misconduct to HR. Execution matters. Doing it calmly, with specific incidents documented, focused on behavior rather than character, and framed in terms of institutional harm, is a different act from doing it emotionally, with vague accusations, framed as personal grievance. Same decision, very different outcomes for everyone involved.

## Step 5: Review

The final step is where growth happens. After you have acted — whether successfully, unsuccessfully, or ambiguously — you review.

Review questions:
- What actually happened? (Observation again, now with more data.)
- Did the outcome match my expectations? If not, where was my model wrong?
- Did my reasoning hold up, or were there steps I skipped or biases I failed to notice?
- What would I do differently next time?
- Is there a rule I should add to my Mode A repertoire, based on what I learned?
- Is there a rule I should modify or remove?

This is the learning loop that turns experience into wisdom. Without it, you repeat the same mistakes indefinitely — each one feeling new because you have not pulled the general lesson out of the specific case. With it, you accumulate moral expertise: a growing body of rules, heuristics, and judgment that handles an expanding range of situations.

The review step should happen *after* the heat of the moment has cooled — not in the middle of the action, not immediately afterward, but later, with some distance. A nightly reflection (even five minutes) is one traditional form. A weekly journal is another. The specific practice matters less than having *some* practice that closes the loop.

> *"If you do not reflect on your day, you cannot improve your life."*
> — attributed to various Stoics

This sounds austere, but the Stoics were right about the mechanism. The unreflective life is not merely unworthy of examination; it is uneducable. You cannot learn from experience you don't examine, and moral development requires learning from experience.

Review also includes updating your beliefs. If your prediction was wrong, your mental model needs updating. If a rule you followed produced bad results, the rule needs revisiting. This is Bayesian reasoning applied to your own moral practice — treat your beliefs and rules as hypotheses, and update them as evidence accumulates.

## The Algorithm in Practice: Three Scenarios

Let me walk through how the algorithm actually runs in three ordinary situations.

### Scenario 1: The Difficult Email

It's Tuesday morning. There's an email in your inbox from a colleague criticizing your recent work. Your immediate impulse is to reply defensively, explain why they're wrong, and move on.

**Observe.** What does the email actually say? Read it carefully. Separate the factual claims from the emotional tone. Are the criticisms valid? Partially valid? Misunderstanding something you could clarify? Don't confuse "I don't like how this is phrased" with "this is wrong."

**Orient.** Your immediate defensive reaction is being generated by your ego — by the part of you that treats criticism as a threat. This is motivated reasoning. Your social standing is in play, which means your elephant is working. Notice this. It doesn't mean the criticism is correct, but it does mean your initial assessment is suspect.

**Decide.** Rules: Don't lie, don't be cruel, respond honestly, try to understand before being understood. Is this a Mode B case? No — nothing catastrophic is at stake. So Mode A applies: respond truthfully, acknowledge what is valid in the criticism, clarify what seems to be a misunderstanding, and engage the substantive issues without personal attacks.

**Act.** Write the email. Take your time. Don't send the first draft. Reread it imagining you are the recipient and asking whether you would find it fair. Revise if necessary. Send it.

**Review.** A day later, how did it go? Did the colleague respond constructively? Did the interaction move the work forward? What would you do differently? Is there a pattern of similar situations you can now handle better?

Total time: maybe 20 minutes, instead of the 3 minutes it would have taken to send the defensive reply. The outcome is almost always dramatically better.

### Scenario 2: The Request to Help

A friend asks for a significant favor. You don't really want to do it. Your first impulse is to invent an excuse.

**Observe.** What is the actual request? How significant is it? What are the friend's circumstances? Do you have the capacity to help? Would helping involve sacrifices you can afford, or sacrifices that would damage your other commitments?

**Orient.** The impulse to invent an excuse is a red flag — it suggests you are about to lie (violating an important rule) for your own convenience. Notice this. But also notice: the fact that you don't *want* to help is not by itself a reason to help. Reluctance is data, not a verdict.

**Decide.** Rules: don't lie, help when you can, meet prior commitments, don't overcommit. Does helping conflict with existing obligations? If yes, that's relevant. If no, then the question is whether "I don't feel like it" is a valid reason to decline — and usually it isn't, especially when the friend is genuinely in need and you genuinely could help. The honest answer is either "yes, I can help" or "no, I can't, and here's the real reason" — not "sorry, I'm busy" when you aren't.

**Act.** Either help (fully and well) or decline (honestly and kindly). Do not half-help while resenting it.

**Review.** How did it go? Did helping cost more or less than you feared? Did declining preserve the friendship? Did you handle the conversation in a way you are proud of? Update your model of yourself and your friendship.

### Scenario 3: The Big Decision

You have been offered a new job. It would be a significant change — more responsibility, more money, but also more stress and a move away from friends. You have a week to decide.

**Observe.** What are the concrete features of the offer? Salary, role, responsibilities, location, benefits, the people you would work with, the realistic day-to-day experience. Talk to people currently in similar roles. Get real information, not optimistic pitches.

**Orient.** Multiple biases are in play: optimism about change, fear of change, status considerations, sunk costs in your current situation, the desire to be decisive. Notice all of them. You are unlikely to reason accurately about any of them on day one. The right response to "I have a week to decide" is to use the week — to let initial reactions settle, to gather more information, to see whether your assessment changes as the novelty wears off.

**Decide.** This is not a Mode A case because no rule covers it. This is genuine deliberation. The tools: the complex value vector from Chapter 3 (hedonic tone, autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, meaning). How does each option score on each dimension? Make the table literally, with actual entries. Discuss with people you trust. Do the premortem (Chapter 2): imagine it's a year later and the decision turned out to be wrong — why did it go wrong? This surfaces risks that optimistic imagination misses.

**Act.** Make the decision before the deadline. Commit. Don't keep one foot out the door.

**Review.** Six months later, how is it going? Which of your predictions were accurate? Which were wrong? What does this tell you about how to make similar decisions in the future? Update your model of yourself and your judgment.

## When the Algorithm Fails

The Daily Algorithm is a tool. Like any tool, it has failure modes.

**Failure 1: Paralysis by analysis.** You get stuck in observation and orientation and never reach decision. Solution: time-box each step. Most decisions do not warrant more than a few minutes of deliberation. If you find yourself still deliberating after 15 minutes on an ordinary matter, you are almost certainly avoiding the decision rather than making it better.

**Failure 2: Mode B creep.** You start treating minor dissatisfactions as justifications for override. Solution: every time you think "this is an exception," apply the full five-condition test rigorously. If you cannot articulate the override publicly, you are in Mode A, and the rule applies.

**Failure 3: Skipping the review step.** You execute but don't reflect, so you don't learn. Solution: install a specific review practice — daily, weekly, whatever works — and honor it even when you feel like skipping.

**Failure 4: Treating it as a performance.** You run the algorithm not to reach the right decision but to *feel* virtuous about having used the algorithm. Solution: notice when the process becomes its own reward. The goal is the outcome, not the checklist.

**Failure 5: Mechanical application.** You run the algorithm as if it were a computer program, losing the judgment and virtue that it presupposes. Solution: remember that the algorithm is a scaffold, not a replacement for character. The virtues from Chapter 11 are what make the algorithm work; without them, the steps become empty motions.

## The Cumulative Effect

One final observation. The Daily Algorithm does not produce dramatic moral victories. It produces *slightly better* decisions, most of the time, in ordinary situations. Slightly more honest. Slightly more thoughtful. Slightly more aligned with reality. Slightly less governed by self-interest and bias.

But "slightly better" compounded over a lifetime is enormous.

A person who gets 55% of their decisions right across 40 years of adult life lives a dramatically different life from one who gets 65% right. The difference is not visible in any single day — it is only visible in retrospect, in the trajectories of careers, relationships, health, character. The compounding is invisible while it is happening, which is why most people don't notice their own trajectories until they are well along them.

The Daily Algorithm is the tool for nudging your percentage upward. Not through moments of dramatic moral heroism, which are rare and not really under your control, but through the quiet discipline of running a slightly better decision process, a thousand times a day, for the rest of your life.

## Summary

1. **The Daily Algorithm is a five-step loop**: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act, Review.

2. **Observe** is about slowing down enough to see what's actually happening, defeating WYSIATI.

3. **Orient** is about catching your own biases and recognizing the type of situation you're in.

4. **Decide** is about applying Mode A by default and checking Mode B conditions rigorously before invoking the override.

5. **Act** is about executing with the competence that moral seriousness requires. Half-measures are failures of virtue.

6. **Review** is where growth happens. Without review, experience does not turn into wisdom.

7. **The compounding is the point.** Small improvements in daily decision quality compound over a lifetime into dramatic differences in who you become and what you accomplish.
