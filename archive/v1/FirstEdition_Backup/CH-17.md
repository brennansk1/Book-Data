# Chapter 17: Why Government? — The Coordination Argument for the State

---

> *"If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary."*
> — James Madison, *Federalist No. 51*

---

## From the Individual to the Collective

Book IV asked what the framework means for a single human life — how to make decisions, how to sustain relationships, how to find meaning, how to face suffering and death. Those are the questions that matter most intimately. But they are not the only questions.

A person can live well by every standard of personal ethics and still be crushed by the society around them. The most virtuous citizen of a failed state still suffers from the failure. The most rational agent in a corrupt institution still faces incentives that punish cooperation and reward defection. Personal ethics is necessary but not sufficient, because most of the suffering the valence axiom cares about is not produced by individual vice — it is produced by collective structures that individual action cannot fix. Moloch, the enemy we diagnosed in Book II, operates primarily at the social scale. And that means defeating Moloch requires not just better individuals but better institutions.

This is why the argument must now turn outward. Books V and VI — political philosophy and economic philosophy — are not a change of subject. They are the direct continuation of the same project: reducing unnecessary suffering and enabling flourishing. The difference is the scale. Personal ethics asks "how should I live?" Political philosophy asks "how should we live *together*?" — and a moral system that cannot answer that second question is dangerously incomplete.

---

## The Question Hobbes Got Half Right

Thomas Hobbes asked the foundational question of political philosophy: without a sovereign, what would human life look like? His answer, from *Leviathan* (1651), is one of the most famous sentences in political thought — life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." Without a common power to enforce rules, Hobbes argued, rational agents would defect from each other constantly, and the result would be a war of all against all. The only escape is to delegate coercive authority to a sovereign who can punish defectors and enforce cooperation.

Hobbes was half right. He was right that coordination is a problem, that defection is a real risk, and that without some enforcement mechanism, many valuable activities become impossible.

But he was half wrong in a way that matters enormously. Axelrod's work (Chapter 7) showed that cooperation can emerge without central authority — the live-and-let-live system in the WWI trenches, the iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments. Ostrom's work (Chapter 8) showed that communities can govern commons sustainably for centuries without state intervention. Hobbes assumed that coordination required a Leviathan; reality shows that some forms of coordination do not.

So the question becomes more precise: **when is the state necessary, and when is it not?** This is the question Book V tries to answer. It is a different question from "is the state good or bad?" and it generates different answers. Sometimes the state is the right tool; sometimes it is not. The framework rejects both statism (treating the state as the default solution) and anarchism (treating the state as inherently wrong). It treats the state as one tool among several, to be evaluated on its fitness to specific coordination problems.

## What the State Actually Does

Before asking whether the state is necessary, we should be clear about what the state actually is and what functions it performs.

A modern state is, functionally, a bundle of institutions that do several distinct things:

1. **Monopolize legitimate violence.** The state claims exclusive authority to use coercive force within its territory (police, military, courts). Private violence is punished; state violence, when authorized by law, is not.

2. **Provide dispute resolution.** Courts adjudicate conflicts between individuals, organizations, and (sometimes) the state itself. Without reliable dispute resolution, cooperation above very small scales becomes impossible.

3. **Enforce property rights.** The state defines what counts as property, who owns what, and what constitutes a violation. This enables investment, exchange, and long-term planning.

4. **Provide public goods.** Some goods — national defense, public health infrastructure, scientific research, certain kinds of environmental protection — are non-excludable and/or non-rivalrous, and cannot be efficiently provided by markets alone.

5. **Correct externalities.** When private actions create costs (pollution) or benefits (vaccination) that the actors do not bear, the state can tax or subsidize to align private incentives with social welfare.

6. **Redistribute resources.** Through taxation and transfers, the state moves resources from some citizens to others, for various purposes: social insurance, reduction of extreme poverty, correction of historical injustice, funding of public goods.

7. **Set and enforce rules.** Regulation of commerce, safety standards, professional licensure, environmental protection, contract law.

Each of these functions is a response to a specific kind of coordination problem. And each could, in principle, be handled in other ways. David Friedman in *The Machinery of Freedom* argues that private legal systems could handle dispute resolution and even some aspects of law enforcement. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can handle commons management. Private insurance could, in principle, handle many risks currently addressed by state programs. Charity and mutual aid can reduce some forms of poverty. The question is not whether any state function is in principle replaceable; the question is which functions are *best* handled by the state, given the specific coordination problems they address.

## The Coordination Case for the State

The framework's positive case for the state runs like this:

**Case 1: When the coordination problem exceeds the scale of community governance.** Ostrom's principles work at village scale, occasionally at larger scales through nesting. But many modern coordination problems are beyond any community's scope. Climate change cannot be solved by any single Swiss valley or Japanese village, however well-governed. Nuclear proliferation cannot be addressed by mutual-aid societies. Pandemics cross borders. Interstate commerce requires rules that apply across jurisdictions. For problems at this scale, the options are (a) states acting individually or cooperatively, (b) some new form of supra-national institution, or (c) failure to solve the problem. In practice, the state is usually the right level — it has enough scale to address large problems, enough legitimacy to enforce solutions, and enough specificity to allow local variation. Supra-national institutions exist and are sometimes necessary, but they generally work through states rather than replacing them.

**Case 2: When the shadow of the future is too short for voluntary cooperation.** Chapter 7 established that cooperation stabilizes when repeated interaction is likely. But some situations do not have this structure. Anonymous interactions in large modern economies (you will never see the cashier again). One-off transactions with strangers. Cross-border commerce. In these cases, the iterative logic does not work, and some external enforcement mechanism is needed. The state provides this mechanism through contract law, tort law, and criminal enforcement.

**Case 3: When public goods must be provided.** Some goods have the property that they benefit everyone (or large groups) whether or not individuals contribute to them. National defense is the classic example: if your country is defended, you benefit regardless of whether you paid taxes. This creates a free-rider problem — rational individuals will not voluntarily contribute to public goods, because they benefit whether they do or not. The result, in the absence of compulsion, is underprovision. The state solves this by taxing everyone and providing the public good with the collected resources.

**Case 4: When externalities require large-scale coordination.** A single polluter can be handled by tort law or by community pressure. A thousand polluters, each contributing small amounts to a shared atmospheric commons, cannot. The Pigovian framework from Chapter 8 (tax the externality at its social cost) requires a mechanism for assessing and collecting the tax, which at scale means the state.

**Case 5: When the exit option is too costly.** Private governance mechanisms work best when people can exit bad arrangements. You can leave a failed company, a failing community, a failing relationship. But you cannot easily leave a country — the costs of emigration are high, the destination options may be bad, and if all states were failing, there would be nowhere to go. When exit is costly, voice (the ability to influence the institution from within) becomes essential, and voice requires the formal institutional structures that democratic states provide.

These five cases are not the only justifications for state action, but they are the core ones. Any function of the state that does not map onto one of them is a function that should be scrutinized: why is the state doing this, rather than markets, communities, or civil society?

## The Case Against State Overreach

The framework is not statist. Having justified some state functions, we must equally carefully identify the failure modes of state action and the limits beyond which the state should not go.

**Failure Mode 1: Knowledge limits.** James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (which we drew on in Chapter 8) documented how states systematically fail when they try to manage phenomena they do not understand. The state requires *legibility* — standardized, measurable, centrally collectable information — to function. But many of the things that matter for human flourishing are *illegible* in Scott's sense: local knowledge, tacit skills, complex adaptations, the countless micro-arrangements that make specific communities work. When the state tries to manage these things by imposing standardized rules, it destroys the local adaptations and replaces them with worse alternatives.

Scott's examples — Prussian scientific forestry that produced dying monocultures, Soviet collectivization that produced famine, high-modernist urban planning that produced alienating cities — are not failures of execution. They are failures built into centralized management of complex adaptive systems. The state cannot know what it cannot see, and it cannot see what cannot be standardized.

The implication: the state should avoid managing things that depend on local knowledge and adaptation. These domains need what Scott calls *metis* — local practical knowledge — and metis cannot be replaced by centralized rules.

**Failure Mode 2: Capture.** States are run by people, and people respond to incentives. A state function can be "captured" by the interests it was supposed to regulate, serving those interests rather than the public. Regulatory capture — where industry regulators are staffed by people who came from and will return to the industry, who depend on industry cooperation, and whose interests are more aligned with the regulated firms than with the public — is a documented and persistent problem. It is not a bug; it is a predictable consequence of incentive structures.

The implication: state functions are not automatically beneficial just because they are officially aimed at public welfare. The design of the institution — who staffs it, how they are selected, how they are accountable, what their incentives are — determines whether the institution actually serves its ostensible purpose. A well-designed regulator may be essential; a captured regulator is worse than no regulator at all, because it legitimizes the interests it was supposed to constrain.

**Failure Mode 3: Coercion cost.** Every state action involves coercion, at least in the background. Taxes are compulsory; laws are enforced with penalties; regulations restrict what people can do. Coercion is not intrinsically wrong — it is the mechanism by which the state solves coordination problems voluntarism cannot — but it is not free. It imposes costs: compliance burdens, reduced autonomy, the risk of being punished for non-compliance, the opportunity costs of foregone alternatives. Any state action must be evaluated not just against its intended benefits but against these coercion costs.

A rough principle: the burden of proof is on those proposing new state action to show that (a) the problem is genuine, (b) state action can solve it, (c) alternatives are insufficient, and (d) the benefits exceed the coercion costs. This is not a libertarian bias against the state; it is epistemic discipline.

**Failure Mode 4: Bureaucratic inertia.** Once a state program exists, it tends to persist even after its original purpose has been served or rendered obsolete. The people who run the program have jobs that depend on it. The constituents it serves become politically organized to defend it. The original justifications fade, but the institution remains. This is a failure of Chapter 8's "Conflict-resolution mechanisms" principle — state institutions often lack the graduated-sanctions and collective-choice mechanisms that would allow them to adapt to changing circumstances or wind down when no longer needed.

The implication: sunset clauses, regular program reviews, and institutional mechanisms for retiring obsolete programs are important features of a well-designed state. Without them, the state accumulates layers of obsolete activity that drain resources and create friction.

**Failure Mode 5: Moloch at the state level.** The state is not immune to the coordination failures it was designed to solve. States can be captured by interest groups (collective action failure). They can engage in arms races with each other (prisoner's dilemma at the international level). They can pursue short-term policies at the expense of long-term ones because politicians face short electoral cycles (tragedy of the time commons). The Moloch diagnosis from Chapter 6 applies to states, and state action can make Moloch worse rather than better when the state itself is trapped in bad equilibria.

## The Principle of Subsidiarity

The preceding analysis suggests a structural principle that the framework endorses: **subsidiarity** — the idea that decisions should be made at the lowest level of governance competent to handle them.

Subsidiarity is not a libertarian position. It does not say "the state is always bad." It says: for any given coordination problem, ask first whether it can be handled at the individual, family, community, local, regional, or national level. Push the decision as far down the hierarchy as the problem allows. Only escalate when the scale of the problem genuinely exceeds the lower level's capacity.

Why subsidiarity?

- **Local knowledge is preserved.** Lower levels have more *metis* — more practical understanding of their specific context — than higher levels.
- **Accountability is tighter.** Citizens have more voice in decisions made by local officials they can meet personally than in decisions made by distant bureaucrats.
- **Mistakes are contained.** A bad policy at the local level affects fewer people and can be corrected more easily than a bad policy at the national level.
- **Experimentation is enabled.** Different localities can try different approaches, generating data about what works. This is impossible when decisions are made uniformly from above.
- **Legitimacy is enhanced.** People are more likely to accept and cooperate with policies made by institutions they feel represent them.

Subsidiarity has limits. Problems that genuinely cross jurisdictional boundaries (pollution, pandemics, organized crime, existential risks) must be addressed at the level that contains them. Purely local solutions to genuinely global problems are forms of evasion. The principle is not "always use the smallest unit" but "use the smallest unit *sufficient* to the problem."

---

## Nested Governance

The implementation of subsidiarity is what Ostrom called **nested governance** — Chapter 8's design principle 8. Different decisions are made at different levels, with each level competent to its own scope, and mechanisms for resolving conflicts between levels.

A concrete example: consider the governance of a water system.

- **Individual household level:** decisions about personal use, conservation, etc.
- **Neighborhood level:** maintenance of local pipes, dispute resolution about use.
- **Municipal level:** reservoir management, treatment infrastructure, water rights within the city.
- **Regional / watershed level:** allocation among municipalities, watershed protection, drought management.
- **National level:** interstate water allocation, large infrastructure (dams), pollution standards.
- **International level:** treaties about shared rivers crossing borders.

Each level handles what it is best positioned to handle. Each has authority in its own scope. Conflicts are resolved by appeal to higher levels when necessary. This is how complex systems actually work when they work well — not through a single central authority, not through pure individualism, but through layered institutions that match their scope to their function.

The modern nation-state is one layer in this structure, not the whole structure. Attempts to flatten all coordination to the national level (centralization) or to eliminate the national level entirely (radical decentralization) both fail, because both ignore the fact that different problems need different scales.

## The Meta-Principle: Engineering, Not Ideology

The framework's view on government is engineering, not ideology. We are not asking "what do we believe about the state in the abstract?" We are asking "what tool best solves this specific coordination problem?" Sometimes the answer is the state. Sometimes it is markets. Sometimes it is community self-governance. Sometimes it is nothing — some problems have no good solution at the current level of knowledge and institution-building, and pretending otherwise causes more harm than it prevents.

This is not fence-sitting. It is the refusal to pre-commit to ideological answers before the question has been specified. A libertarian who believes the state is always bad will be wrong about pandemic response. A statist who believes the state can manage anything will be wrong about community commons. The framework commits to neither position. It commits to the method of evaluating each case on its specific structure, using the tools developed in Books I-III.

This will be unsatisfying to readers looking for clean ideological commitments. What the framework offers instead is a set of tools and principles that, applied rigorously, generate defensible answers to specific political questions. The answers will sometimes align with libertarians, sometimes with progressives, sometimes with conservatives, and sometimes with none of them. This is not a failure of coherence; it is coherence at a deeper level than political tribalism operates.

## What the Framework Expects of a Legitimate State

Putting the above together, the framework's minimal expectations for a legitimate state are:

1. **It solves actual coordination problems.** Not imaginary ones. Not problems invented to justify the state's existence. Real problems that would otherwise go unsolved.

2. **It does so more effectively than alternatives.** Including alternatives that might not have been tried. A state function is legitimate only if community governance, markets, and civil society would not do it better.

3. **It is subject to error-correction.** Elections, courts, free press, institutional review. Without these mechanisms, the state cannot update when it is wrong, and errors compound.

4. **It respects the knowledge and adaptation of lower levels.** Subsidiarity is respected. Local knowledge is not destroyed in the name of standardization unless the cost of not standardizing is very high.

5. **It operates within constitutional constraints.** Rights are protected. The state's own power is checked. Arbitrary coercion is limited.

6. **Its functions are visible and legible to citizens.** People can know what the state is doing and evaluate it. Secret functions, beyond the narrow cases of genuine national security, are corrosive.

7. **It is accountable to those it governs.** Through voice (democratic participation) and/or exit (emigration, in the worst cases). Governance without accountability becomes tyranny, regardless of the good intentions of those in power.

These are minimum expectations, not a complete theory of the good state. The next seven chapters develop more: institutional design (CH-18), rights (CH-19), democracy (CH-20), free speech (CH-21), justice (CH-22), war (CH-23), policy evaluation (CH-24). Together they form a comprehensive political philosophy derived from the coordination analysis of Book II and the moral architecture of Book III.

## Summary

1. **Hobbes was half right:** coordination is real, and without mechanisms to enforce it, some valuable activities cannot occur.
2. **Hobbes was half wrong:** not all coordination requires central authority. Some cooperation emerges voluntarily in iterated games, and some is handled by community self-governance.
3. **The state is one tool among several** for solving coordination problems, to be evaluated on its fitness to specific problems rather than as an ideological commitment.
4. **The positive case for the state** rests on five conditions: scale exceeding community governance, short shadows of the future, public goods provision, large-scale externalities, and costly exit.
5. **The case against state overreach** rests on five failure modes: knowledge limits, capture, coercion costs, bureaucratic inertia, and Moloch at the state level.
6. **Subsidiarity and nested governance** are the structural principles: decisions at the lowest level competent to them, with layered institutions for layered problems.
7. **The framework's approach is engineering, not ideology.** It matches tools to problems rather than pre-committing to libertarian, statist, or communitarian answers.
8. **A legitimate state** solves real problems effectively, corrects errors, respects subsidiarity, operates within constraints, remains legible, and is accountable.

The next chapter develops how such a state should actually be designed — the institutional architecture that embodies these principles.
