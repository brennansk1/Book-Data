# Chapter 39: Against Pure Utilitarianism

---

> *"The greatest happiness of the greatest number is the foundation of morals and legislation."*
> — Jeremy Bentham, *A Fragment on Government*

## The Closest Rival

Of all the major moral traditions, utilitarianism is the one the framework has the most in common with — and also the one that requires the most careful engagement. Utilitarianism, at its core, holds that morality is about welfare: what we should do is what produces the best outcomes, measured by the welfare of those affected. This is very close to what the framework says.

But there are crucial differences. The framework accepts the welfare-based grounding but rejects several specific features of pure utilitarianism:

1. **Welfare as a scalar** (summable into a single number) vs. welfare as a vector (multiple dimensions that cannot be reduced to one)
2. **Aggregation across persons** (adding up welfare across different individuals) vs. recognition of individual boundaries
3. **Impartiality** (counting every person equally in every decision) vs. partial obligations to specific people
4. **Pure consequentialism** (only outcomes matter) vs. the dual-process architecture where rules have independent weight
5. **Maximization** (always choose the action with the best outcome) vs. satisficing and respecting constraints

These are not small differences. They generate different answers to important moral questions. This chapter develops the case against pure utilitarianism while acknowledging that the framework has absorbed much of what utilitarianism gets right.

---

## Steel-Manning Utilitarianism

Let us present pure utilitarianism in its strongest form, drawing on its best defenders (Bentham, Mill, Sidgwick, and contemporary figures like Peter Singer, Derek Parfit, and Toby Ord).

**The core claim**: the morally right action is the one that produces the greatest total welfare (or the least total suffering) across all affected beings.

**Why this is attractive**:

**1. It takes welfare seriously.** If what matters morally is how people fare — whether they suffer or flourish — then the consistent position is to maximize good outcomes. Any other view seems to care about something other than welfare, which is strange given that welfare is what the moral concern was originally about.

**2. It is impartial.** Utilitarianism does not let you prefer your own welfare to others' in the moral calculation. Your suffering counts the same as a stranger's. This counters the natural human bias toward self and tribe, which has produced so much historical injustice. Peter Singer states the principle clearly:

> *"The essence of the principle of equal consideration of interests is that we give equal weight in our moral deliberations to the like interests of all those affected by our actions... The principle of equal consideration of interests acts like a pair of scales, weighing interests impartially... an interest is an interest, whoever's interest it may be."*
> — Peter Singer, *Practical Ethics*

This principle is powerful and, in its core form, correct. The framework absorbs it as part of what impartiality means, even while adding structural commitments that block some of the conclusions pure impartialism would reach.

**3. It has clear action-guidance.** When faced with a decision, calculate expected welfare consequences and choose the option with the highest expected value. No agonizing about which moral principle applies; the single principle applies everywhere.

**4. It treats moral questions empirically.** What actually produces welfare? This is an empirical question that can be studied. Utilitarianism transforms ethics into applied science, subject to evidence and revision.

**5. It has been historically progressive.** Utilitarian thinkers were early advocates of animal welfare, women's suffrage, legal reform, prison reform, and many other moral advances. The framework associates utilitarianism with moral progress, and this is not coincidental — the willingness to follow the argument wherever it leads has produced genuine insight. Singer's extension of equal consideration to nonhuman animals is the clearest example:

> *"The principle also implies that the fact that beings are not members of our species does not entitle us to exploit them, and it similarly implies that the fact that other animals are less intelligent than we are does not mean that their interests may be discounted or disregarded."*
> — Peter Singer, *Practical Ethics*

The framework accepts this expansion (Chapter 34, POS-F03) and owes Singer and the broader utilitarian tradition a direct debt for it. A tradition that was willing to follow the argument past its own cultural comfort zone — to say that moral concern tracks sentience rather than species membership — deserves credit for moral insight even when we diverge on other questions.

**6. It handles scale well.** When millions of lives are at stake, utilitarian reasoning scales up naturally. Non-consequentialist frameworks often struggle with large-number cases (is saving a million people a thousand times as important as saving a thousand?); utilitarianism just says yes, and this answer is often correct.

**7. Its objections often turn out to be objections to straw versions.** Serious utilitarians have developed sophisticated responses to the classic objections, and many of these responses are better than critics acknowledge.

Any fair engagement with utilitarianism must grant these strengths. The framework owes substantial debts to the utilitarian tradition. The disagreement is not "utilitarianism vs. some completely different view" but "utilitarianism vs. a view that shares most core commitments but diverges on specific features."

## The Classic Objections

Utilitarianism faces a set of classic objections. Some are stronger than others. Let us examine them:

### The Utility Monster

Robert Nozick's thought experiment: imagine a being that gets enormously more pleasure from any given resource than ordinary people. A utility monster's delight from consuming an apple exceeds a normal human's delight by a factor of a million. Pure utilitarianism, following its maximization logic, would give all available resources to the monster. Normal humans would starve so the monster could feast, because that maximizes total utility.

The conclusion seems monstrous, but the logic is airtight under pure utilitarianism. If what matters is total welfare and the monster produces more welfare per unit of resources, then giving the monster everything is the right answer.

Responses utilitarians offer:
- **"Diminishing marginal utility."** Each additional apple produces less pleasure than the last, so eventually the monster's marginal utility drops below a human's. But this just delays the problem; for resources where marginal utility remains high, the conclusion holds.
- **"The scenario is impossible."** No real being has utility monster properties. But the thought experiment tests what the theory says, not whether the scenario is actual. If the theory gives the wrong answer in a possible case, the theory has a problem.
- **"We should include freedom and dignity in the calculation."** This is a move away from pure utilitarianism toward the framework's position -- recognizing that welfare includes dimensions beyond pleasure.

The framework's response: **the utility monster shows that treating welfare as a scalar is wrong**. If you sum all welfare into a single number and maximize, you get monstrous conclusions in possible cases. The fix is to treat welfare as a vector (Chapter 3): hedonic tone, autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, meaning. You cannot aggregate these into a single number, so the "give everything to the monster" conclusion fails — even if the monster scores very high on hedonic tone, the resources taken from others reduce their autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, and meaning. The trade-off cannot be a simple sum.

### The Repugnant Conclusion

Derek Parfit, working carefully through the implications of utilitarianism for population ethics, identified what he called the repugnant conclusion: given utilitarianism's aggregation principle, a very large population of lives barely worth living can produce more total welfare than a smaller population of lives that are clearly wonderful. Therefore, utilitarianism seems to prefer the large barely-worth-living population.

Parfit himself found this "repugnant" — hence the name. It conflicts with strong intuitions about what makes a world good. Most people, asked to choose between a world of 10 billion thriving humans and a world of 100 billion humans whose lives are barely worth continuing, prefer the first. Pure utilitarianism prefers the second if the math works out.

Responses utilitarians offer:
- **"Maybe the intuition is wrong."** Perhaps we should accept the repugnant conclusion as counterintuitive but correct. Some serious utilitarians (including Parfit himself, at times) were willing to go this route.
- **"Adjust the utility function."** Use something other than a simple sum — perhaps average welfare, or a function that discounts as population grows. But each adjustment creates new problems (average welfare prefers eliminating unhappy people; discount functions are ad hoc).
- **"Introduce critical level utilitarianism."** Lives below a certain threshold don't count, even if they are technically "worth living." This is closer to the framework's position but is hard to specify non-arbitrarily.

The framework's response: **the repugnant conclusion is another consequence of treating welfare as a scalar you can aggregate indefinitely**. If welfare is multidimensional, and if a "barely worth living" life scores very low on multiple dimensions, then adding more such lives does not produce the equivalent welfare gain that pure summation suggests. The math of the repugnant conclusion depends on being able to freely add welfare across beings, which the vector view questions.

### The Transplant Case

Classic trolley-problem variant: a surgeon could save five dying patients by killing one healthy patient and harvesting their organs. Pure utilitarian math says: save five at the cost of one, net four lives saved. Therefore, kill the healthy patient.

Most people's moral intuitions reject this firmly. The doctor should not kill a patient to save others. But utilitarianism seems to endorse it.

Responses utilitarians offer:
- **Rule utilitarianism**: following a rule against killing innocents, rather than calculating case by case, produces better long-term outcomes. This is moving toward the framework's position.
- **"Rule compliance has its own utility."** People value living in a society where doctors don't kill patients, so the utility of the rule matters in addition to the direct utility of individual cases. This is also moving toward the framework.
- **"We have to weigh the downstream consequences."** If people knew doctors might kill them, trust would collapse, and long-term utility would decrease. Correct, but this is exactly the framework's argument for why rules work better than case-by-case calculation.

The framework's response: **the transplant case shows that pure case-by-case consequentialism fails**. The correct response is rule-based — don't kill innocents — because the rule produces better outcomes than the alternative. The framework's dual-process architecture (Chapters 9-10) explicitly defends this structure: Mode A (rules) handles the default, Mode B (calculation) is restricted to extreme cases that meet strict conditions. This absorbs the utilitarian insight (consequences matter) while avoiding the catastrophic implications of pure case-by-case maximization.

### The Demandingness Objection

Peter Singer and other utilitarian ethicists have argued that if utilitarianism is correct, we should be doing vastly more to help those in desperate need. If you can prevent a child's death for $5,000 through effective charity, you should do so -- and you should keep doing so until your own welfare equals the welfare gain from further giving. For most people in wealthy countries, this means radical lifestyle changes.

Singer accepts this conclusion. Most people find it impossibly demanding. A moral theory that demands what humans cannot actually do is not a practical moral theory.

Responses utilitarians offer:
- **"The demand is correct; we are just morally inadequate."** Singer's position. But a moral theory that everyone fails at is not providing usable guidance.
- **"Reasonable demandingness is achievable."** Modest commitments (10% of income is a common target) that most people in wealthy countries could meet. This waters down pure utilitarianism but is more practical.
- **"Supererogation is real."** Some acts are praiseworthy but not required. Pure utilitarianism has no room for supererogation -- anything short of maximum welfare is wrong. But the intuition that there is room for optional goodness is strong.

The framework's response: **the demandingness objection shows that pure maximization is too strong a requirement**. Moral theories should distinguish between what is required (a floor below which you should not fall) and what is praiseworthy (a ceiling that you should aspire to but are not obligated to reach). Pure utilitarianism collapses this distinction. The framework preserves it by recognizing multiple dimensions of moral practice — rules to follow (Mode A), obligations to fulfill, permissions to use, and aspirations to pursue. Not everything that would improve the world is obligatory.

### The Integrity Objection

Bernard Williams argued that pure utilitarianism requires agents to abandon their own projects, relationships, and commitments whenever doing so would produce slightly more welfare. A person who has devoted their life to their family would be obligated to leave their family to pursue a higher-welfare career. A person with specific skills would be obligated to use those skills wherever they produce the most welfare, regardless of personal meaning. The result: no one has a personal life; everyone is a node in a welfare-maximizing machine.

This, Williams argued, conflicts with something important about human integrity — the specific attachments, projects, and identities that make us who we are. A moral theory that requires us to abandon these is not asking us to be better people; it is asking us to stop being people at all.

Responses utilitarians offer:
- **"Personal attachments have welfare value."** Your love for your family produces welfare, so staying is justified. But this is weak — utilitarianism implies you should love your family exactly as much as it maximizes welfare, not as an end in itself.
- **"Integrity has instrumental value."** A person with stable identity is a more effective welfare-maximizer. This makes integrity a means rather than an end, which misses Williams's point.

The framework's response: **the integrity objection points at something important about human flourishing that pure utilitarianism misses**. Personal attachments are part of what makes a good life good, not merely instruments for producing generic welfare. The framework's complex value vector includes connection as a dimension of flourishing, and this dimension is not reducible to "the welfare you produce for others through your attachments." Your love for your children has value independent of its contribution to general welfare calculations.

## Additional Problems

Beyond the classic objections, pure utilitarianism faces several additional difficulties:

**1. The measurement problem.** Utilitarianism requires comparing welfare across people, across time, and across types of good. How do you compare your pleasure from a sunset to my pain from a toothache to a stranger's satisfaction from completing a difficult project? The theory requires these comparisons to be possible. In practice, they rarely are. Attempts to formalize interpersonal utility comparisons have been inconclusive.

**2. The expected value problem.** Utilitarianism requires calculating expected utility, which requires probability estimates that are often unavailable. When consequences are uncertain, the theory becomes hard to apply. The framework has the same problem to some extent, but we are explicit about the limits of calculation and provide rule-based backups (Chapter 10).

**3. The motivational problem.** Even if you accept utilitarianism intellectually, its motivational force is weak in specific cases. The generic injunction "maximize welfare" is abstract; concrete moral commitments (to your family, to honesty, to specific projects) are more motivating. Humans are not built to respond strongly to abstract welfare maximization. A moral theory that fails to motivate is a theory that doesn't get acted on, regardless of its theoretical virtues.

**4. The boundaries problem.** Utilitarianism does not naturally handle boundaries between individuals. Each person's welfare is folded into the aggregate, and the aggregate is what matters. But we have strong intuitions that people are not merely containers of welfare — that individuals have separate moral existence that cannot be dissolved into aggregates. The transplant case illustrates this: the five patients' welfare is counted, but so is the one patient's *being an individual* whose body cannot be used without consent.

**5. The time problem.** Utilitarianism aggregates across time as well as across people. Future welfare counts (at least on some versions), but the specific discount rate is contested. And the aggregation raises strange puzzles about identity across time — does your current self owe something to your future self in the way you owe something to a stranger?

These problems do not refute utilitarianism, but they add to the accumulated weight against the pure version. A theory with many problems, each of which requires ad hoc fixes, is suspect. The fixes taken together often add up to something that is no longer pure utilitarianism.

## What Utilitarianism Gets Right

The framework has absorbed much of what utilitarianism gets right. It is worth stating this directly.

**1. Welfare is central to morality.** The framework's valence axiom is essentially the utilitarian starting point: conscious experience matters morally. Anyone who denies this is not a serious moral theorist.

**2. Consequences matter.** The framework is not pure deontology; it takes consequences seriously. Rules are justified partly by the consequences of following them, and rules can be overridden (Mode B) when following them would produce catastrophic consequences.

**3. Scale matters.** The welfare of a million people is more important than the welfare of one, and the framework accepts this. Pure deontological frameworks often struggle with scale; the framework does not.

**4. Impartiality has its place.** Other things being equal, a stranger's suffering matters as much as your friend's. The framework accepts this as a default while also allowing for partial obligations to specific people (relationships, family, promises). The balance is not arbitrary; it reflects the insight that both partiality and impartiality track real moral features.

**5. Utilitarian analysis is useful for public policy.** When you are making decisions that affect many people whose specific relationships to you are varied, utilitarian-style analysis (who benefits, who bears costs, how much) is the right framework. This is why we use it extensively in Book V (politics) and Book VI (economics), even though we are not pure utilitarians.

**6. Moral progress is possible through welfare reasoning.** Utilitarian reasoning has driven many historical moral advances — abolition of slavery, extension of rights to women, attention to animal welfare, concern about future generations. The framework endorses this method of moral progress, which is essentially utilitarian in its structure.

**7. Demanding more than minimal compliance is correct.** Pure utilitarianism may ask too much, but it is right to ask for more than minimum morality. Most moral traditions let their adherents off too easily. The utilitarian insistence that you should actually do good, not just avoid doing bad, is a corrective the framework endorses.

## The Framework's Position, Precisely

Where exactly does the framework disagree with pure utilitarianism?

**1. Welfare is a vector, not a scalar.** We cannot simply sum welfare across people or across dimensions. This blocks the utility monster, the repugnant conclusion, and some aspects of the transplant case.

**2. Rules have independent weight.** Not because rules are metaphysically special, but because following rules reliably produces better outcomes than case-by-case calculation, given realistic human agents. This gives us Mode A as the default and Mode B as a narrow override.

**3. Individual boundaries matter.** You cannot sacrifice one person's welfare for another's without crossing a moral line that is not captured by simple aggregation. The framework's rights framework (Chapter 19) specifies where these lines are.

**4. Maximization is too strong.** The framework supports doing good, but it does not require maximizing welfare. There is room for personal projects, commitments, and identity that are not required to contribute to the maximum.

**5. The specification of welfare is richer.** Welfare includes hedonic tone, autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, and meaning. This is more complex than simple pleasure/pain utilitarianism and produces different answers in many specific cases.

**6. Supererogation is real.** Some acts are praiseworthy without being required. The framework recognizes this distinction.

**7. Partial obligations are real.** You have stronger obligations to people you are in specific relationships with (family, friends, colleagues, fellow citizens). This is not a departure from morality; it is part of how moral relationships actually work.

These are not minor differences. They produce different answers to specific questions. But they share utilitarianism's core commitment — that welfare is central to morality — while avoiding the failure modes of the pure version.

## The Modal Utilitarian

A useful concept for thinking about the framework's relationship to utilitarianism: the modal utilitarian. Imagine a utilitarian who has taken the classic objections seriously and modified their theory to address them. They end up with:

- A multidimensional welfare concept (avoiding utility monster problems).
- Rule-based defaults with narrow case-by-case overrides (avoiding transplant case problems).
- Recognition of individual boundaries and rights (avoiding sacrifice cases).
- Reasonable demandingness with supererogation (avoiding impossibility).
- Acceptance of partial obligations (avoiding integrity problems).
- Attention to empirical realities of measurement and prediction (avoiding the measurement problem).

This modified utilitarian is... approximately the framework. The distance between sophisticated contemporary utilitarianism and the framework's position is smaller than the rhetoric suggests. Many contemporary utilitarians (especially rule utilitarians, preference utilitarians, and those working on population ethics) are converging on positions that look like the framework.

This convergence is worth noting. If careful thinking about utilitarianism produces something close to the framework, and careful thinking from non-utilitarian starting points also produces something close to the framework, that is evidence the framework is tracking genuine moral features rather than arbitrary constructions. The agreement from multiple directions increases confidence in the conclusions, even as the terminology debates continue.

## The Verdict

The framework rejects pure utilitarianism but preserves most of its substance. The disagreement is:

- **About welfare** (vector vs. scalar)
- **About rules** (independent weight vs. purely derivative)
- **About boundaries** (individuals are separate vs. welfare is aggregate)
- **About demandingness** (reasonable vs. unlimited)
- **About personal projects** (integrity has value vs. purely instrumental)

The agreement is:

- **Welfare is morally central**
- **Consequences matter**
- **Scale matters**
- **Impartiality has its place**
- **Utilitarian analysis is useful for many public policy questions**
- **Moral progress happens through welfare reasoning**

This is not a dismissal. It is a critical engagement that takes utilitarianism seriously enough to absorb its insights while identifying its limits. The framework is closer to utilitarianism than to any other major moral tradition, and the debt is significant.

If a reader wants to continue thinking of themselves as a utilitarian after reading this chapter, that is fine. The label matters less than the substance. What matters is getting the substance right, and the framework and sophisticated utilitarianism are converging on similar answers to most specific questions, even when the labels and philosophical frameworks differ.

## Summary

1. **Utilitarianism's strengths** include taking welfare seriously, impartiality, clear action-guidance, empirical grounding, historical progressiveness, handling scale, and sophisticated responses to classical objections.

2. **The classic objections** (utility monster, repugnant conclusion, transplant case, demandingness, integrity) all point at genuine problems with pure utilitarianism.

3. **The framework's responses** to these objections involve treating welfare as a vector (not a scalar), giving rules independent weight, recognizing individual boundaries, distinguishing obligation from aspiration, and including personal projects in the conception of flourishing.

4. **Additional problems** for pure utilitarianism include measurement, expected value, motivation, boundaries between persons, and time.

5. **The framework agrees with utilitarianism** on most core commitments: welfare is central, consequences matter, scale matters, impartiality has its place, and moral progress happens through welfare reasoning.

6. **The modal utilitarian** — one who has modified their theory to handle the objections — ends up very close to the framework's position. The distance between sophisticated utilitarianism and the framework is smaller than the rhetoric suggests.

7. **The verdict**: reject pure utilitarianism, keep most of its substance, recognize the debt.

The next chapter addresses the opposite tradition: pure deontology, particularly Kantian ethics. If utilitarianism is too consequentialist, Kantianism may be too rule-bound. The framework's dual-process architecture should handle both problems, but we must engage directly with the Kantian tradition to show this.
