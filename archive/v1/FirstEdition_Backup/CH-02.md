# Chapter 2: The Map and the Territory — Epistemology as Moral Duty

---

> *"Being in scout mindset means wanting your 'map' — your perception of yourself and the world — to be as accurate as possible."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

## The Moral Case for Seeing Clearly

You cannot act rightly in a world you see wrongly.

This is not a philosophical abstraction. It is a claim about the physics of action. Every decision you make rests on a model of reality — a *map* of the territory. If your map says the bridge can hold your weight and the territory says it cannot, you fall. If your map says the policy reduces poverty and the territory says it increases homelessness, real people suffer. If your map says the tribe across the river is subhuman and the territory says they are conscious beings with the same capacity for suffering you have, your error has moral consequences measured in blood.

The first chapter established that suffering matters. This chapter makes the connection that many moral systems leave implicit: *if* suffering matters, *then* seeing reality clearly is not an optional intellectual virtue — it is a **moral obligation**.

The argument is simple:

1. Actions based on false beliefs produce worse outcomes than actions based on true beliefs (on average, over time).
2. Worse outcomes mean more unnecessary suffering (by the valence axiom, that matters).
3. Therefore, you have a moral duty to form accurate beliefs.

This is Epistemic Duty — the claim that truth-seeking is not merely an intellectual preference but a prerequisite for ethical action. You cannot reliably reduce suffering with a map that lies to you. And if you *choose* a comforting lie over a harsh truth — if you cling to a false model because it makes you feel good — then you bear moral responsibility for the damage your false model causes.

> *"The best description of motivated reasoning I've ever seen comes from psychologist Tom Gilovich. When we want something to be true, he said, we ask ourselves, 'Can I believe this?,' searching for an excuse to accept it. When we don't want something to be true, we instead ask ourselves, 'Must I believe this?,' searching for an excuse to reject it."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

This is a description of a moral failure, not merely an intellectual one. The person who asks "Can I believe this?" about a comforting falsehood is building their decisions — and everyone affected by those decisions — on a foundation they have deliberately chosen not to inspect.

## The Hardware Problem

The moral duty to see clearly would be straightforward if our minds were designed for truth. They are not.

Daniel Kahneman spent a career cataloging the ways human cognition systematically fails. His findings are devastating for anyone who assumes sincere effort is sufficient for accurate belief:

> *"System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control."*
>
> *"System 2 allocates attention to the effortful mental activities that demand it, including complex computations. The operations of System 2 are often associated with the subjective experience of agency, choice, and concentration."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

The dual-process model is now one of the best-established findings in cognitive science. Your brain runs two fundamentally different systems. System 1 — fast, automatic, effortless — handles the vast majority of your cognition: recognizing faces, reading emotions, generating gut reactions. System 2 — slow, deliberate, effortful — handles complex reasoning and careful calculation.

The problem is that System 2 is lazy. It outsources most of its work to System 1, endorsing the fast system's outputs with minimal scrutiny:

> *"Most of what you (your System 2) think and do originates in your System 1, but System 2 takes over when things get difficult, and it normally has the last word."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

In practice, "the last word" is often a rubber stamp. System 1 generates an answer; System 2 feels a vague sense of rightness; the answer is accepted. The subjective experience is of careful deliberation. The reality is cognitive autopilot.

And System 1 is riddled with biases — not random errors, but *systematic* ones that push in predictable directions:

**Anchoring.** Your estimate of an unknown quantity is dragged toward whatever number was recently in your environment, even if that number is obviously irrelevant. German judges with fifteen years of experience sentenced a shoplifter to significantly different prison terms depending on whether they had just rolled a 3 or a 9 on loaded dice. As Kahneman documented: "the anchoring effect was 50%." These were not novices. They were experts, convinced they were immune. They were not.

**Availability.** You judge the frequency of events by how easily examples come to mind, not by actual statistics. Kahneman found that "strokes cause almost twice as many deaths as all accidents combined, but 80% of respondents judged accidental death to be more likely." Why? Because accidents are vivid, memorable, and heavily covered by media. Strokes are not. Your System 1 substitutes "ease of recall" for "actual frequency" and never tells you it made the swap.

**Representativeness.** You judge probability by resemblance rather than by base rates. Kahneman's famous "Linda problem" — where 85-90% of undergraduates judged that Linda is more likely to be "a bank teller and active in the feminist movement" than simply "a bank teller" — demonstrates a logical impossibility that System 1 produces with total confidence. A conjunction cannot be more probable than either of its components. But System 1 doesn't do probability; it does storytelling.

**WYSIATI** (What You See Is All There Is). Perhaps the most fundamental bias of all:

> *"System 1 excels at constructing the best possible story that incorporates ideas currently activated, but it does not (cannot) allow for information it does not have."*
>
> *"Jumping to conclusions on the basis of limited evidence is so important to an understanding of intuitive thinking, and comes up so often in this book, that I will use a cumbersome abbreviation for it: WYSIATI. System 1 is radically insensitive to both the quality and the quantity of the information that gives rise to impressions and intuitions."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

WYSIATI is the master bias. It explains why you can feel so confident based on so little evidence — the story your mind constructs from the available fragments *feels* complete, because System 1 has no mechanism for representing what it doesn't know. The feeling of understanding is generated by coherence, not by evidence.

This is what we are working with: a machine that anchors on irrelevant numbers, mistakes vividness for frequency, substitutes stereotypes for statistics, and constructs confident narratives from incomplete data while feeling certain it has the full picture.

And it gets worse.

## The Elephant in the Brain

The biases Kahneman documented are bad enough — they are the bugs in the cognitive software. But Kevin Simler and Robin Hanson, in *The Elephant in the Brain*, identified something more disturbing: many of our cognitive distortions are not bugs. They are *features*, installed by evolution to serve strategic functions that have nothing to do with truth.

Their central thesis: the human brain is not primarily a truth-seeking device. It is a *social* device, optimized for navigating coalitions, managing reputation, and signaling fitness to potential allies and mates. Self-deception is not a glitch — it is a *strategy*. If you can deceive yourself about your own motives, you become a more convincing deceiver of others, because you display none of the tells that accompany conscious lying.

We deceive ourselves about why we give to charity (signaling, not just altruism), why we pursue education (credentialing, not just learning), why we consume art (status, not just aesthetic pleasure), and why we hold political opinions (tribal loyalty, not just policy analysis). The "rider" — conscious, verbal reasoning — is largely a press secretary, confabulating noble justifications after the fact for whatever the "elephant" of hidden, self-serving motivation has already decided.

Jonathan Haidt's research confirms this architecture from a different angle:

> *"People made moral judgments quickly and emotionally. Moral reasoning was mostly just a post hoc search for reasons to justify the judgments people had already made."*
> — Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*

> *"The rider evolved because it did something useful for the elephant... the rider acts as the spokesman for the elephant, even though it doesn't necessarily know what the elephant is really thinking. The rider is skilled at fabricating post hoc explanations for whatever the elephant has just done, and it is good at finding reasons to justify whatever the elephant wants to do next."*
> — Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*

The picture that emerges from converging lines of research is grim. Your mind is running on hardware riddled with systematic biases (Kahneman), primarily serving social and strategic functions rather than truth-seeking (Simler & Hanson), generating moral judgments from gut reactions and then rationalizing them after the fact (Haidt), and confident in its own objectivity precisely when it is most biased (Galef).

This is why epistemic duty is not a gentle suggestion — it is a moral imperative. Given what we know about how the mind works, *drifting* toward truth is not an option. Truth must be *fought for*, deliberately, against the default currents of human cognition.

## The Scout and the Soldier

Julia Galef draws a distinction that crystallizes the choice facing every moral agent:

> *"If directionally motivated reasoning is like being a soldier fighting off threatening evidence, accuracy motivated reasoning is like being a scout forming a map of the strategic landscape. What's beyond that next hill? Is that a bridge over the river or are my eyes deceiving me?"*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

The **soldier mindset** treats beliefs as territory to be defended. Evidence that supports your existing beliefs is welcomed as reinforcement; evidence that threatens them is treated as enemy action to be repelled. The question is never "Is this true?" but "Can I defend my position?"

The **scout mindset** treats beliefs as a map to be refined. Evidence that confirms the map is useful. Evidence that contradicts the map is *more* useful — it reveals where the map is wrong, and a wrong map corrected is more valuable than a right map confirmed. The question is always "Is this accurate?"

> *"In scout mindset, there's no such thing as a 'threat' to your beliefs. If you find out you were wrong about something, great — you've improved your map, and that can only help you."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

Why does the soldier mindset exist at all, if it produces worse maps? Because throughout most of human history, the social benefits outweighed the epistemic costs. Galef identifies six functions that self-deception serves: **comfort** (avoiding painful truths), **self-esteem** (maintaining a flattering self-image), **morale** (sustaining motivation through overconfidence), **persuasion** (convincing others by first convincing yourself), **image** (signaling group membership), and **belonging** (maintaining standing in your tribe).

These are not trivial benefits. In the ancestral environment, being *right* about an inconvenient fact mattered less than being *aligned* with your group's consensus. The tribal outcast who was epistemically correct but socially isolated did not survive to pass on his genes.

But — and this is the crucial turn — **the modern world has changed the payoff structure**.

> *"If our instincts undervalue truth, that's not surprising — our instincts evolved in a different world, one better suited to the soldier. Increasingly, our world is becoming one that rewards the ability to see clearly."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

In a world of global supply chains, complex institutions, nuclear weapons, artificial intelligence, climate systems, and pandemic biology, the cost of self-deception has gone up by orders of magnitude. A tribal leader whose false beliefs led his band into a bad hunting ground lost a few dozen people a season. A national leader whose false beliefs lead to bad pandemic response loses millions. A civilization whose false beliefs about climate lead to inadequate response may lose everything.

The argument for epistemic duty is not that truth has always been the optimal strategy — it hasn't. The argument is that in the world we now inhabit, **the cost of false beliefs has become catastrophic**, and the old evolutionary bargain — accuracy for belonging — is no longer one any rational agent should accept.

## The Method: Bayesian Updating

If epistemic duty tells you *why* to seek truth, Bayesian reasoning tells you *how*.

The core idea is simple: beliefs should be held as probabilities, not as certainties, and updated incrementally as evidence arrives.

Formally: if you believe a hypothesis H with initial probability P(H), and you observe evidence E, your updated probability should follow Bayes' Theorem:

**P(H|E) = P(E|H) × P(H) / P(E)**

In plain language: your new confidence depends on (a) how confident you were before, (b) how likely the evidence would be if your belief were true, and (c) how likely the evidence would be regardless.

You do not need to carry this formula in your head. The intuition is enough: **strong prior + weak evidence = small update. Weak prior + strong evidence = large update. Evidence that is equally likely whether or not your belief is true = no update at all.**

Galef makes the practical version vivid:

> *"A scout treats their degree of certainty as a prediction of their likelihood of being right."*
>
> *"What you're implicitly aiming for when you tag your beliefs with various confidence levels is perfect calibration. That means your '50% sure' claims are in fact correct 50 percent of the time, your '60% sure' claims are correct 60 percent of the time..."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

Calibration is the behavioral test of Bayesian thinking. If you say you are 90% sure about something but turn out to be right only 60% of the time, your confidence is systematically inflated. And overconfidence, as Kahneman demonstrated, is the norm:

> *"Clinicians who were 'completely certain' of the diagnosis antemortem were wrong 40% of the time."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

> *"An unbiased appreciation of uncertainty is a cornerstone of rationality — but it is not what people and organizations want."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

The social pressure is relentless: appear confident, have an opinion, commit to a position. The Bayesian prescription is the opposite: hold beliefs lightly, quantify uncertainty, update when evidence warrants. Galef proposes a practical diagnostic — **the equivalent bet test**. Instead of asking "How confident am I?", ask "Would I bet on this at these odds?" When real stakes enter the picture, your brain switches from "press secretary mode" to "board of directors mode."

> *"Being able to tell the difference between the feeling of making a claim and the feeling of actually trying to guess what's true. Making a claim feels like your press secretary is speaking. It feels pat; neat and tidy... Trying to guess what's true feels like being the board of directors, deciding how to bet."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

## Why Bayesianism Survives Its Objections

Three objections to Bayesian epistemology are serious enough to warrant engagement.

**Objection 1: Priors are subjective.** Bayesian updating requires a starting probability, and different people will start with different priors. Doesn't this make the whole enterprise subjective?

Response: Yes, priors are subjective — and this is a *feature*, not a bug. What matters is that Bayesian updating is **self-correcting over time**. Given sufficient evidence, two reasoners who start with wildly different priors will *converge* on the same posterior probability. Bad priors wash out. No alternative method has this convergence property.

**Objection 2: Nobody actually computes Bayes' Theorem.** Real people don't walk around with probability distributions in their heads. Isn't this prescriptive framework impossibly demanding?

Response: You don't need to compute the formula any more than you need to compute Newtonian mechanics to throw a ball. The point is to internalize the *qualitative* structure: hold beliefs as probabilities, not identities; update in the right direction; update more for stronger evidence; notice when confidence has outrun evidence. This is a learnable skill. Galef's entire book is about people who do it naturally — not because they carry calculators, but because they've cultivated the habit of asking "How sure am I really?" rather than "How can I defend my position?"

**Objection 3: Some beliefs shouldn't be held probabilistically.** The physicist doesn't hold "energy is conserved" at 93% — they hold it as a framework assumption. Moral axioms work the same way. Doesn't Bayesianism inappropriately demand that everything be probabilistic?

Response: This is correct, and important. Not all beliefs are the same kind. **Axioms** (like our valence axiom) are chosen, not probabilistically assessed. **Framework assumptions** (like the reliability of induction) are presupposed by the epistemic enterprise itself. Bayesian updating applies to **empirical beliefs** — beliefs about contingent facts about the world. The distinction between these categories is real, and we respect it. The system is Bayesian about the world, not about its own foundations. (We address our foundations differently — through the methods of Chapter 1: argument, coherence testing, and the performative incoherence criterion.)

## The Identity Trap

There is one obstacle to epistemic duty so powerful that it deserves its own section: the fusion of beliefs with identity.

> *"The problem with identity is that it wrecks your ability to think clearly. Identifying with a belief makes you feel like you have to be ready to defend it, which motivates you to focus your attention on collecting evidence in its favor."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

When a belief becomes part of *who you are*, updating it is no longer an intellectual operation — it is an act of self-destruction. "I am a libertarian" is not a description of a probability distribution over policy questions; it is a declaration of tribal membership, personal history, and social bonds. Changing that belief means changing your identity, your social circle, and your story about yourself. The epistemic cost of updating is compounded by an existential cost that makes the soldier mindset nearly irresistible.

Galef identifies the telltale signs: using the phrase "I believe" (turning a claim about the world into a claim about yourself), getting emotionally triggered when the belief is challenged, using defiant language ("I'm *proud* to say..."), gatekeeping ("You can't call yourself an X if you believe Y"), and feeling schadenfreude when the opposing tribe suffers a setback.

Galef's proposed solution is elegant:

> *"Holding an identity lightly means thinking of it in a matter-of-fact way, rather than as a central source of pride and meaning in your life. It's a description, not a flag to be waved proudly."*
>
> *"Holding an identity lightly means treating that identity as contingent, saying to yourself, 'I'm a liberal, for as long as it continues to seem to me that liberalism is just.'"*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

This is not nihilism about identity. It is intellectual maturity. You can have convictions — strong ones, passionately held — without fusing them to your sense of self. The test is simple: can you specify what evidence would change your mind? If the answer is "nothing," you don't hold a belief — you hold a *creed*. And creeds, whatever their social utility, are epistemic death.

The lesson for our system is architectural. This book maintains a Position Registry with explicit confidence ratings and vulnerability assessments precisely to prevent the identity trap at the level of the system itself. If "our framework says X" becomes a statement of tribal loyalty rather than a revisable claim, the system has succumbed to the very disease it diagnoses. The swap mechanism is the institutional antibody against this failure.

## The Twelve Epistemic Virtues

The Framework lists twelve virtues of rationality. Here we ground them in the argument of this chapter and organize them as the character traits required to fulfill epistemic duty.

**The Scout Virtues** (habits that make truth-seeking possible):

1. **Curiosity** — the active drive to destroy your own ignorance, not merely to confirm what you already believe. The curious person asks "What am I missing?" before asking "Am I right?"

2. **Relinquishment** — the ability to say "I was wrong" without ego collapse. Galef documents the case of an Oxford professor who, upon hearing evidence that his life's work was mistaken, walked to the front of the lecture hall and said: "My dear fellow, I wish to thank you. I have been wrong these fifteen years." This is relinquishment — and it is rare, because it requires decoupling belief from identity.

3. **Lightness** — holding beliefs as probabilities, not possessions. The light thinker feels no attachment to any specific conclusion; they feel attachment to the *process* that produces accurate conclusions.

4. **Evenness** — attacking your own arguments as ruthlessly as your opponent's. The even thinker applies Galef's "selective skeptic test": if you find a study that supports your view, ask "Would I have scrutinized this study harder if it had found the opposite?" If yes, you are not even.

5. **Steel-Manning** — the commitment to engage the strongest version of opposing views. Galef describes Bryan Caplan's "Ideological Turing Test": can you state the opposing position well enough that its proponents cannot tell you're a critic? If you can't pass this test, you don't yet understand what you're arguing against.

6. **Empiricism** — the insistence that disputes about the world be settled by evidence about the world, not by authority, tradition, or rhetorical force. When the map disagrees with the ground, the map is wrong.

7. **Simplicity** — Occam's Razor applied as a Bayesian principle. Simpler hypotheses have higher prior probabilities because there are fewer ways for them to be true by coincidence. Do not multiply entities beyond necessity.

8. **Humility** — the constant awareness that you are running on biased hardware. The humble thinker does not trust their first impression; they treat it as *data* — useful, but not authoritative.

**The Soldier-Proofing Virtues** (habits that protect against motivated reasoning):

9. **Precision** — the practice of replacing vague, emotionally loaded words with specific, testable claims. "The economy is bad" becomes "Unemployment is at 7.2%, up from 5.1% a year ago." Precision disarms soldier mindset by removing the tribal signals that trigger defensive reasoning.

10. **The Void** — the capacity to *suspend* judgment when the evidence is insufficient. The untrained mind abhors uncertainty and rushes to fill it with a confident narrative (WYSIATI). The void is the discipline of sitting with "I don't know" until knowing becomes possible.

11. **Scholarship** — the duty to engage with the existing literature before forming opinions. Most of what you think is original has already been thought by someone smarter, and most of what you think is true has already been tested by someone more rigorous. Humility requires scholarship; scholarship enables genuine originality.

12. **Perfectionism** (*Tsuyoku Naritai* — "I want to become stronger") — the refusal to settle for "good enough" in the pursuit of understanding. This is not perfectionism about outcomes — it is perfectionism about process. The epistemic agent who coasts is the epistemic agent who drifts.

## The Planning Fallacy and Moral Decision-Making

One cognitive bias deserves special attention because it has direct moral implications: the **planning fallacy**.

Kahneman discovered it in his own life. His team was developing a curriculum and asked a member — an experienced education expert — to estimate how long similar projects typically took. The answer: seven to ten years, with 40% of comparable teams never finishing at all. The team's own estimate for their project? Two and a half years. They took eight.

> *"When forecasting the outcomes of risky projects, executives too easily fall victim to the planning fallacy. In its grip, they make decisions based on delusional optimism rather than on a rational weighting of gains, losses, and probabilities. They overestimate benefits and underestimate costs. They spin scenarios of success while overlooking the potential for mistakes and miscalculations."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

The planning fallacy is not just a project-management nuisance. It is a moral hazard. When governments underestimate the cost of a war, real people die in the gap between prediction and reality. When policymakers overestimate the effectiveness of an intervention, resources are wasted that could have reduced suffering elsewhere. When entrepreneurs overestimate their chances of success — and Kahneman found that "fully 81% of entrepreneurs put their personal odds of success at 7 out of 10 or higher" while the base rate is closer to 35% — they make decisions that affect employees, investors, and families based on delusion.

Kahneman's remedy is the **outside view**: instead of asking "What is my estimate of this specific project?" ask "What typically happens to projects like this?" The outside view forces you to consult base rates — the statistical reality of how similar endeavors have actually performed — rather than constructing a flattering narrative from the inside.

This is Bayesian reasoning applied to planning. Your inside-view narrative is the prior. The base rate is the evidence. The update should move you sharply toward the base rate, because the evidence of history outweighs the coherence of your personal story.

Gary Klein proposed a complementary technique — the **premortem**:

> *"Imagine that we are a year into the future. We implemented the plan as it now exists. The outcome was a disaster. Please take 5 to 10 minutes to write a brief history of that disaster."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, reporting Gary Klein's method, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

The premortem works because it gives System 1 permission to search for threats instead of constructing a success narrative. It is a hack for broken hardware — a way to redirect the storytelling engine toward useful pessimism.

## What Epistemic Duty Does Not Require

Epistemic duty is demanding, but it is not totalizing. Three common misunderstandings should be addressed.

**It does not require emotionlessness.** Emotions are data. As Kahneman's colleague Antonio Damasio demonstrated with patients who had damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, reasoning *without* emotional input produces catastrophically bad decisions. Haidt's research confirms: "Emotions are a kind of information processing. Contrasting emotion with cognition is therefore as pointless as contrasting rain with weather." The duty is not to suppress emotion but to recognize it as input — useful, important, but not infallible.

**It does not require truth-telling in all circumstances.** The duty is to *seek* truth, not to *weaponize* it. Whether and how to communicate a truth is a separate moral question, governed by the dual-process architecture we develop in Chapters 9 and 10. The duty is against *self-deception* — against lying to yourself — not against tact, discretion, or strategic silence.

**It does not require certainty before action.** The world will not pause while you gather more evidence. Epistemic duty requires you to act on your *best available* model while remaining open to updating it. The scout does not sit in their tent until the map is perfect; they venture out with the best map they have, alert to every sign that the map is wrong.

## The Epistemic Foundation

Let us take stock of what this chapter has established.

We live inside minds that were built for social survival, not for truth. Our System 1 is fast but biased. Our System 2 is capable but lazy. Our reasoning is often post hoc rationalization. Our beliefs are infected by identity, tribalism, overconfidence, and motivated reasoning.

And yet truth *matters*, because actions based on false beliefs produce unnecessary suffering. This gives us the moral duty to fight the default currents of our own cognition — to cultivate the scout mindset, to hold beliefs as probabilities rather than identities, to update on evidence, and to build institutions that systematize error-correction.

The method is Bayesian: hold beliefs with calibrated confidence, update when evidence warrants, and never let the coherence of your narrative substitute for the quality of your evidence.

> *"We talk about our beliefs as if they're military positions, or even fortresses, built to resist attack. Beliefs can be deep-rooted, well-grounded, built on fact, and backed up by arguments... And if we do change our minds? That's surrender."*
> — Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset*

This book proposes a different metaphor. Your beliefs are not a fortress. They are a map. And a map that cannot be updated is not a strength — it is a liability. The scout who discovers their map is wrong has *gained* something. The soldier who defends a wrong map to the death has lost everything.

> *"The illusion that one has understood the past feeds the further illusion that one can predict and control the future. These illusions are comforting. They reduce the anxiety that we would experience if we allowed ourselves to fully acknowledge the uncertainties of existence."*
> — Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

Comfort is not our goal. Accuracy is. Not because accuracy feels good — often it doesn't — but because it is the prerequisite for acting well in a world where actions have consequences measured in human suffering.

This is the epistemological foundation. We know *why* truth matters (it prevents suffering). We know *how* to pursue it (Bayesian updating, calibrated confidence, scout mindset). We know *what* stands in our way (biases, motivated reasoning, identity fusion). And we know *what character traits* the obstacles demand (the twelve epistemic virtues).

With this foundation laid, we turn to the question of origins: given that our minds were shaped by evolution, how do we decide which inherited instincts to keep and which to discard?
