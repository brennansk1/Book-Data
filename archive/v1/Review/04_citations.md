# Phase 4: Source, Citation, and Quote Verification

**Date:** 2026-04-05
**Scope:** All 48 chapter drafts in `Book/Drafts/`
**Method:** Systematic extraction of all block quotes, epigraphs, syllabus entries, and empirical claims; verification against known texts using direct knowledge of the primary sources.
**Limitation:** WebSearch and WebFetch were unavailable during this review. All verification is based on the reviewer's direct knowledge of the cited texts. Quotes flagged as UNVERIFIABLE require follow-up with physical or digital copies of the source texts.

---

## 4A. Direct Quote Verification

### Summary Statistics

- **Total block quotes found:** ~130+ across 48 chapters
- **Quotes from identifiable primary sources:** ~115
- **Quotes attributed to the framework itself or anonymous:** ~15
- **Quotes verified as CORRECT or LIKELY CORRECT:** ~95
- **Quotes flagged for problems:** 14

### Methodology

Each quote was checked for:
1. Is the quote real (i.e., does the attributed author actually say something like this in the attributed work)?
2. Is the attribution correct (right author, right work)?
3. Is the wording accurate (exact or close paraphrase)?

Confidence levels: VERIFIED = high confidence the quote is real and correctly attributed; LIKELY CORRECT = the quote matches the author's known positions and style but exact wording cannot be confirmed without the physical text; FLAGGED = a problem was identified; FABRICATED = the quote does not appear in the attributed source.

**Verification:** This refers to a real study by Birte Englich, Thomas Mussweiler, and Fritz Strack (2006), "Playing Dice With Criminal Sentences: The Influence of Irrelevant Anchors on Experts' Judicial Decision Making," published in *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*. The study is real and the description is accurate. The "50% anchoring effect" statistic Kahneman reports is from this study.

**Status:** VERIFIED. Severity: None.

### Claim 2: Strokes vs. accidents death perception (CH-02, line 57)

**Claim:** "strokes cause almost twice as many deaths as all accidents combined, but 80% of respondents judged accidental death to be more likely"

**Verification:** This statistic comes from a classic study by Lichtenstein, Slovic, Fischhoff, Layman, and Combs (1978), reported in Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. The specific numbers (strokes cause ~2x as many deaths, 80% misjudgment) are as Kahneman reports them. Note: The underlying mortality statistics were accurate for the late 1970s when the study was conducted. Current mortality ratios may differ.

### Commendations

The book demonstrates notably good citation practices in several areas:

1. **The Durant/Aristotle handling (CH-11, CH-42):** The "We are what we repeatedly do" misattribution is one of the most common in popular philosophy. The book correctly identifies it as Will Durant's paraphrase, not Aristotle's words, both times it appears. This is exemplary.

2. **Honest labeling of uncertain attributions:** CH-10, CH-13, CH-20, CH-30, CH-33, and CH-35 all use qualifying language ("attributed to," "approximately," "source uncertain," "a synthesis of") when the source is uncertain. This is much better practice than presenting uncertain quotes as definitive.

3. **Accurate Akademie edition references (CH-39):** The Kant citations include correct AK pagination (4:394, 4:402, 4:429), demonstrating familiarity with the primary text.

4. **Accurate Bekker numbers (CH-42):** The Aristotle citations include correct Bekker pagination (1094a, 1098a).

5. **Accurate Summa references (CH-44):** The Aquinas citations include correct Question and Article references from the *Summa Theologica*.

6. **Empirical claims are well-sourced and accurately stated:** All 10 empirical claims spot-checked were verified as accurate or defensibly stated within appropriate ranges.

7. **No fabricated quotes detected among primary philosophical sources.** Every Kant, Rawls, Aristotle, Mill, Axelrod, Schelling, Dawkins, and Haidt quote checked against known texts appears to be genuine.

### Overall Assessment

The book's citation practices are **above average for a philosophy manuscript** but have **a handful of problems that need correction before publication**. The most serious are the apocryphal Marcus Aurelius epigraph (CH-16), the McMahan/Kamm conflation in the syllabus (CH-47), and the likely apocryphal Mark Twain epigraph (CH-47). The in-text quotation of primary philosophical sources (Kant, Rawls, Aristotle, Axelrod, Schelling, Dawkins, Haidt, Singer, Ostrom) is consistently accurate, which is the area that matters most for the book's credibility.
