## Patterns Observed Across Books I-III

1. **Most common issue: redundancy.** Nearly every chapter restated its thesis or key insight at least once more than necessary. The most frequent pattern was a paragraph that made the point, followed by another paragraph that made the same point with slightly different words.

2. **Hedging was minimal.** These chapters are written with unusual confidence for a philosophy text. Very little "it seems possible that perhaps" language. The few instances of excess hedging were cut (e.g., "we do not claim it is definitively correct. But it is a serious position defended by serious philosophers").

3. **Paragraph length was generally well-controlled.** A few paragraphs in Chapters 2, 3, and 5 exceeded 200 words and were trimmed or split.

4. **Transitions were strong throughout.** Very few weak transitions needed repair; the chapters flow well as written. Where transitions were added, they were minimal (a sentence or phrase to bridge two paragraphs).

5. **Jargon was appropriate for the audience.** Technical terms (Nash equilibrium, performative incoherence, compatibilism, Pigovian tax) are consistently explained on first use. No changes were needed for jargon without payoff.

6. **No substantive arguments, direct quotes, section headings, or epigraphs were altered.**
