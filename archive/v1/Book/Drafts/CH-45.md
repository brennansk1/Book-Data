# Chapter 45: Against Natural Law Theory

---

> *"The light of natural reason, whereby we discern what is good and what is evil, which is the function of the natural law, is nothing else than an imprint on us of the Divine light."*
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, I-II, Q.91, A.2

---

## The Rival

Natural Law Theory is one of the oldest and most institutionally consequential moral frameworks in Western thought. It holds that moral truths are not invented by human beings, not revealed by sporadic divine commands, and not constructed from coordination pressures — they are **written into the structure of reality itself**, specifically into the nature of the things that morality concerns. To know what is good for a human being, you look at what a human being *is*. To know what is right, you look at what human flourishing consists in, which is fixed by human nature, which is fixed by the intelligent ordering of the cosmos.

This framework has powerful adherents. The Thomistic synthesis built by Aquinas in the thirteenth century remains the official moral theology of the Roman Catholic Church and shapes the moral reasoning of over a billion people. In the twentieth century, John Finnis, Germain Grisez, and Robert George developed "new natural law theory," which detaches the core arguments from explicit Aristotelian metaphysics and defends them in terms accessible to secular readers. Natural law undergirds much of international human rights law, the Nuremberg prosecutions (which appealed to a law higher than positive German law), and serious strands of contemporary jurisprudence.

It is the most sophisticated non-theistic-in-practice alternative to our framework: theistic in its foundations but argued at length in terms of reason, nature, and flourishing rather than scriptural commands. For this reason it demands a different kind of engagement than Chapter 37's treatment of Divine Command Theory. Natural law is not "God said so" — it is "reason, reflecting on what human beings are, discovers what human beings should do." That is a serious claim.

This chapter steel-mans natural law in both its Thomistic and new natural law forms, identifies what the framework owes it (which is considerable), and then shows where it breaks down.

## Steel-Manning Natural Law: The Thomistic Version

Aquinas's treatment in the *Summa Theologica* (Prima Secundae, Questions 90–97) is the canonical statement. The structure is precise:

**Law is ordered reason.** Aquinas defines law as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated" (Q.90, A.4). This is not arbitrary will — divine or human — but reason directed to the good of those subject to the law. A law that violates this definition is not merely a bad law; it is not genuinely a law at all. "Lex iniusta non est lex" — an unjust law is no law.

**Natural law is eternal law reflected in rational creatures.** Behind natural law stands eternal law: God's own reason, which orders all of creation toward its ends. Irrational creatures participate in eternal law by their natural operations (fire burns, water flows, plants grow). Rational creatures participate in a distinctive way — by *understanding* the order they are part of and freely directing themselves toward their end. This rational participation in eternal law is the natural law. As Aquinas puts it, "the natural law is promulgated by the very fact that God instilled it into man's mind so as to be known by him naturally" (Q.90, A.4, ad 1).

**The first precept is good is to be done.** Aquinas holds that practical reason has first principles analogous to the first principles of theoretical reason. The first indemonstrable principle of theoretical reason is the law of non-contradiction. The first precept of practical reason is this:

> *"Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided. All other precepts of the natural law are based upon this."*
> — Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, I-II, Q.94, A.2

This is not derived. It is self-evident to practical reason. Just as no one can coherently deny the law of non-contradiction (because denial presupposes it), no one can coherently act without treating some end as good and pursuing it. "Every agent acts for an end under the aspect of good."

**Specific precepts derive from natural inclinations.** From the first precept, more specific content comes via reflection on the natural inclinations of the human being. Aquinas distinguishes three levels:

1. **Inclinations shared with all substances**: self-preservation. "Whatever is a means of preserving human life, and of warding off its obstacles, belongs to the natural law."
2. **Inclinations shared with other animals**: reproduction and education of offspring. "Those things are said to belong to the natural law which nature has taught to all animals, such as sexual intercourse, education of offspring and so forth."
3. **Inclinations proper to human rational nature**: "to know the truth about God, and to live in society; and in this respect, whatever pertains to this inclination belongs to the natural law; for instance, to shun ignorance, to avoid offending those among whom one has to live, and other such things."

Moral reasoning, on this picture, is the articulation of what these inclinations, understood rightly, require. You discover your duties by understanding your nature.

**Natural law is universal and (mostly) unchangeable.** Because natural law is grounded in what human beings are, and human nature does not change, the natural law is the same for all human beings across all times and places (Q.94, A.4). Aquinas allows for some variation at the level of secondary principles (because particular circumstances can affect how a principle applies) but not at the level of the first precept and its most proximate specifications.

**Human law should be derived from natural law.** Positive law — the actual legal enactments of human communities — is legitimate only when it is consistent with natural law. Laws contrary to the natural law are "acts of violence rather than laws" (Q.96, A.4). This is not a recipe for disobedience in every case (Aquinas is cautious about when one must actively disobey) but it establishes a standard above positive law by which positive law can be judged.

This framework has features that should be noted before critique. It is **rational** rather than voluntarist -- moral truths are discovered by reason, not commanded by will. It is **teleological** -- things have ends built into them, and moral reasoning reflects on those ends. It is **objective**, **universal**, and **integrated with politics and law** -- moral theory grounds legal theory, which grounds institutional practice.

## Steel-Manning Natural Law: The New Natural Law Version

The Thomistic version depends on metaphysical commitments (teleological biology, eternal law, rational theism) that many contemporary readers do not share. Finnis and Grisez's "new natural law theory," beginning with Finnis's *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980), reformulates the core argument without requiring those commitments.

**Basic goods.** Finnis argues that reflection on human action reveals a set of **basic goods** — things that are intelligibly valuable as ends in themselves, not merely as means to something else. His list, with some variation over the years: life (including health and bodily integrity), knowledge (truth), play, aesthetic experience, sociability (friendship), practical reasonableness, and religion (or, in later versions, harmony with a transcendent source of meaning). These are not the same as felt desires; they are the ends that make desires intelligible.

**Incommensurability.** The basic goods are irreducibly plural. You cannot trade off knowledge against friendship on a common scale, because they are different kinds of good. This is a critical move: it blocks the utilitarian strategy of reducing everything to one metric and maximizing.

**Practical reasonableness.** Given the basic goods, how should one act? Finnis identifies nine "requirements of practical reasonableness": have a coherent life plan; no arbitrary preference among values; no arbitrary preference among persons; detachment from any one project; commitment to one's commitments; efficiency within reason; respect for every basic value in every act (the famous "never directly attack a basic good" principle); respect for the common good; follow your conscience.

**Moral absolutes.** The seventh requirement — "never directly attack a basic good" — generates absolute prohibitions. You may not directly kill an innocent person even to save more people. You may not lie even when lying would prevent great harm. You may not destroy a basic good in order to promote more of it or more of another good. This is close to Kantian deontology but grounded in the structure of the goods themselves rather than in the form of rational will.

**Natural rights.** Rights are the reverse side of duties generated by practical reasonableness: because you must not directly attack the basic good of life in another, each person has a right not to be so attacked. Natural law thereby generates natural rights without needing a separate grounding theory.

This framework is more accessible to secular readers than the Thomistic original, but its core claims are the same: moral truths are objective features of reality discovered by practical reason reflecting on what is good for human beings.

## What the Framework Owes to Natural Law

Natural law is genuinely insightful in several ways the framework must acknowledge.

**1. The teleological intuition is partly right.** The framework's valence realism (Chapter 3) says conscious experience has intrinsic valence — suffering is genuinely bad and flourishing is genuinely good. This is a more modest claim than Aquinas's full teleology (we do not claim that hearts exist for the sake of pumping blood in a morally relevant sense), but it captures the core of the natural law intuition: **there are facts about what is good for conscious beings, and those facts are not invented by preference**.

**2. Practical reason has first principles.** The framework agrees that moral reasoning has starting points that cannot be further derived. Our starting points are the valence axiom (suffering matters) and the epistemic duty (truth-seeking is obligatory). These play a role very similar to Aquinas's first precept of practical reason. The framework differs in what the starting points are, but not in the structural claim that some starting points are needed.

**3. Moral truths are objective in a relevant sense.** The framework is not relativist. It holds that some moral systems work better than others, that some arrangements produce more suffering and less flourishing, that these are matters of fact rather than taste. This is not the full Thomistic metaphysical objectivism, but it shares the insistence that morality is not merely subjective.

**4. Flourishing is the right category.** The framework's complex value vector (hedonic tone, autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, meaning — Chapter 3) is a cousin of the natural law theorist's basic goods. We are not eudaimonists in the Aristotelian sense, but we agree that human well-being is multidimensional and not reducible to hedonic tone alone. This is a major point of contact with natural law.

**5. Law and morality are not separate domains.** The framework agrees with Aquinas that the legitimacy of positive law depends on its conformity with moral standards. Our political philosophy (Chapters 17–24) treats institutions as evaluable by their contribution to flourishing and their fit with coordination requirements, not as self-justifying by virtue of being enacted. An unjust law is, in our framework as in Aquinas's, a failure of law — though we would describe the failure in different terms.

**6. There are absolute prohibitions.** The framework's Mode A / Mode B architecture (Chapters 9–10) preserves many of the strong rules that Aquinas and Finnis defend absolutely. We do not treat any rule as metaphysically unbreakable, but we set the override threshold so high that in practice the strongest rules function much as natural law absolutes do. The difference is structural (we have an articulated override mechanism) but the extensional overlap is significant.

This is a substantial debt. Natural law is not a rival we dismiss; it is a rival we have learned from. The specific places where we diverge are therefore worth stating carefully.

## Where the Framework Disagrees

### Disagreement 1: The Teleological Premise Is Too Strong

Natural law, in its Thomistic form, depends on an Aristotelian teleological biology: things have intrinsic ends (*telē*) built into what they are, and morality consists in acting in accordance with those ends. The heart exists *for* pumping blood; the eye exists *for* seeing; the human being exists *for* rational participation in the divine order.

Modern biology tells a different story. Organs and traits exist because they were selected for by differential reproduction over evolutionary time. There is no telos in the strong Aristotelian sense -- no purpose built into the structure of things, only the residue of past selection pressures (see Chapter 4). The heart's "function" is a historical fact about what it was selected for, not a metaphysical fact about what it is ordered to.

This matters because natural law's ability to generate specific moral content depends on reading ends off nature. If the ends are not there in the strong sense, the content cannot be read off. Finnis's move to "basic goods" is an attempt to rescue the framework by relocating the ends — not in biology but in what practical reason recognizes as intelligibly valuable. But this is a significant retreat: it gives up the claim that ends are written into the nature of things and admits that the goods are identified by reflection on action. Once you make that concession, the framework looks much less like "reading nature" and much more like the kind of reflective equilibrium that any contemporary moral theorist engages in.

The framework's response: **accept the valence realism but reject the strong teleology.** Suffering and flourishing are genuine features of conscious experience; they are not projected onto neutral facts. But the move from "suffering is bad" to "here are the specific things you must do" is not a reading of natural ends. It is a reflective construction informed by valence, coordination, and epistemic constraints. The construction is not arbitrary — it is disciplined by the objective features of reality — but it is construction, not decipherment.

### Disagreement 2: The "Naturalistic Fallacy" Lands Harder Than Natural Law Admits

Hume's worry that you cannot derive ought from is, and G. E. Moore's related "naturalistic fallacy," are aimed at positions like natural law. The natural law theorist says: human beings have a natural inclination to self-preservation, *therefore* they ought to preserve themselves. But the "therefore" carries a normative weight that the premise does not obviously supply. Why should natural inclinations be morally authoritative? What argument bridges the descriptive fact that humans tend to self-preserve and the normative conclusion that they should?

Aquinas's answer is that the inclinations are themselves instances of rational participation in eternal law — that is, the descriptive facts are already morally loaded because they are part of God's ordering of creation. This answer is available if you accept the theistic metaphysics, but it reintroduces the dependence on God that Finnis is trying to avoid. Finnis's answer is that the basic goods are recognized as goods by practical reason itself, without needing a further bridge — the recognition is immediate. But critics have argued persuasively that this just relocates the problem: how do we know that practical reason's recognitions track something real, rather than reporting the preferences of creatures like us?

The framework's response: **the valence axiom handles the bridge more honestly than natural law does**. We admit up front that a normative claim is being asserted (suffering matters) and defend it on the grounds that denying it is performatively incoherent and that every moral system requires at least one such axiom. Natural law smuggles the normative content in by calling inclinations "natural" and then treating "natural" as already normative. Our version is more transparent about where the normativity enters.

### Disagreement 3: The Content-Generation Problem

A moral framework has to actually tell you what to do in specific cases. Natural law has a long track record of generating specific content, and much of this content is disputable in ways that suggest the method is less reliable than its defenders claim.

Consider the natural law tradition's positions on sexuality. From the premise that the sexual faculty has a natural purpose (procreation and unitive love within marriage), the tradition derives absolute prohibitions on contraception, masturbation, same-sex sexual activity, and extramarital sex -- taught as exceptionless moral norms grounded in the structure of the natural faculty.

But look at the actual inferential chain. Sexual activity serves multiple functions across cultures: reproduction, pleasure, pair-bonding, social cohesion, play, expression, consolation, signaling. Natural law's move is to privilege one of these functions (reproduction + marital union) as *the* natural purpose and treat deviations as morally disordered. This privileging is a choice, not an observation. The faculty is multifunctional in fact. The decision to make one function normatively privileged is an extra step that natural law's method does not justify from inside itself.

Similar issues arise with the tradition's positions on lying (all lies forbidden, even to save innocent life), on self-defense (complex doctrine of double effect to license killing in self-defense), on capital punishment (varies by period), on usury (once absolutely forbidden, now permitted under new accounts). The content generated by the method has shifted over time in ways that suggest what is actually happening is moral reflection informed by a changing set of inputs — not the direct reading of an unchanging nature.

The framework's response: **acknowledge that moral content is generated through reflective reasoning informed by empirical and theoretical considerations, rather than by reading nature.** Our method is fallibilist — it expects to update and it has an explicit mechanism (the Position Registry, Chapter 1) for tracking updates. Natural law's self-presentation as reading unchanging truths makes it harder for the tradition to acknowledge and learn from its own shifts.

### Disagreement 4: The Absolutism Problem

New natural law theory generates absolute prohibitions via its "never directly attack a basic good" principle. You may never intend the death of an innocent person, even to save many. This produces the famous cases where natural law theorists hold that you must not, for example, push a fat man onto trolley tracks to save five others, even if the math is clear.

The framework takes these intuitions seriously — they motivate the dual-process architecture in Chapters 9–10. But we do not handle them by making the rules metaphysically absolute. We handle them by setting a very high override threshold, justified by the game-theoretic reasons rules need to be pre-committed (Chapters 7–9). In practice, the framework reaches the same conclusion as the natural law theorist in the trolley case (do not push) but through a different route.

The advantage of our route is that it can handle the cases where natural law absolutism produces bizarre conclusions. Natural law holds that you must not lie to a murderer asking where your children are hiding, because lying directly attacks the basic good of knowledge. Most readers find this monstrous, and most contemporary natural law theorists find ways to mitigate it. But the mitigations always look like departures from the strict view. Our framework can simply say: in this case the override conditions are met, honesty is a strong default but not metaphysically absolute, and protecting the children is the right choice.

The framework's response: **the game-theoretic grounding of rules preserves the strength of natural law's absolutes in normal cases while allowing principled deviation in extreme cases**, without requiring the metaphysics that makes absolute prohibitions difficult to revise even when clearly wrong.

### Disagreement 5: Moral Progress and the Fixed-Nature Problem

If natural law is fixed because human nature is fixed, then genuine moral progress is hard to explain. The abolition of slavery, the expansion of political rights to women, the recognition of children as distinct persons with interests of their own, the moral seriousness now given to animal suffering — these look like discoveries, as if we came to see something we previously could not see. But if the natural law has always been the same and was always available to rational reflection, why did rational reflection repeatedly fail to notice it?

Natural law theorists have responses. One is that the core principles were always there but the applications were obscured by sin, prejudice, or limited empirical knowledge — slavery was always wrong, but the error was protected by ideological fog and the difficulty of the application. Another is that there is less progress than appears; some past views were defensible in their context and the progress narrative oversimplifies. Both responses have merit.

But neither fully accommodates the framework's Chapter 5 argument about the historical track record of moral blindness. That track record is long, systematic, and sobering. The most plausible explanation is not that each past generation was simply failing to apply unchanging principles, but that the moral circle expands as our understanding of who counts and why improves, and that this expansion is a real learning process, not merely the unveiling of pre-known truths. Our framework is built to expect such expansion and has architecture for handling it. Natural law, in its traditional form, is less well-equipped for this because its self-understanding treats the content as already given.

The framework's response: **moral epistemology is progressive and fallible, and our architecture should reflect this.** Natural law's commitment to fixed content creates friction with moral progress in a way that our framework does not.

### Disagreement 6: The Common Good Problem

Both natural law and our framework invoke "the common good." For Aquinas, the common good is the end of political community and the standard by which laws are evaluated. For us, the common good emerges from the game-theoretic analysis of coordination: arrangements that produce more flourishing and less suffering are better than arrangements that produce less.

The natural law conception, though, tends to treat the common good as a substantive good that authoritative reasoners can identify and direct the community toward. This creates a risk: those who claim knowledge of the common good gain a license to impose it on those who do not. Historical natural law practice has included, at various points, persecution of heretics, suppression of dissent, and imposition of specific religious practices — all justified on the grounds that the true common good required it.

Our framework, with its epistemic humility (Chapter 2) and error-correction primacy (Chapter 18), is more resistant to this pathology. The common good is something we are still discovering, and any claim to know it definitively should be viewed with suspicion, especially when the claim is used to override the voices of those affected. Natural law's confidence that reason can read the common good from nature has sometimes been a cover for confidence that the reader's own preferences are the common good. Our framework tries to build structural checks against this.

## The Deepest Issue: Sources of Normativity

The disagreements above cluster around a single deeper question: **where does normative authority come from?**

Natural law answers: from the intelligible order of reality, which (on Thomistic versions) is the expression of divine reason, and which (on Finnis's version) is recognized by practical reason reflecting on what is intelligibly valuable.

Our framework answers: from the objective reality of conscious experience (valence is real), plus the minimal axiom that suffering matters, plus the constraints imposed by the mathematics of coordination and the limits of our epistemic access to reality.

These are different answers, but not as different as they might appear. Both say that morality is not arbitrary. Both say that moral truths answer to something beyond individual preference. Both say that reason has a central role in moral knowledge. The disagreement is about the structure of the grounding: whether moral truths are features of an ordered cosmos with intrinsic purposes (natural law), or whether they emerge from the interaction of specific natural facts (conscious experience, game theory, cognitive limits) with minimal axioms we explicitly own (our framework).

The framework's bet is that the second grounding is more honest about what we actually know, more robust against the metaphysical objections raised in the last four centuries, and more able to accommodate moral progress. Natural law's bet is that the second grounding is too thin to sustain the moral seriousness the tradition wants to preserve — that without a telic, ordered cosmos, moral language loses its grip.

We are taking the bet.

## What We Take From Natural Law

Despite the disagreements, the framework takes a great deal from natural law:

1. **The insistence that moral truths are objective** — not merely subjective or conventional.
2. **The idea that flourishing is multidimensional** — not reducible to hedonic tone.
3. **The integration of moral and political theory** — moral standards apply to laws and institutions, not just to individual choices.
4. **The seriousness given to practical reason** — reasoning about action is genuine reasoning, not the rationalization of prior preference.
5. **The suspicion of arbitrary will** — neither divine nor human will can make something right if it is not.
6. **The notion of common good** — there is a good of the community that cannot be reduced to aggregated individual preferences.
7. **The acknowledgment of strong moral defaults** — some things must almost never be done, even for good reasons.

These are not trivial takeaways. Much of what is valuable in contemporary human rights discourse, in constitutional jurisprudence, and in moral philosophy's treatment of dignity and inviolability comes from the natural law tradition. The framework's political and legal philosophy owes more to this tradition than its explicit citations suggest.

What we do not take is the metaphysical framework that generates the specific content. The teleological biology, the claim that practical reason directly perceives fixed goods, the absolutism without override mechanism, and the self-presentation as discovering rather than constructing — these are the parts we modify or replace.

## Summary

1. **Natural law is the most sophisticated non-utilitarian, non-Kantian rival** the framework faces. It claims that moral truths are built into reality and accessible to practical reason reflecting on human nature.

2. **The Thomistic version** grounds natural law in eternal (divine) law and uses an Aristotelian teleological biology; the first precept is "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided," with specific content derived from natural inclinations.

3. **The new natural law version** (Finnis) defends a list of basic goods and requirements of practical reasonableness without requiring explicit theism, generating absolute prohibitions via the "never directly attack a basic good" principle.

4. **The framework owes natural law substantial debts**: moral objectivity, flourishing as multidimensional, integration of moral and political theory, suspicion of voluntarism, acknowledgment of strong defaults.

5. **The framework disagrees** with natural law on: the strong teleological premise, the handling of the is-ought gap, the reliability of content generation from nature, the absolutism without override mechanism, the fixed-nature account of moral progress, and the common-good authority problem.

6. **The deepest disagreement** is about the source of normativity — natural law locates it in the intrinsic order of reality, the framework locates it in the reality of conscious experience plus minimal axioms we explicitly own.

7. **The framework's bet** is that the second grounding is more honest, more robust to modern objections, and more capable of accommodating moral progress. Natural law's bet is that the second grounding is too thin. We are taking our bet, but with genuine respect for the tradition we are departing from.

Natural law and our framework are not enemies. They are disciplined alternatives in a conversation that has been going on for eight hundred years and will continue. If the framework is right that the grounding can be thinner than Aquinas thought, the tradition can incorporate much of what we say while preserving its core commitments. If natural law is right that our grounding is too thin, we will need to strengthen it or accept that we cannot sustain the moral seriousness we aim at.
