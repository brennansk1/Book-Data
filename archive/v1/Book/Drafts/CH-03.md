# Chapter 3: The Axiom of Valence — Grounding Value in Consciousness

---

> *"The evolution of the capacity to simulate seems to have culminated in subjective consciousness. Why this should have happened is, to me, the most profound mystery facing modern biology."*
> — Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene*

## The Problem We Must Solve

In Chapter 1, we made a claim and a confession.

The claim: the suffering of a conscious being matters. The confession: this is an axiom — a starting point chosen rather than proved. We argued that denying it is performatively incoherent, but we left the heavy lifting for later.

This is later.

This chapter must do what no other chapter in the book can do for it: explain *why* valence — the spectrum from suffering to joy — provides a legitimate foundation for a moral system. Not assert it. Not appeal to intuition. Actually *argue* for it in a way that survives the most sophisticated objections.

The stakes are total. If this chapter fails, the rest of the book is a castle built on sand. Every political recommendation, every economic analysis, every debate with a rival system bottoms out here. If this claim cannot bear the weight, nothing above it stands.

Three specific challenges must be met:

1. **The Is-Ought Gap** (Hume's Guillotine): You cannot derive a moral obligation from a factual observation. How does the *fact* that suffering exists generate the *obligation* to reduce it?

2. **The Error Theory Challenge** (Mackie): Perhaps suffering is real but moral facts are not. People *act* as if suffering matters, but there are no objective moral properties in the world — just subjective reactions we mistakenly project onto reality.

3. **The Scope Problem**: If valence grounds morality, whose valence counts? Animals? Future AIs? Ecosystems? Where is the line, and what justifies it?

We take them in order.

---

## What Valence Is

Before arguing that valence matters, we need to be precise about what it is.

Valence is not an emotion. It is not happiness. It is not preference satisfaction. It is the qualitative character of conscious experience along the suffering-to-flourishing spectrum — the raw fact that some states of consciousness are experienced as bad and others as good, prior to any interpretation or judgment.

Consider pain. When your hand touches a hot stove, a cascade of physical events occurs: nociceptors fire, signals travel along C-fibers and A-delta fibers to the spinal cord, the thalamus routes the information, the somatosensory cortex processes it. But something else happens too — something that constitutes what Dawkins called "the most profound mystery facing modern biology." There is *something it is like* to be in pain. The neural activity doesn't just process information; it *hurts*.

This "something it is like" — what philosophers call *phenomenal consciousness* or *qualia* — is the foundation we are building on. Not the neural correlates visible on brain scans, but the experience itself, known only from the inside.

Daniel Kahneman drew a crucial distinction between two aspects of wellbeing. The "experiencing self" lives in the continuous present — the you who actually feels pleasure and pain, moment to moment. The "remembering self" constructs narratives about the past and often gets the evaluation wrong. A colonoscopy patient whose procedure ended with a mild final phase rated the *overall* experience as less painful than one whose procedure was shorter but ended abruptly — even though the first patient experienced more total pain. The remembering self was tricked by the "peak-end rule." The experiencing self was not.

This distinction matters because our axiom is grounded in the experiencing self, not the remembering self. What matters morally is not how people *evaluate* their lives in retrospect — evaluations are subject to framing effects, cognitive biases, and narrative distortions. What matters is the moment-to-moment stream of actual experience.

This is a substantive commitment, and it puts us at odds with certain versions of utilitarianism that define welfare in terms of preference satisfaction or life-satisfaction reports. We are claiming something stronger: that there is a fact of the matter about how a conscious being is doing at any given moment, and that this fact is not determined by what the being *says* or *thinks* about how it is doing, but by the actual qualitative character of its experience.

## The Argument from Undeniability

We begin with the weakest-seeming but actually strongest argument for valence as a moral foundation: you cannot sincerely deny it.

Consider the following thought experiment. A philosopher publishes a paper arguing that suffering has no moral significance — that it is merely a physical state with no normative implications, like the temperature of a rock. The paper is clever, well-argued, and logically valid.

Now imagine that same philosopher strapped to a chair while someone slowly increases the electric current running through their body. At some point — early, we suspect — they will scream. They will beg for it to stop. They will offer anything to make it end.

In that moment, what has happened to their thesis?

It has not been *refuted* in the strict logical sense. But it cannot be *acted on*. The screaming, the begging, the desperate search for escape — the philosopher's entire behavioral repertoire constitutes a living demonstration that they treat suffering as mattering.

This is performative incoherence. The denial of valence's moral significance cannot be sincerely *lived*. It can only be *asserted* from the safety of an armchair, where the stakes are zero.

> *"Be warned that if you wish, as I do, to build a society in which individuals cooperate generously and unselfishly towards a common good, you can expect little help from biological nature. Let us try to teach generosity and altruism, because we are born selfish. Let us understand what our own selfish genes are up to, because we may then at least have the chance to upset their designs, something that no other species has ever aspired to."*
> — Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene*

Dawkins is right that evolution gives us no help with morality — but notice what he takes for granted. He assumes, without arguing for it, that building "a society in which individuals cooperate generously" is a goal worth pursuing. Why? Because the alternative — a society of ruthless selfishness — would be, as he puts it elsewhere, "a very nasty society in which to live." And "nasty" is a valence judgment. Even the arch-gene-selectionist, when he steps back from description to prescription, cannot avoid grounding his moral vision in the fact that some states of being are experienced as worse than others.

**The asymmetry of denial.** Notice a crucial asymmetry. The claim "suffering matters" can be lived. People routinely organize their entire lives around reducing suffering — their own and others'. The claim "suffering does not matter" cannot be lived by any conscious being. This asymmetry is not proof, but it is powerful evidence. When one side of a philosophical debate can be lived and the other cannot, we have strong reason to suspect that the livable side is tracking something real.

## Crossing the Is-Ought Gap

Now for Hume's Guillotine, the objection that most threatens us.

David Hume observed in 1739 that many moral arguments commit a subtle fallacy: they begin with statements about what *is* the case and suddenly switch to statements about what *ought* to be the case, with no justification for the leap. You cannot derive an obligation from a description. From "suffering exists" alone, you cannot conclude "suffering ought to be reduced."

Hume was right. And we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Our system does not derive "ought" from "is." It *starts* with an irreducible normative commitment — the axiom that conscious suffering matters — and then uses empirical facts to determine the best strategies for honoring that commitment. The "is" does not generate the "ought." The "ought" is the axiom. The "is" tells us how to act on it.

This is structurally identical to how every other rational enterprise works. Engineering does not derive "bridges ought to stand up" from physics. It *starts* with the commitment that bridges should stand up and uses physics to determine how. Medicine does not derive "patients ought to survive" from biology. It *starts* with the commitment to patient welfare and uses biological knowledge to advance it. The Hippocratic Oath is not a theorem of biochemistry — it is an axiom of the medical profession.

Our system works the same way:

- **Axiom (normative):** Conscious suffering matters; other things being equal, it should be reduced.
- **Empirical input:** Game theory, evolutionary psychology, neuroscience, and institutional analysis tell us what actually reduces suffering in practice.
- **Output:** Specific moral, political, and economic recommendations.

The is-ought gap is real, but it is not a problem for us, because we never claimed to cross it through pure logic. We claimed to *locate* it precisely — at the axiom — and then build rigorously on both sides. Every system needs at least one axiom. We are honest about ours.

**But why *this* axiom?** A fair objection: you can start with any axiom. Why not "the will of God matters" or "racial purity matters" or "the number of paperclips in the universe matters"?

Because not all axioms are equal. An axiom should be:

1. **Hard to deny.** Our axiom passes the undeniability test above. "The will of God matters" does not — a sincere atheist can deny it without performative incoherence. "Racial purity matters" does not — members of the "inferior" race can deny it while living consistently. Only valence has the property that *every* conscious being, when pressed, reveals through their behavior that they treat it as mattering.

2. **Minimal.** Our axiom assumes as little as possible. We don't start with "happiness should be maximized" (that would smuggle in utilitarianism) or "persons have inherent dignity" (that would smuggle in Kantianism). We start with the bare claim that the suffering-joy spectrum is morally significant. Everything else is argued for, not assumed.

3. **Productive.** Our axiom generates a rich moral system when combined with empirical knowledge. Not all axioms do. "Everything is permitted" is hard to deny from a nihilist's perspective, but it generates nothing — it is an axiom for paralysis.

## Answering the Error Theorist

J.L. Mackie, in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), mounted the most sophisticated attack on moral realism. His argument from "queerness" goes like this: if moral facts existed, they would be utterly unlike any other kind of fact in the universe. They would have to be simultaneously descriptive (telling us how the world is) and prescriptive (telling us what to do) — a combination found nowhere else in nature. Since such queer entities probably don't exist, moral realism is probably false.

Mackie's argument is serious and deserves a serious answer. Here is ours:

**Valence is not "queer" in Mackie's sense.** Mackie imagined moral facts as free-floating normative properties — as if "wrongness" were an invisible substance hovering over acts of cruelty. But valence is not that kind of thing.

Valence is a physical property of conscious systems. When a person experiences agony, their brain is in a state that is detectable, measurable, and as physical as the temperature of a star. Valence is not hovering over reality; it *is* reality — a specific, empirically accessible subset of it.

The normative dimension enters not because valence *intrinsically commands*, but because it *intrinsically motivates*. No conscious being experiencing severe suffering needs a philosophical argument to want it to stop. The motivation is built into the experience itself. Mackie assumed that moral facts would need some mysterious power to compel action. But valence compels action the same way hunger compels eating — through the ordinary causal mechanisms of conscious experience.

**The error theorist's dilemma.** Mackie claimed that when people make moral judgments, they are systematically in error — projecting subjective reactions onto the world as if they were objective facts. But consider what this means in practice. The error theorist must say: "When the torture victim screams 'This is bad!' they are making a factual error. Torture is not *really* bad — it merely causes a physical state that the victim *mistakenly* interprets as having normative significance."

This is not a refutation. It is a *reductio*. Any philosophical position that requires you to say "torture is not really bad" has, by any sane accounting, proven too much. The error theorist has not discovered a deep truth about the nature of morality — they have demonstrated the inadequacy of their theory by showing that it conflicts with the most basic deliverance of conscious experience.

We are not claiming this settles the debate in academic metaethics; error theory remains a live position defended by serious philosophers. What we are claiming is that for *practical* purposes — for building a moral system that real people can use — error theory is a non-starter. A moral system cannot be built on the premise that morality is an error.

**The illusionist challenge.** A deeper version of the error theory comes from illusionism about consciousness (Dennett, Frankish). On this view, phenomenal consciousness — the "what it is like" that grounds our valence realism — is itself an illusion. If illusionism is correct, the physical property we are calling "valence" does not exist in the way we claim.

The framework's response is twofold. First, illusionism faces its own reductio: "Your experience of reading this sentence is an illusion" cannot be asserted without presupposing the very phenomenon it denies. Dennett himself does not claim that pain is unreal; he claims it is differently constituted than folk psychology supposes. Second, the framework can operate on functional valence — the causally efficacious states that make beings avoid damage and seek flourishing — even if the metaphysics of phenomenal consciousness turns out differently than we assume. The axiom requires that suffering *matters*, not that it has a specific metaphysical character. If illusionism is true, the functional states still exist, still cause harm, and still demand moral attention.

This is an honest concession: the framework's grounding is robust to the hard problem but not immune to it. If someone were to show that nothing even functionally analogous to suffering exists, the foundation would crack. We do not believe this is possible, but we name the dependency explicitly. Chapter 5 develops this further.

## Value Beyond Suffering: The Complex Vector

A common misunderstanding must now be addressed. If valence is the foundation, does that mean we are just utilitarians in disguise — that everything reduces to maximizing pleasure and minimizing pain?

No. And the distinction is critical.

Classical utilitarianism treats value as a **scalar** — a single number on a pleasure-pain axis that can be summed across people and maximized. This is what Bentham meant by "the greatest happiness of the greatest number." It is what leads to the repugnant conclusion (a world of ten billion people barely worth living is "better" than a world of one billion people flourishing) and the utility monster (a being that experiences superhuman pleasure can consume everyone else's resources).

We treat value as a **complex vector** — a multi-dimensional space of qualitatively distinct goods, of which the suffering-joy axis is the foundation but not the totality.

What are the other dimensions? Drawing on the best available psychology, neuroscience, and philosophical reflection, we propose (provisionally) these irreducible components of human flourishing:

**1. Hedonic tone** — the suffering-to-joy axis. The foundation. A life of unrelieved agony is bad regardless of what else it contains.

**2. Autonomy** — the experience of being the author of one's own actions. A life of pleasure that was entirely manipulated or controlled would be missing something essential, even if the moment-to-moment hedonic tone was positive. This is why Nozick's Experience Machine is unsatisfying — most people would not choose a lifetime of simulated bliss over a real life with real challenges.

**3. Competence** — the experience of mastery and growth. Humans are not designed for static contentment. We need challenge, skill development, and the satisfaction of overcoming obstacles. This is what Csikszentmihalyi documented as "flow" — the state where challenge and skill are perfectly matched.

**4. Connection** — the experience of genuine relationship with other conscious beings. Solitary pleasure is not the same as shared joy. The deepest forms of human wellbeing — love, friendship, community — are inherently relational.

**5. Understanding** — the experience of comprehending reality. Curiosity is not a means to pleasure; it is a distinct form of flourishing. The scientist who understands a phenomenon is not merely "pleased" — they are engaging in a qualitatively different kind of good.

**6. Meaning** — the experience of one's actions and existence as significant. Viktor Frankl, writing from inside Auschwitz, observed that prisoners who maintained a sense of purpose survived at higher rates than those who lost it, even when their material conditions were identical. Meaning is not reducible to hedonic tone — it is a distinct dimension of the value vector.

This list is provisional. We hold it with moderate confidence and expect it to be refined as the system develops. What we hold with high confidence is the *structure*: value is a vector, not a scalar. This single move dissolves most of the classic objections to welfare-based ethics:

- The Experience Machine fails against a vector because it only provides hedonic tone while destroying autonomy, genuine connection, and real understanding.
- The Repugnant Conclusion fails against a vector because lives "barely worth living" score near zero on most dimensions, not just hedonic tone.
- The Utility Monster fails against a vector because you cannot aggregate qualitatively distinct goods into a single sum to be maximized.

## The Evolutionary Grounding

> *"One way for genes to solve the problem of making predictions in rather unpredictable environments is to build in a capacity for learning. Here the program may take the form of the following instructions to the survival machine: 'Here is a list of things defined as rewarding: sweet taste in the mouth, orgasm, mild temperature, smiling child. And here is a list of nasty things: various sorts of pain, nausea, empty stomach, screaming child.'"*
> — Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene*

Dawkins here describes something essential: valence is the mechanism by which genes steer organisms. Pain and pleasure are not accidents of biology — they are the control system. The gene cannot directly tell the organism what to do in every situation, so it installs a reward-punishment architecture and lets the organism learn.

But here is the insight that makes our moral system possible: **the control system has become more important than the controller.**

The gene "wants" (metaphorically) nothing more than its own replication. It uses pleasure and pain as *instruments* toward that end. But from the organism's perspective — from the perspective of the conscious being who actually *experiences* the pleasure and pain — the valence is not a means to anything. It is the most immediate reality there is. The gene uses sugar-pleasure to make you eat calories. But *you* don't experience "a gene-replication-promoting state." You experience *sweetness*. The experience is real even though the gene's "purpose" in creating it was purely instrumental.

> *"Whatever the philosophical problems raised by consciousness, for the purpose of this story it can be thought of as the culmination of an evolutionary trend towards the emancipation of survival machines as executive decision-takers from their ultimate masters, the genes. Not only are brains in charge of the day-to-day running of survival-machine affairs, they have also acquired the ability to predict the future and act accordingly. They even have the power to rebel against the dictates of the genes, for instance in refusing to have as many children as they are able to."*
> — Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene*

This is the emancipation thesis, and it is crucial for our project. Once consciousness evolves — once there is *something it is like* to be an organism — the organism's welfare becomes a fact about the world that is independent of gene-level fitness. The gene created the pain system to promote survival. But the pain is *real to the organism* regardless of whether it promotes survival. A person dying of cancer is in agony; the agony has no survival function; but it is absolutely real. Their suffering does not stop being a fact just because evolution has no further use for them.

This gives us a clean answer to the evolutionary debunker — the philosopher who argues that because our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection, they are unreliable guides to moral truth. We agree that moral intuitions are unreliable. That is why we don't ground our system in intuitions. We ground it in *valence itself* — the raw physical reality of conscious experience — and use reason, game theory, and empirical evidence to determine what to do about it.

Jonathan Haidt's research demonstrates exactly how unreliable moral intuitions are:

> *"People made moral judgments quickly and emotionally. Moral reasoning was mostly just a post hoc search for reasons to justify the judgments people had already made."*
> — Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*

> *"The mind is divided, like a rider on an elephant, and the rider's job is to serve the elephant. The rider is our conscious reasoning — the stream of words and images of which we are fully aware. The elephant is the other 99 percent of mental processes — the ones that occur outside of awareness but that actually govern most of our behavior."*
> — Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*

The elephant — our automatic moral intuitions — was shaped by evolution for genetic fitness, not for tracking moral truth. The rider — our conscious reasoning — is mostly a press secretary confabulating justifications after the fact. This is exactly why we cannot build a moral system on intuitions alone. Intuitions are data, but they are not *foundations*.

Valence is the foundation. The elephant's reactions are variable, culturally shaped, and often biased. But the fact that *there is an elephant at all* — that conscious beings have experiences ranging from agony to ecstasy — is not variable. It is the common substrate beneath every moral intuition, every cultural variation, every philosophical tradition. Every moral system in human history has been built in response to the same underlying reality: conscious beings can suffer, and that suffering presses upon us with a force that demands response.

## The Scope Question: Whose Valence Counts?

If valence is the moral foundation, then any system that has valence is a moral patient — an entity whose welfare must be considered. This follows directly and cannot be avoided. But it raises a hard question: *which systems have valence?*

We need to be honest: we do not have a complete answer. The "hard problem" of consciousness — explaining why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience — remains unsolved. We cannot yet say with certainty which systems are conscious and which are merely processing information without any accompanying experience.

But we can say more than "we don't know." We can establish principles for acting under uncertainty.

**Principle 1: Biological consciousness is not in serious doubt.** All mammals, and probably all vertebrates, share the neural architecture associated with conscious experience in humans — the thalamo-cortical system, nociceptors, endogenous opioids, behavioral pain responses. The scientific consensus, formalized in the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012), is that "non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, possess the neurological substrates that generate consciousness." Denying animal consciousness at this point requires motivated reasoning.

**Principle 2: Under uncertainty, err on the side of moral inclusion.** If we are uncertain whether a system is conscious, the cost of wrongly *excluding* it (ignoring the suffering of a conscious being) is far greater than the cost of wrongly *including* it (extending moral consideration to a non-conscious system). This is a straightforward application of expected-value reasoning under asymmetric costs.

**Principle 3: The boundary will move, and the system must accommodate this.** As neuroscience advances, our understanding of which systems are conscious will change. As AI develops, entirely new candidates for moral patienthood may emerge. A rigid system that draws the line once and for all will be wrong eventually. We need a framework that can expand its moral circle as evidence warrants — which is exactly what the Position Registry and swap mechanism are designed to enable.

**Principle 4: Degree of moral consideration tracks confidence in consciousness and complexity of experience.** Not all conscious beings are morally equal in every respect. A being with a richer conscious life — capable of suffering in more ways, over longer time horizons, with greater self-awareness — warrants greater moral weight than a being with simpler experience. This is not speciesism; it is a direct application of the valence foundation. A being that can experience existential dread, the loss of loved ones, and the destruction of life projects is morally different from a being that can only experience simple pain and simple pleasure — not because of what species it belongs to, but because of the *scope* of its valence.

This is a provisional position (POS-F03 in the Registry, rated HIGH vulnerability), and we know it faces serious objections. The most dangerous: without a test for consciousness, how do we apply these principles in practice? The honest answer is: imperfectly, with humility, and with constant updating as evidence accumulates. This is a chapter where the system acknowledges genuine uncertainty rather than pretending to have answers it doesn't have.

## What We Have Built

Let us take stock.

We began with a mystery — why should anything matter? — and a challenge: ground a moral system in something real without pretending to derive "ought" from "is."

Here is what we have argued:

1. **Valence is real.** Suffering and joy are objective physical states of conscious systems, not mere projections or cultural constructs. They are as real as any other physical phenomenon.

2. **Valence is undeniable.** No conscious being can sincerely live as though their own suffering doesn't matter. The denial of valence's significance is performatively incoherent — it can be asserted but not lived.

3. **The is-ought gap is respected, not violated.** Our system starts with an explicit normative axiom (suffering matters) and uses empirical facts to determine how to honor it. The axiom is the bridge across the gap. We do not pretend the bridge isn't there.

4. **The axiom is the most defensible available.** It is harder to deny than any alternative foundation, more minimal than any competing axiom, and more productive than nihilism.

5. **Value is a vector, not a scalar.** Hedonic tone is the foundation, but autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, and meaning are irreducible dimensions of flourishing. This dissolves the classic objections to welfare-based ethics.

6. **Evolution explains valence without debunking it.** Genes created the pleasure-pain system as a control mechanism, but the conscious experiences it produces are real to the organisms that have them, independent of their evolutionary function.

7. **Moral patienthood extends to all conscious beings**, with degree of consideration tracking confidence in consciousness and complexity of experience.

This is the foundation. Everything that follows in this book — every rule, every political recommendation, every economic analysis, every debate with a rival system — rests on this chapter. The invitation stands: find the crack. Show us a better foundation. If you can, you have improved the system, and we will update accordingly.

> *"We are built as gene machines and cultured as meme machines, but we have the power to turn against our creators. We, alone on earth, can rebel against the tyranny of the selfish replicators."*
> — Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene*

The rebellion begins with this: the genes built us to feel. We choose to take that feeling seriously.
