# Chapter 6: Moloch — The God of Coordination Failure

---

> *"UNDER WHAT CONDITIONS will cooperation emerge in a world of egoists without central authority?"*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

---

## The Question That Organizes This Book

Book I laid the groundwork. We established that conscious suffering is real and that it matters (Chapter 3). We committed ourselves to seeing the world clearly, even when clarity is uncomfortable (Chapter 2). We traced our moral instincts to their evolutionary origins and learned which ones to trust and which to suspect (Chapter 4). We admitted, honestly, the hard problems we cannot yet solve (Chapter 5).

But a foundation is not a house. Knowing that suffering matters does not explain why there is so much of it. And unless we understand *why* the world produces so much unnecessary suffering — suffering that no one wants, that no one benefits from, that virtually everyone would eliminate if they could — we cannot build a moral system capable of reducing it. The foundations tell us the goal. This book tells us the obstacle.

Ask someone why the world contains so much suffering, and you will usually get one of two answers.

The religious answer: *evil exists*. There are bad people, bad choices, bad spirits. Sometimes Satan, sometimes human depravity, sometimes the inherited consequences of some ancestral fall. Suffering is the signature of wickedness, and reducing it is a matter of reducing wickedness.

The cynical answer: *self-interest*. People are selfish. They pursue their own advantage and the collateral damage is the world we see. If only people were nicer, less greedy, more cooperative, most of the suffering would disappear.

Both answers are wrong — or rather, both are incomplete in ways that mislead us about the solution.

The religious answer fails because most suffering is not caused by bad people. It is caused by ordinary people — who love their children, feed their neighbors, and follow the rules of their societies — making choices that are individually reasonable but collectively catastrophic. The factory worker who pollutes the river is not a villain. The consumer who buys cheap meat is not a sadist. They are you and me, responding to incentives in a system whose outcomes none of us intend.

The cynical answer fails because even perfectly moral people, trying sincerely to do right, can produce terrible collective outcomes when the structure of the situation rewards defection and punishes cooperation. Make everyone a saint, and you will still have traffic jams, pollution, arms races, and housing crises — because these are produced not by sin but by the *logic* of multi-agent interaction in the absence of coordination mechanisms.

So what actually produces most suffering? The framework this book is building has a name for it, borrowed from Allen Ginsberg's 1955 poem "Howl":

**Moloch.**

Moloch was a Canaanite god to whom parents sacrificed their children. In Ginsberg's usage, the name became a metaphor for the logic of sacrifice itself — the system that demands the destruction of what we love in exchange for advantages we did not seek and cannot refuse. Moloch is not a person. Moloch is not a conspiracy. Moloch is the name we give to the phenomenon in which individually rational choices combine to produce collectively irrational outcomes.

Moloch is the god of bad Nash equilibria. And most of the world's unnecessary suffering is the product of his operation.

## The Core Insight: Bad Equilibria Can Be Stable

To understand Moloch, you must first understand a concept from game theory: the **Nash equilibrium**.

A Nash equilibrium is a situation in which no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing their strategy, *given what the other players are doing*. It is a stable resting point — a place the system falls into and stays.

Here is the crucial fact: **Nash equilibria are not always good.** A situation can be a stable equilibrium even if everyone in it is worse off than they would be in some other equilibrium. Once the system is stuck there, no individual has an incentive to deviate, even if deviating would benefit everyone.

The classic illustration is the **Prisoner's Dilemma**.

Two suspects are arrested and interrogated separately. Each has two choices: stay silent (cooperate with the other suspect) or confess (defect). The payoffs are arranged so that:

- If both stay silent, both serve a light sentence (say, 1 year).
- If both confess, both serve a heavy sentence (say, 5 years).
- If one confesses and the other stays silent, the confessor goes free and the silent one serves a very heavy sentence (say, 10 years).

Individual rationality says: confess. No matter what your partner does, you do better by confessing. If they stay silent, you go free instead of serving 1 year. If they confess, you serve 5 years instead of 10. Confessing dominates in every scenario.

Both players reason this way. Both confess. The outcome: 5 years each. This is the Nash equilibrium — stable, rational for each individual — and *strictly worse* for both than the outcome they could have achieved by cooperating.

> *"Individual rationality leads to a worse outcome for both than is possible. Hence the dilemma."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

**Rationality is not enough.** Two perfectly rational agents, each maximizing their own outcome, can be led by their rationality into an outcome both would reject if they could choose together. The *structure* of the situation traps them.

Axelrod puts the scope of the problem bluntly:

> *"Richardson's model of the arms race is based on an interaction which is essentially a Prisoner's Dilemma... Oligopolistic competition can also be modeled as a Prisoner's Dilemma... The ubiquitous problems of collective action to produce a collective good are analyzable as Prisoner's Dilemmas with many players.... In fact, many of the best-developed models of important political, social, and economic processes have the Prisoner's Dilemma as their foundation."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

> *"The iterated Prisoner's Dilemma has become the E. coli of social psychology."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

He is not exaggerating. Arms races between nations. Price wars between firms. Pollution by industries. Overfishing of oceans. Antibiotic overuse. Academic paper inflation. The college-degree arms race. Social media attention competition. All share the same underlying structure: individually rational behavior producing collectively catastrophic outcomes that persist because no individual can break out without bearing the full cost alone.

This is Moloch.

## The Tragedy of the Commons

The multi-player version of the same problem has its own name: the **Tragedy of the Commons**, introduced by Garrett Hardin in 1968.

Imagine a pasture shared by a group of herders. Each herder has their own cattle, and each can add more cattle to the commons at no direct cost. The grass is finite. If the total number of cattle stays below a certain threshold, the pasture regenerates and everyone does well indefinitely. If it exceeds the threshold, the pasture is destroyed and everyone loses.

From any individual herder's perspective, the calculation is simple: adding one more cow gives me all the benefit while distributing the cost across all herders. If there are ten herders, I get 100% of the benefit but bear only 10% of the cost. The math says add another cow.

Every herder does this calculation. Every herder adds another cow. The pasture is destroyed. No one did anything wrong by the standards of individual rationality. No one was a villain. And yet the collective outcome is disaster.

Hardin's original formulation was slightly misleading — we will see in Chapter 8 that commons can sometimes be governed successfully without either privatization or state coercion. But the core insight holds: whenever the benefits of a choice are concentrated on the chooser and the costs are distributed across many, there is systematic pressure toward the choice that destroys the collective good.

Modern examples are everywhere:

- **Climate change.** Each country's emissions benefit that country economically while distributing the atmospheric damage across all countries. Every country has an incentive to free-ride on others' emissions cuts.

- **Antibiotic resistance.** Each patient (and each doctor under pressure) benefits from using antibiotics for marginal infections, while the resistance burden falls on future patients everywhere. The individual calculus rewards overuse; the collective consequence is a post-antibiotic world.

- **Attention economy.** Each media company benefits from designing content to be maximally attention-grabbing, while the cognitive damage — shortened attention spans, addiction, degraded discourse — falls on everyone. No single company can unilaterally adopt less addictive designs without losing market share.

- **Housing.** Each homeowner benefits from policies that restrict new construction (preserving their property values), while the housing shortage falls on everyone trying to rent or buy. Individually rational NIMBYism produces collectively catastrophic housing markets.

In every case, the individual herders are not evil. They are trapped.

## The Stag Hunt: Why Trust Is So Fragile

Jean-Jacques Rousseau introduced a second game that captures a different face of coordination failure: the **Stag Hunt**.

Two hunters can coordinate to hunt a stag together, producing a large reward for both — but only if both commit to the stag hunt and neither defects. Alternatively, each hunter can go alone and hunt a rabbit. Rabbits are small, but a rabbit is guaranteed, while a stag requires teamwork.

The best outcome is mutual stag hunting. But if you commit to hunting the stag and your partner defects to rabbit-hunting, you get nothing — you starve. The *safe* strategy is always rabbit. The *optimal* strategy is stag, but only if you trust your partner to also choose stag.

The Stag Hunt has *two* stable equilibria: (stag, stag) and (rabbit, rabbit). Both are Nash equilibria. The first is better for everyone. The second is where groups get stuck when trust fails.

This is the structure of most genuine cooperation problems in human life. A startup only succeeds if the whole team commits; any defector dooms the venture. A relationship requires both partners to invest; unilateral investment ends in heartbreak. A democratic transition requires enough citizens to risk opposition simultaneously; if you go alone, you get shot. A scientific research program requires enough researchers to converge on a shared paradigm; if you're the only one using the new method, your papers get rejected.

In all these cases, the *technology* of cooperation is understood. The obstacle is not knowledge. The obstacle is *assurance* — the confidence that others will cooperate if you do.

This is the deepest reason Moloch is so hard to defeat. Even when everyone agrees that a better equilibrium exists — even when they can see it, describe it, and prefer it — they cannot get there without coordinated movement, and coordinated movement without trust is impossible.

## Why Moloch Is Worse Than Malice

Malice is a much easier enemy than Moloch.

If suffering were caused by bad people making bad choices, the solution would be straightforward: identify the bad people, stop them, punish them, educate them, or replace them with good people. This is how most moral systems throughout history have conceptualized the problem, and it produces a predictable set of responses — wars against enemies, inquisitions against heretics, revolutions against oppressors, moral crusades against the wicked.

But when suffering is caused by Moloch — by the structural logic of coordination failure — all of these responses fail, and many of them *make things worse*.

**Replacing the individuals does not help.** The factory polluting the river is not polluting because the owner is evil. Replace the owner with a saint, and the saint faces exactly the same competitive pressure: pollute or go out of business. The saint may feel worse about it. The river will still die.

**Moral exhortation does not help.** Telling people to "just cooperate" in a Prisoner's Dilemma does not change the game. Moral pressure may persuade a few to take the lower payoff, but it cannot stabilize cooperation at scale — and the few who try get eaten by the defectors who didn't listen to the sermon.

**Even revolution often fails.** If the underlying structure is a coordination failure, new leaders face the same incentives as the old ones. The French Revolution replaced the king with the Terror. The Russian Revolution replaced the Tsar with Stalin. The Iranian Revolution replaced the Shah with the Supreme Leader. The problem was never the person at the top; it was the game they were forced to play.

The horror of Moloch is that it operates *through* the decisions of ordinary people acting with ordinary intentions. There is no villain to defeat. There is no conspiracy to expose. There is only a structure producing exactly what its logic produces.

This is why the name *Moloch* is not hyperbole. The experience of living inside a coordination failure *feels* like being oppressed by an intelligent, malign entity — something extracting sacrifices, turning good intentions into bad outcomes, making effort feel futile — even though the entity is only the mathematics of the interaction.

## The Good News: Moloch Is Not Metaphysically Necessary

If Moloch were simply the way of things — if coordination failure were baked into the structure of reality — this chapter would be a counsel of despair. But Moloch is not metaphysically necessary. He is defeatable. And humans have defeated him before, many times, in many places, in ways we can study and replicate.

The central discovery, formalized by Robert Axelrod in a set of computer tournaments in the late 1970s, is this: **when interactions are repeated over long time horizons, the cooperative equilibrium becomes achievable without central authority**. The trick is the "shadow of the future" — the possibility that the person you're interacting with now will interact with you again tomorrow, and that today's defection will be punished by tomorrow's retaliation.

> *"What makes it possible for cooperation to emerge is the fact that the players might meet again. This possibility means that the choices made today not only determine the outcome of this move, but can also influence the later choices of the players. The future can therefore cast a shadow back upon the present and thereby affect the current strategic situation."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

We will develop the mechanics of this in the next chapter. For now, the point is merely that Axelrod's research provides a proof of concept: cooperation can emerge, stabilize, and defend itself against invasion by defectors — without any central enforcer, without any assumption of altruism, and without requiring that the agents even understand what they are doing.

Axelrod's empirical evidence for this claim comes from a surprising source: the trenches of the First World War. On the Western Front, where millions of soldiers faced each other in horrific conditions, an unauthorized phenomenon emerged that the military command could neither understand nor suppress:

> *"During World War I, the Western Front was the scene of horrible battles for a few yards of territory. But between these battles, and even during them at other places along the five-hundred-mile line in France and Belgium, the enemy soldiers often exercised considerable restraint."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

A staff officer observed: *"astonished to observe German soldiers walking about within rifle range behind their own line. Our men appeared to take no notice.... These people evidently did not know there was a war on. Both sides apparently believed in the policy of 'live and let live.'"*

Soldiers on opposing sides, who were theoretically committed to killing each other, developed tacit truces. They fired their weapons at predictable times, in predictable places, at predictable targets — sending signals that allowed the other side to avoid harm. An English officer described a German shell that arrived *"at seven... so regularly that you could set your watch by it.... It always had the same objective, its range was accurate, it never varied laterally or went beyond or fell short of the mark."* This was not incompetence. This was the most hostile environment imaginable producing the most surprising outcome imaginable: cooperation.

Even more striking was the Saxon incident, in which a German unit's artillery accidentally caused British casualties, and a German soldier stood on the parapet to apologize: *"We are very sorry about that; we hope no one was hurt. It is not our fault, it is that damned Prussian artillery."* Axelrod's comment captures the philosophical significance:

> *"This Saxon apology goes well beyond a merely instrumental effort to prevent retaliation. It reflects moral regret for having violated a situation of trust, and it shows concern that someone might have been hurt."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

What destroyed the system? Not the soldiers. The command structure above them. Headquarters eventually introduced a practice — the planned raid — that made cooperation impossible to sustain, because raids could be verified and their absence could be punished. The soldiers, left to themselves, had constructed an impressive peace in the middle of an unprecedented war. It took active institutional effort to prevent them from cooperating.

The lesson: **Moloch is defeatable, but not automatic**. Cooperation can emerge, but it requires specific conditions — repeated interactions, stable participants, the ability to recognize each other, and the capacity to retaliate against defection. When these conditions are present, cooperation can flourish even in the most hostile environments imaginable. When they are absent, defection dominates even among people who would sincerely prefer to cooperate.

Designing institutions that create the conditions for cooperation — that cast a long shadow of the future over present interactions, that enable recognition and retaliation, that make trust possible — is the engineering project we call "mechanism design." And mechanism design, we will argue, is just another name for moral and political philosophy, correctly understood.

## Morality as Anti-Moloch Technology

We are now in a position to state the central thesis of Book II, which will organize everything that follows:

**Morality is the technology we invent to defeat Moloch.**

This is not a metaphor. It is not a loose analogy. It is a precise claim about what moral systems are *for* and why they exist.

Consider the core moral rules found in almost every human society:

- **Don't lie.** Why? Because lying destroys trust, and trust is the foundation of iterated cooperation. A society where lying is common cannot solve coordination problems that require honest communication. The prohibition on lying is not arbitrary — it is the stabilizing mechanism for a cooperative equilibrium.

- **Don't steal.** Why? Because theft is defection in a property-rights coordination game. A society where theft is common cannot accumulate the capital, infrastructure, and long-term investments that require secure property. Property rights are a focal point (in Schelling's sense) around which peaceful coordination can crystallize.

- **Don't murder.** Why? Because retaliatory spirals of violence are among the most destructive bad equilibria humans can enter. Societies that tolerate murder for private grievances produce vendettas that can consume them for generations. The prohibition on murder is the institutional mechanism that breaks the cycle.

- **Keep your promises.** Why? Because the ability to make credible commitments is the foundation of any cooperation more complex than single interactions. Without promise-keeping, no Stag Hunts can be sustained, no Stag Hunts means no civilization.

- **Reciprocate kindness and punish cruelty.** Why? Because reciprocity is the strategy that stabilizes cooperation in iterated games (as Axelrod demonstrated). Societies that internalize reciprocity create cooperative cultures; societies that don't fall into defection spirals.

Each of these rules, viewed through the game-theoretic lens, is revealed as a **coordination device** — an institutional solution to a specific class of Moloch problem. They were discovered — not invented — over millennia of cultural evolution, as groups that adopted them outcompeted groups that didn't. The most successful moral traditions are those whose rules most effectively defeat the widest range of coordination failures.

This gives us a new way of evaluating moral systems. We no longer need to ask "does this feel right?" (the elephant's verdict) or "does this come from a legitimate authority?" (the theocrat's question). We can ask a more precise question:

**Does this rule, if widely adopted, reliably shift agents out of bad equilibria and into better ones?**

This is testable. It is not purely subjective. It is not culturally relative in the deep sense. It provides the empirical grounding that moral systems have always lacked. And it connects morality to the rest of human knowledge — to biology, economics, political science, computer science, and the mathematics of strategic interaction.

## The Scope of the Claim

This analysis does not claim that *all* moral problems are coordination failures. Some problems are individual (What makes a life well-lived?). Some are metaphysical (Whose consciousness counts?). Some are aesthetic (Is this action beautiful or ugly in a morally significant sense?). The valence axiom (Chapter 3) grounds the system, and coordination analysis does not replace it.

But the claim is that **the vast majority of unnecessary suffering in the world is produced by coordination failures**, and therefore the vast majority of moral and political work is the work of defeating Moloch. If you want to reduce suffering at scale — and the valence axiom gives you a reason to want exactly this — then learning the mechanics of coordination is not optional. It is the single most important applied skill in ethics.

This reframes many debates. When we ask "what political system is best?" we are really asking "which institutional design best solves the coordination problems a society faces?" When we ask "what economic policy is best?" we are really asking "which mechanism best aligns individual incentives with collective outcomes?" When we ask "what personal virtues should we cultivate?" we are really asking "which character traits are most cooperative-equilibrium-preserving?"

The rest of Book II develops the technical machinery — the physics of trust (Chapter 7) and the engineering of institutions (Chapter 8) — that will let us answer these questions rigorously. The rest of the book applies that machinery to personal ethics, politics, economics, and the hard frontier problems.

## Summary: What This Chapter Established

1. **Most suffering is not caused by bad people.** It is caused by ordinary people trapped in situations where individually rational choices produce collectively catastrophic outcomes.

2. **This phenomenon has a formal structure.** Game theory gives us the Prisoner's Dilemma, the Tragedy of the Commons, and the Stag Hunt as precise models of what is happening.

3. **Nash equilibria can be stable and bad.** A situation can be locked in even when everyone in it would prefer a different situation, because no individual can escape unilaterally.

4. **We call this phenomenon Moloch.** Not because there is a literal god, but because the phenomenology of living inside a coordination failure is accurately captured by the theological metaphor — something is extracting sacrifices from us, and our individual efforts feel futile against it.

5. **Moloch is not metaphysically necessary.** Axelrod's research, and the empirical evidence from places like the WWI trenches, shows that cooperation can emerge under specific conditions even without central authority.

6. **Morality is anti-Moloch technology.** The rules that every successful society has discovered — honesty, property, non-violence, promise-keeping, reciprocity — are best understood as coordination devices that shift agents out of bad equilibria and into better ones.

7. **This reframes ethics.** Moral and political philosophy become, in large part, the engineering discipline of mechanism design — figuring out how to build institutions that turn individual self-interest into collective welfare.

The next chapter develops the physics of trust: the specific game-theoretic mechanisms by which cooperation emerges, stabilizes, and defends itself. The chapter after that develops mechanism design: how we can deliberately engineer institutions to defeat Moloch in the wild.

The enemy has been named. The tools to defeat him are next.
