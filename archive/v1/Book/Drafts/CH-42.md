## The Rival Worth Taking Most Seriously

John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) is arguably the most important work of political philosophy in the twentieth century. It revived the social contract tradition, developed a powerful thought experiment (the original position behind the veil of ignorance), and produced specific principles of justice that have shaped political theory, legal scholarship, and public policy for half a century. If there is one contemporary rival the framework must engage with seriously, it is Rawls.

This chapter is different from others in Book VIII in that it is less "Against Rawls" and more "Engagement with Rawls." The framework owes a significant debt to Rawlsian thinking — the insurance argument for welfare (Chapter 29) is essentially a Rawlsian move, and several other parts of our political philosophy draw on Rawls's insights. At the same time, the framework is not simply Rawlsian; we diverge on specific points that matter.

The chapter's shape: **steel-man Rawls, identify where we agree, identify where we disagree, and explain why our disagreements are principled rather than arbitrary**.

---

## Steel-Manning Rawls

Rawls's core move: **principles of justice are the principles that rational, self-interested agents would agree to behind a "veil of ignorance" — that is, without knowing their own specific circumstances**. Rawls describes the setup:

> *"Among the essential features of this situation is that no one knows his place in society, his class position or social status, nor does any one know his fortune in the distribution of natural assets and abilities, his intelligence, strength, and the like... The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance. This ensures that no one is advantaged or disadvantaged in the choice of principles by the outcome of natural chance or the contingency of social circumstances."*
> — John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, §3

The thought experiment: imagine you are about to enter a society but do not know where you will be placed within it. You do not know your race, sex, class, talents, temperament, or religion. You know general facts about how societies work but nothing about your specific situation.

In this position, what principles of social organization would you agree to?

Rawls argues that you would agree to two principles, ordered lexically:

> *"First: each person is to have an equal right to the most extensive scheme of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar scheme of liberties for others. Second: social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so that they are both (a) reasonably expected to be to everyone's advantage, and (b) attached to positions and offices open to all."*
> — John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, §11

In Rawls's later, more refined formulation, "to everyone's advantage" becomes the difference principle: inequalities are justified only when they benefit the least advantaged members of society.

The first principle takes priority: you cannot sacrifice basic liberties for economic benefits. The second principle addresses economic and social arrangements: inequality is acceptable only when it benefits those worst off, and all positions must be genuinely open to all.

Why would rational agents choose these principles? Rawls's argument draws on several ideas:

**1. Risk-aversion behind the veil.** Since you don't know where you will land, you should plan for the worst case. A rational agent would choose principles that protect them if they end up in the worst possible position, rather than maximizing expected outcomes (which might leave them in a very bad position if the expected value came with high variance). This is sometimes called "maximin" reasoning — maximize the minimum.

**2. The equal worth of persons.** Behind the veil, you are not any particular person. You cannot privilege yourself because you don't know who you are. This forces you to treat all positions as equally possible for you, which generates impartial principles.

**3. The separateness of persons.** Rawls criticizes utilitarianism for ignoring the separateness of persons — for treating society as if it were a single super-agent whose total welfare should be maximized. His contractualist alternative treats each person as a separate bearer of rights and interests, with the agreement of each person being necessary for the legitimacy of the arrangement.

**4. Reflective equilibrium.** Rawls does not claim his principles are derived from pure reason. They are the result of moving back and forth between principles and moral intuitions, adjusting each in light of the other until they cohere. This is a form of moral epistemology that acknowledges the role of intuition without treating intuitions as sacred.

These are serious arguments. The original position is one of the most powerful thought experiments in the history of moral philosophy, and the two principles have real force when taken together. Any framework that claims to address justice must engage with Rawls's work.

## Where the Framework Agrees

The framework agrees with Rawls on several substantive points:

**1. The basic structure of society matters morally.** Much of what produces welfare or suffering happens at the level of institutions — laws, economic arrangements, political structures — not at the level of individual choices. Moral theory must address institutional structure, not just personal behavior. This is a major Rawlsian insight that the framework incorporates throughout Book V.

**2. Rational agents under uncertainty can reach agreement on institutional principles.** The original position is a useful device for clarifying what rational agents would consent to without bias. The framework uses versions of this move in several places — notably the insurance argument for the welfare state (Chapter 29), which is essentially Rawlsian.

**3. Basic liberties are not subject to aggregate welfare trade-offs.** You cannot justify suppressing political dissent because suppression would make more people happier on average. Liberties protect individuals against the calculus of aggregate welfare, which aligns with the framework's position on rights (Chapter 19).

**4. The worst-off deserve special consideration.** In distributions of resources and opportunities, the people who are most disadvantaged have stronger claims than equal-per-capita arguments would suggest. The framework's commitment to a meaningful safety net (Chapter 29) agrees with this.

**5. Impartiality in institutional design.** Institutions should not be designed to privilege specific groups based on arbitrary features. The original position captures this by forcing you to design institutions without knowing which group you belong to.

**6. Moral reasoning involves reflective equilibrium.** Neither pure deduction from principles nor pure reliance on intuitions produces good moral conclusions. The back-and-forth between principles and intuitions, adjusting each in light of the other, is how moral reasoning actually works and should work.

This is a substantial list of agreements. The framework is significantly Rawlsian in its political philosophy -- Rawls's work has influenced every serious theorist on these questions since 1971.

## Where the Framework Disagrees

Despite these agreements, the framework differs from Rawls on several specific points:

### Disagreement 1: Maximin vs. Expected Value

Rawls argues that rational agents behind the veil would choose maximin reasoning — maximize the minimum, protect against the worst case. The framework is skeptical of this.

Behind a veil of genuine uncertainty, maximin is not the only rational choice. It is the rational choice if you are extremely risk-averse, but not everyone is. A rational agent might also consider expected value, which would lead to principles that maximize average welfare (subject to some minimum floor) rather than strictly maximizing the minimum.

Rawls has responses: the stakes are high enough and the uncertainty severe enough that risk-aversion is especially rational. But these are contestable empirical claims, not obvious truths. The framework's position: **the choice between maximin and expected value is not settled by pure rationality; it depends on risk preferences that can reasonably vary**.

This matters because maximin and expected value can generate different principles. Maximin implies strict equality unless inequality benefits the worst off. Expected value implies more tolerance for inequality that benefits the average without harming the worst. The framework is closer to expected value (tempered by concern for the worst off) than to pure maximin.

### Disagreement 2: The Difference Principle Is Too Strong

Rawls's difference principle says inequality is justified only if it benefits the least advantaged. This is a demanding standard. It rules out many inequalities that produce aggregate gains but don't specifically help the worst off.

The framework's view: **the difference principle is a reasonable starting point but too strong as an absolute requirement**. Consider: a policy change that makes everyone better off except the worst off (who are unaffected) is not a violation of the framework's principles, even though it does nothing specifically for the worst off. Rawls's strict version of the difference principle would be indifferent between this and a policy that helps the worst off but leaves everyone else unchanged, as long as both do nothing to further help the worst. This indifference is odd.

More generally, the framework allows inequality to be justified by aggregate welfare gains up to some point, with special attention to the worst off as a constraint. This is more permissive than strict Rawlsianism but more restrictive than pure utilitarianism.

### Disagreement 3: The Veil of Ignorance Is Too Thick

Rawls's veil hides a great deal. Behind it, you know almost nothing about yourself — not your sex, class, race, talents, or even your conception of the good. This is supposed to ensure impartiality by preventing you from tailoring principles to your own advantage.

But the veil is so thick that it is unclear what the person behind it actually prefers. Rawls assumes they prefer to have "primary goods" (basic liberties, opportunities, income, self-respect) because these are useful for any conception of the good. But the specific weighting of these goods — how much liberty vs. how much opportunity vs. how much income — depends on values that are themselves hidden behind the veil.

This makes the thought experiment underdetermined. Different plausible assumptions about what the person behind the veil wants produce different principles. Rawls argues for his specific principles, but critics have produced alternative arguments yielding different principles, each claiming to represent what "rational agents behind the veil" would choose.

The framework's position: **the veil of ignorance is a useful heuristic but not a decision procedure**. It clarifies impartiality and forces thinking about who benefits from various arrangements. It does not generate specific principles without additional assumptions, and the additional assumptions are doing a lot of work that Rawls's framework disguises.

### Disagreement 4: Public Reason Is Narrower Than Rawls Claims

Rawls's later work (*Political Liberalism*, 1993) introduced the concept of "public reason" — the kind of argument that is appropriate for political contexts involving citizens with different comprehensive views. Public reason requires using arguments that all reasonable citizens could accept, avoiding appeals to specific religious or philosophical doctrines.

This is a valuable concept, and the framework uses something like it. But Rawls's version restricts public reason too narrowly. He holds that citizens should not appeal to their comprehensive doctrines in political argument, even when those doctrines provide the real support for their positions. This produces a kind of enforced disingenuousness — citizens are expected to offer sanitized reasons in public while holding different reasons in private.

The framework's position: **public reason should be about the quality of arguments that can persuade reasonable people across ideological boundaries, not about pretending your own reasoning doesn't draw on broader commitments**. You can acknowledge your own comprehensive doctrine while also offering arguments that don't depend on it. This is more honest and more effective than strict Rawlsian public reason.

### Disagreement 5: The Focus on Distribution Misses Production

Rawls focuses almost entirely on the just distribution of what a society produces. How the production happens — what kinds of work people do, what their working conditions are, how they relate to each other economically — is secondary. This is a conspicuous gap in the theory, and it has been noted by critics from multiple traditions (including Marxists, virtue theorists, and libertarians).

A theory of justice that addresses distribution without addressing production is incomplete. Work is part of how people live their lives, develop their capacities, and relate to each other. A society that distributes goods fairly but treats its workers poorly is not fully just.

The framework's position: **production matters morally, not just distribution**. Chapter 29 (labor and automation) addressed some of this; Chapter 28 (safety net) addressed more. The framework's concept of the value vector (including autonomy, competence, and meaning) is designed to accommodate the full range of morally relevant features, not just distribution.

### Disagreement 6: Rawls's Liberalism Is Historically Contingent

Rawls's principles are presented as universal — what rational agents behind the veil would choose, regardless of historical context. But his specific principles look suspiciously like the liberal welfare state as it existed in mid-twentieth-century developed democracies. The correlation between his abstract argument and the particular institutions of his time and place is striking.

This does not refute Rawls, but it raises the suspicion that his "universal" principles are more historically specific than he acknowledged. A person behind the veil in different historical circumstances, with different background knowledge about what societies can achieve, might choose different principles. Rawls's principles are defensible, but they are not uniquely rational.

The framework's position: **principles of justice should be held with historical humility**. What we can defend now, given current empirical knowledge and moral reflection, may differ from what a future generation can defend. This is consistent with the framework's general commitment to moral progress and updating.

## The Nozick Challenge

A different kind of critique of Rawls comes from the libertarian direction, most famously from Robert Nozick in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974). Nozick's position is not the framework's, but engaging with it clarifies what the framework does and doesn't take from Rawls.

Nozick's argument: Rawls focuses on distribution at a point in time, but justice is about the *history* of how holdings came to be. If I justly acquire something (say, by earning it through my labor), and I justly transfer it to you (by giving it or trading for it), then your having it is just — regardless of how it fits into some pattern of distribution. Rawls's approach, Nozick argues, treats distributions as patterns to be imposed rather than as the results of legitimate transactions.

Nozick's alternative is an entitlement theory: justice depends on whether holdings are acquired and transferred through just processes, not on whether the resulting pattern looks fair by some standard external to the transactions.

The framework's response: **both distribution and process matter, and the pure extremes fail**. Pure process-based justice (Nozick) ignores the fact that initial acquisitions can be unjust (the first settlers taking land from native populations, for instance), and that these original injustices affect current distributions. Pure pattern-based justice (Rawls) ignores the fact that processes can be legitimate or illegitimate, and that people have reasonable expectations about what they can keep when they earn it.

The framework's position combines both: starting distributions matter, but so do ongoing processes. Initial acquisitions that were unjust (conquest, slavery, theft) create ongoing claims for remediation. But voluntary exchanges and legitimate production also create entitlements that cannot simply be overridden by appealing to distributive patterns.

## Amartya Sen and the Capability Critique

Another important challenge to Rawls comes from Amartya Sen, whose capabilities approach we drew on heavily in Chapter 28. Sen's critique: Rawls focuses on "primary goods" (liberties, opportunities, income), but what matters is what people can *actually do* with those goods. Two people with the same primary goods may have very different capabilities — one person's income may enable them to live a full life while another person, due to disability or health, cannot achieve similar functioning with the same resources.

Rawls's focus on primary goods obscures these differences and can produce "just" distributions that leave some people unable to live decent lives. Sen's alternative is to focus on capabilities directly — what people can actually be and do — rather than on the resources they possess.

The framework's position: **Sen is right, and the framework's welfare conception incorporates his insight**. The value vector (hedonic tone, autonomy, competence, connection, understanding, meaning) is closer to a capability conception than to a primary goods conception. A policy that produces equal primary goods but unequal capabilities is not fully just. The framework thus departs from Rawls on this specific point while preserving his broader insight that institutional structure matters and that the worst off deserve special attention.

## The Original Position, Revisited

Despite the disagreements, the original position remains a powerful thought experiment, and the framework uses versions of it. It is worth being precise about what the device does and does not accomplish.

**What it does well**:
- Clarifies the role of bias in political thinking. By imagining a perspective without personal stakes, we notice how much of our political reasoning is self-interested.
- Provides a framework for impartial institutional design. Whatever specific principles we settle on, they should be defensible to people who might end up anywhere in the society.
- Generates insurance-type arguments for welfare and risk-sharing that the framework endorses.
- Supports the case for basic liberties that protect individuals against aggregate welfare arguments.

**What it does not do well**:
- Generate uniquely determined principles from rationality alone. The device underdetermines the answer, and additional assumptions are needed to reach specific conclusions.
- Replace actual political engagement. Real politics involves real people with real interests, not hypothetical agents behind imaginary veils. The thought experiment clarifies thinking but does not substitute for dialogue, compromise, and democratic decision-making.
- Handle dynamic considerations. Rawls's framework is largely static — what a just society looks like at a point in time. It handles change and dynamism less well.
- Address non-citizens. The original position assumes the participants are all in the same society. Questions about people outside the society (immigrants, foreigners, future generations) are handled awkwardly.

The framework uses the original position as one tool among several, not as the foundational device it is for Rawls.

## The Framework's Political Philosophy, Restated

The framework's political philosophy (developed in Book V) can be compared to Rawls directly:

**On basic liberties**: agreement with Rawls that these take priority and cannot be traded off against aggregate welfare, though we ground them in coordination considerations rather than in pure agreement behind a veil.

**On fair equality of opportunity**: agreement with Rawls that positions should be genuinely open to all, combined with attention to the capability considerations Sen raised.

**On distribution**: we reject the strong difference principle but accept that the worst off deserve special consideration. Our position is between Rawls and pure utilitarianism.

**On the basic structure**: agreement that institutions matter morally, combined with specific attention to how institutions actually function (mechanism design, not just pattern-specification).

**On public reason**: agreement that political argument should use reasons that can be shared across ideological lines, combined with honest acknowledgment of broader commitments when relevant.

**On production as well as distribution**: the framework cares about work, meaning, and the structure of economic life in ways Rawls does not fully address.

**On moral epistemology**: agreement on reflective equilibrium as the method, combined with more attention to empirical considerations (game theory, psychology, history).

The overall position is recognizable as Rawlsian in its broad structure but differs in specific details. The framework is what you might get if you started with Rawls and adjusted to accommodate the capability critique, the coordination analysis, the concerns about production, and the empirical grounding of Book I.

## The Verdict

Rawls is the rival the framework respects most. His insights are real, his arguments are serious, and his influence on the framework is significant. Where we disagree, the disagreement is specific and substantive, not fundamental. A person who accepts Rawls's framework and then carefully engages with the considerations in Book I and elsewhere would likely arrive at something close to the framework's position.

This chapter has not refuted Rawls — his position remains defensible in its main outlines. What it has done is identify the specific adjustments the framework makes and explain why. The result is a political philosophy that is Rawlsian in structure but modified by considerations Rawls did not fully engage with.

If you want to call the framework a form of "modified Rawlsianism," that is not quite right (because we differ on several specific points), but it is closer to the truth than calling it Kantian or utilitarian would be. The debt to Rawls is the largest of any contemporary philosopher, and the engagement with him is the most sustained.

## Summary

1. **Rawls's contract argument** derives principles of justice from the choices rational agents would make behind a veil of ignorance. This is the most powerful framework in contemporary political philosophy.

2. **The framework agrees with Rawls** on the importance of basic structure, the utility of original-position reasoning, the priority of basic liberties, the special status of the worst off, impartiality in institutional design, and reflective equilibrium as method.

3. **The framework disagrees with Rawls** on maximin reasoning (we prefer modified expected value), the strength of the difference principle (we are more permissive), the thickness of the veil (we think it underdetermines the answer), public reason (we allow more honest acknowledgment of commitments), the focus on distribution alone (production matters too), and the historical universality of Rawlsian liberalism.

4. **Nozick's libertarian critique** points at real problems with pure pattern-based justice, which the framework addresses by recognizing that both process and distribution matter.

5. **Sen's capability critique** is right — the framework's welfare conception is closer to capabilities than to primary goods, and this matters for the specific content of justice.

6. **The original position is a useful tool but not a decision procedure.** It clarifies impartiality and generates insurance arguments but does not yield unique principles from rationality alone.

7. **The framework's political philosophy is significantly Rawlsian** in structure while differing on specific points. The debt is real and the engagement is the most sustained of any rival system.
