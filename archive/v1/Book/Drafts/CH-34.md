# Chapter 34: Bioethics — Enhancement, Genetic Engineering, and the Boundaries of the Human

---

> *"To refuse the gift of enhancement in the name of naturalness is to refuse the responsibility of being human."*
> — a position the framework takes seriously but does not fully endorse

---

## The Shape of the Problem

Biotechnology is a peculiar domain in the framework's scope. Unlike AI, it is not new — humans have been modifying biology through selective breeding for millennia. Unlike climate change, the consequences are often individual rather than collective. Unlike nuclear weapons, the technology is distributed rather than concentrated. Yet bioethics is one of the most contentious and unsettled areas of applied ethics, precisely because the capabilities are expanding faster than the moral frameworks to evaluate them.

This chapter addresses several distinct but related questions:

1. **Genetic engineering of humans**: Should we edit the genomes of individuals? Of embryos? What about germline edits that pass to future generations?

2. **Enhancement vs. therapy**: Is there a morally relevant line between fixing diseases and enhancing normal human capabilities?

3. **Reproductive technologies**: IVF, selection, cloning, surrogate motherhood.

4. **End-of-life decisions**: Euthanasia, physician-assisted death, persistent vegetative state.

5. **Research ethics**: Consent, exploitation, dual-use research.

6. **Synthetic biology**: Creating organisms that do not exist in nature.

Each of these deserves more space than we can give here. The chapter will sketch the framework's approach to each and indicate where additional work is needed. The common thread: **bioethics should be approached with the same tools as the rest of applied ethics — valence analysis, coordination considerations, rights frameworks, mechanism design — rather than with special appeals to "nature" or "dignity" that operate outside the framework**.

---

## The "Nature" Argument

A recurring move in bioethics is to invoke "nature" or "the natural" as a moral constraint. Genetic engineering is wrong because it is "unnatural." Cloning is wrong because it violates "the natural order." Enhancement is wrong because we should accept our "natural" capacities rather than modifying them. This kind of argument shows up across political and religious traditions.

The framework's response: **the natural/unnatural distinction is not doing the moral work its defenders claim**.

Consider: vaccines are unnatural. Eyeglasses are unnatural. Agriculture is unnatural. Birth control is unnatural. Antibiotics are unnatural. Medicine in general is unnatural -- most people throughout history died of natural causes that we now consider treatable.

Nobody seriously argues that vaccines are wrong because they are unnatural. The natural/unnatural distinction is selectively deployed -- unnatural things we like are fine, unnatural things we dislike are objectionable. This is not a principle; it is a veneer over other considerations.

The framework's position: **what matters is whether the intervention produces welfare or harm, preserves or destroys important capabilities, respects or violates rights — not whether it is "natural"**. Arguments against specific biotechnologies must appeal to these considerations, not to the natural/unnatural framing.

This does not mean the opposing intuitions are wrong. When people react negatively to certain biotech developments, the reaction often tracks real concerns -- safety, distributional fairness, unknown consequences, power concentration. These deserve engagement, but in the language of welfare and rights, not in the language of nature.

### The Sandel Challenge: Giftedness and Hubris

The strongest version of the anti-enhancement argument comes not from crude naturalism but from Michael Sandel's *The Case Against Perfection* (2007). Sandel argues that the drive to genetically enhance our children represents a "bid for mastery and dominion" that fails to appreciate the gifted character of human life:

> *"The more alive we are to the chanced nature of our lot, the more reason we have to share our fate with others."*
> — Michael Sandel, *The Case Against Perfection*

Sandel's worry is not that enhancement is unnatural but that it changes the relationship between parents and children in a morally significant way. A parent who accepts a child as a gift — with whatever traits they happen to have — relates to that child differently from a parent who selected the child's traits from a menu. The selecting parent exercises a form of control over the child's identity that the accepting parent does not, and this control may undermine the solidarity and humility that come from sharing an unchosen fate.

This is a serious argument and the framework does not dismiss it. The response: Sandel is right that unconstrained enhancement could undermine solidarity, and that an attitude of pure mastery toward our children's biology is morally impoverished. But the framework distinguishes between *enhancement that removes suffering* (correcting genetic disease, preventing disability) and *enhancement that imposes parental preferences on a child's open future* (selecting for intelligence, appearance, temperament). The first is warranted by the valence axiom; the second faces a legitimate Sandel-type objection that the framework takes seriously as a constraint on enhancement policy, not as an absolute prohibition.

## Genetic Engineering: Therapy and Enhancement

Genetic engineering of humans can be divided into two categories:

**Somatic gene therapy**: modifying the genes of a specific person's cells, usually for therapeutic purposes. The changes do not pass to future generations and are broadly similar to other medical treatments.

**Germline gene editing**: modifying the genes of embryos, sperm, or eggs in ways that pass to all descendants. This is qualitatively different because it affects not only the individual but everyone in their genetic lineage.

The framework's position on somatic gene therapy: **when it is safe and effective, it is defensible on the same grounds as other medical interventions**. If a child has a genetic disease and gene therapy can cure them, the case is straightforward. The relevant questions are empirical (does it work?) and procedural (is there informed consent? is the risk-benefit analysis honest?). There is no special "it's genetic" consideration that changes the analysis.

The framework's position on germline editing is more cautious, for several reasons:

**1. Heritable changes affect people who cannot consent.** Future generations will live with the modifications. They cannot be asked whether they want them. This is a genuine concern, though it applies to almost any significant decision parents make for their children, including the decision to have children at all.

**2. The long-term consequences are uncertain.** A change that seems beneficial in context A may produce unexpected problems in context B. With heritable changes, the consequences propagate across generations before the problems are detected.

**3. Early adoption creates hard-to-reverse conditions.** If some populations undergo germline enhancement and others do not, the differences could compound in ways that create permanent divisions. This is not the strongest argument against germline editing (slippery slope arguments are often weak), but it is worth considering.

**4. Safety standards are still developing.** Current gene editing techniques (CRISPR and its successors) have off-target effects, mosaicism, and other issues that make them not yet safe enough for reliable germline use in humans.

Given these considerations, the framework's current position on germline editing:
- **Therapeutic germline editing for serious diseases**: defensible in principle when the technology is safe enough, though currently it is not safe enough. The welfare gain from preventing severe heritable diseases is real and large.
- **Enhancement germline editing**: more cautious, not because enhancement is inherently wrong, but because the stakes are higher, the reversal is harder, and the social consequences are more complex. This does not mean "never" — it means "with more caution and more oversight."
- **Moratorium on irresponsible deployment**: the He Jiankui case (the first reported germline edits in humans, conducted with inadequate safety protocols) shows what happens when cautious norms break down. International coordination to prevent such cases from becoming common is warranted.

## The Enhancement Debate

A distinct question: assuming gene editing or other enhancement technologies become safe, *should* people use them for non-therapeutic purposes?

The framework's answer: **mostly yes, with caveats**.

The positive case for enhancement:

**1. Enhancement that genuinely improves welfare is good.** If a technology lets people be smarter, healthier, happier, more able to pursue their goals, it is producing welfare — and welfare is what the framework's valence axiom cares about. There is no moral reason to refuse welfare gains.

**2. The line between therapy and enhancement is arbitrary.** What counts as "normal" and what counts as "above normal" is a moving target. Vaccines are enhancement — they give the immune system capabilities it did not have before. Eyeglasses are enhancement — they give people vision they would not naturally have. Nothing morally relevant happens at the boundary between "fixing a deficiency" and "adding a capability."

**3. Voluntary enhancement respects autonomy.** If informed adults choose to enhance themselves, their choice reflects their own values about what kind of person they want to be. Restricting enhancement on paternalistic grounds violates the autonomy that the framework has been defending throughout.

**4. The alternative is not "humans as we are now" but "humans as accidents of biology made them."** The framework rejects treating evolutionary outcomes as normative (Chapter 4). Our current capacities are what natural selection happened to produce, not what a wise creator designed, not what we would choose if we could choose. There is no principled reason to preserve them unchanged.

The caveats:

**1. Access and distribution.** Enhancement technologies that are only available to the wealthy will increase inequality in ways that compound across generations. The framework's commitment to capability for all (Chapter 29) implies that enhancement, if it happens, should be accessible rather than restricted.

**2. Competitive dynamics.** Some enhancements are "positional" — their value depends on having them when others don't. If everyone takes cognitive enhancers, nobody gains a relative advantage, but everyone pays the costs. This creates Moloch traps where individually rational decisions produce collectively worse outcomes. The framework supports coordination mechanisms (regulation, norms) to prevent the worst races.

**3. Long-term consequences.** We should be more cautious about enhancements whose effects are hard to reverse or hard to predict, especially if they affect heritable traits.

**4. Diversity.** A world where everyone is "enhanced" in the same directions could lose the diversity that currently enriches human experience and that provides resilience against unforeseen challenges. Preserving genetic and neurological diversity is worth some consideration, even if it means not pushing everyone toward a single "optimal" profile.

**5. Preservation of meaningful human experience.** Some things that look like "flaws" from one angle are the conditions for meaningful human experience from another. A certain amount of struggle, effort, uncertainty, and difficulty is part of what makes human life valuable, and enhancements that eliminate these entirely might diminish what they were supposed to enhance. This is a vague concern and should not become an argument against all improvement, but it deserves thought.

On balance, the framework supports enhancement in most cases while recognizing the legitimate concerns that moderate this support. This puts us at odds with both traditional bioconservatism and pure techno-optimism. The right position is engagement with specific cases, informed by empirical reality and honest assessment of tradeoffs.

## Reproductive Technologies

IVF, genetic screening, selection, cloning, artificial wombs — these technologies have transformed human reproduction in ways that were science fiction a generation ago.

The framework's general approach: **reproductive decisions are among the most personal and should generally be respected as autonomous choices, subject to limits when the welfare of the resulting child is at stake**.

Specific positions:

**IVF**: uncontroversially supportable. It helps people have children they could not otherwise have. The welfare gain is real, the risks are manageable, and the moral concerns raised against it (disruption of "natural" reproduction) fail the nature-argument critique above.

**Prenatal genetic testing**: supportable, with the usual caveats about informed consent and psychological support for parents making decisions based on test results.

**Embryo selection for disease prevention**: defensible. Selecting embryos without severe genetic diseases is morally similar to treating a disease after birth, except that it prevents the disease from occurring at all.

**Embryo selection for non-medical traits (sex, eye color, predicted intelligence, etc.)**: more contested. The framework's position: in principle these choices are within parental autonomy, but they raise concerns about the specific traits being selected (sex selection in cultures that devalue girls), about the quality of the information driving selection (polygenic scores for complex traits are noisy), and about the effects on the child of being brought into existence as an engineered product. Not forbidden, but subject to more scrutiny than disease selection.

**Reproductive cloning**: contested, but the framework is not opposed in principle. The strongest arguments against cloning have been about psychological welfare of clones (feeling like copies rather than individuals), safety (current cloning techniques have high failure rates), and distribution (who would be cloned and why). These are reasonable concerns that should be addressed through specific policy, not through absolute prohibition.

**Artificial wombs**: potentially revolutionary and broadly supportable. They could reduce the health risks of pregnancy, enable earlier viability for premature infants, and expand reproductive options. They also raise new questions about parental relationships, commodification of reproduction, and distribution of access. These need engagement as the technology develops.

**Surrogate motherhood**: depends heavily on the specific arrangement. Voluntary surrogacy with proper consent and compensation is defensible. Exploitative surrogacy that takes advantage of poor women's economic desperation is not. The framework endorses regulation that enables the former while preventing the latter.

## End-of-Life Decisions

The framework's position on end-of-life decisions was sketched in Chapter 16. To summarize and extend:

**1. Patient autonomy is central.** Competent adults have strong rights to make decisions about their own end-of-life care, including refusing treatment that would extend their lives.

**2. Palliative care is a moral imperative.** Regardless of other decisions, people facing serious illness should have access to effective pain management and symptom control. Denying this is cruelty, not principled ethics.

**3. Physician-assisted death**: the framework supports it in narrow circumstances — terminal illness with intractable suffering, competent adult patient, informed decision after time for reflection, appropriate safeguards. The concerns about slippery slopes and abuse are real but can be addressed through procedure (waiting periods, multiple medical opinions, psychological evaluation, mandatory exploration of alternatives). Well-regulated physician-assisted death programs in several jurisdictions have worked tolerably well without the predicted cascade of abuse.

**4. Persistent vegetative state**: when there is no realistic prospect of recovery, continuing life-sustaining treatment indefinitely against the apparent preferences of the patient (as expressed in advance directives, by legal surrogates, or inferred from their prior values) is not required by the framework. The resources can be better used, and the patient's autonomy (to the extent it survives) suggests they would often prefer not to continue in such a state.

**5. Advance directives matter.** People should have the right to specify in advance what kinds of medical interventions they do and do not want. The state should enforce these directives. Medical institutions should honor them. Family members who override them because of their own discomfort with death are imposing their preferences on the patient.

**6. The distinction between active and passive decisions is less morally important than it appears.** Refusing treatment that would save a life and actively taking a life are both decisions to end a life. The moral framework applies to both, and the difference between "letting die" and "killing" is often more rhetorical than substantive. This does not mean all end-of-life decisions are equivalent, but it does mean that the acts/omissions distinction should not do all the moral work it is sometimes asked to do.

These positions are moderate by the standards of American bioethics (more cautious than the Netherlands, less restrictive than Catholic hospitals) and reflect the framework's commitment to patient autonomy and welfare rather than to any specific religious or philosophical tradition.

## Research Ethics

Research ethics is a well-developed field within bioethics, and the framework mostly endorses its mainstream conclusions:

**1. Informed consent is essential.** Research on human subjects requires that subjects understand what they are agreeing to, have the right to refuse without penalty, and can withdraw at any time. The Tuskegee syphilis study, the Nazi medical experiments, and many other historical abuses show what happens when this principle is violated.

**2. Risk-benefit analysis must be honest.** Researchers have incentives to downplay risks and overstate benefits. Independent review boards (IRBs) exist to provide a check on this. The framework supports IRBs while recognizing that they have their own failure modes and need ongoing reform.

**3. Protection of vulnerable populations.** Prisoners, children, people with cognitive disabilities, and people in desperate circumstances cannot provide fully free consent and require additional protections. This is not paternalism but recognition of power asymmetries that compromise autonomy.

**4. Dual-use research**: research that could produce both beneficial applications and harmful ones (like gain-of-function research on pathogens). The framework's position: such research requires more than usual scrutiny, and some forms may warrant moratoriums or strict containment. The question is always whether the benefits exceed the risks under realistic assumptions about both — not whether the research sounds exciting or unnerving.

**5. Benefit sharing**: when research is conducted in poor countries or on specific populations, the benefits should be shared with those populations, not just extracted for wealthier communities. This is both a fairness consideration and a practical one — without benefit sharing, research participation declines over time as populations realize they are being exploited.

**6. Publication and transparency**: scientific research should be published openly to enable replication, criticism, and building on findings. The framework opposes the current system in which much publicly-funded research is trapped behind publisher paywalls, and supports movements toward open access.

## Synthetic Biology

Synthetic biology — engineering biological systems or creating new ones — raises distinctive concerns. Unlike genetic engineering of existing organisms, it involves designing organisms from scratch or heavily modifying them to do things they would never do naturally.

The framework's position:

**1. The potential benefits are large.** Synthetic biology could produce new medicines, new materials, new approaches to pollution remediation, new food sources. These are real goods worth pursuing.

**2. The risks are also large.** Engineered organisms that escape into the environment could have unpredictable ecological effects. Synthetic pathogens (whether created deliberately or as byproducts of dual-use research) could cause pandemics that exceed anything naturally occurring. The power to create new biological systems is also the power to create new biological dangers.

**3. Biocontainment and biosafety are essential.** Research on potentially dangerous synthetic organisms should be conducted in facilities with appropriate containment, with appropriate oversight, and with attention to the consequences of containment failure.

**4. International coordination matters.** Like AI, synthetic biology faces race-to-the-bottom dynamics. Countries with weaker regulations could become hubs for dangerous research. International norms and agreements are necessary, even if difficult to achieve.

**5. Distinguishing beneficial from dangerous research.** Most synthetic biology is beneficial (microbes engineered to produce medicines, for example) and should proceed. Some is dangerous (pathogens engineered to be more transmissible) and should be restricted or prohibited. The distinction requires case-by-case judgment and cannot be reduced to simple rules.

**6. Public engagement.** The public has a legitimate interest in how powerful biotechnologies are developed and deployed. Developing these technologies behind closed doors without public input creates both legitimacy problems and risk of backlash. Meaningful public engagement is difficult but important.

## The Common Theme

Across the different topics in this chapter, a common theme emerges: **bioethics should be approached with the same tools as the rest of applied ethics**. The questions are specific, the technologies are specific, and the moral analysis should proceed through welfare considerations, rights frameworks, and coordination analysis rather than through appeals to the naturalness of the status quo or to vague dignity claims that do not specify what is actually being violated.

This makes the framework's bioethics positions more liberal than traditional bioethics on enhancement, genetic engineering, physician-assisted death, and reproductive technologies -- but more cautious than pure transhumanism on distributional concerns, long-term consequences, and irreversibility.

The position is not middle-of-the-road splitting. It is principled analysis that yields positions sometimes aligning with one camp and sometimes with another, depending on which considerations are weightiest in specific cases.

## What the Framework Does Not Fully Address

Several areas remain underdeveloped:

**1. Animal welfare in research.** The framework's position on animal welfare (Chapter 5) has implications for animal research that are not fully worked out here. In general, animal research that produces significant human welfare gains is defensible, subject to minimizing suffering and using alternatives where possible. The specific calibration needs more work.

**2. Neuroethics.** Brain-computer interfaces, cognitive enhancement through direct neural intervention, memory modification. These raise distinctive questions about identity and autonomy that the framework can approach with its general tools but has not fully addressed.

**3. The moral status of enhanced or modified humans.** If cognitive enhancement produces individuals substantially different from current humans, do they have the same moral status? Do they have obligations to unmodified humans? This is a version of the AI consciousness question applied to biology.

**4. Commons aspects of biology.** The genome is in some sense a shared inheritance. Modifications to the human genome affect not just individuals but the species. This has implications the framework has not fully worked through.

These are left as open questions, flagged for future development rather than pretended to be resolved.

## Summary

1. **The nature/unnature distinction does not do the moral work** that bioconservatism needs. Arguments against biotechnologies must appeal to welfare, rights, and coordination.

2. **Somatic gene therapy** is defensible on standard medical grounds.

3. **Germline gene editing** requires more caution because of irreversibility and effects on non-consenting future generations. Therapeutic uses are more defensible than enhancement uses.

4. **Enhancement is broadly supportable** when safe and accessible, with caveats about distribution, positional competition, diversity, and preservation of meaningful human experience.

5. **Reproductive technologies** are generally within parental autonomy, with concerns about specific uses (sex selection, exploitation) requiring specific responses.

6. **End-of-life decisions** should prioritize patient autonomy, palliative care, and carefully regulated access to physician-assisted death.

7. **Research ethics** should preserve informed consent, risk-benefit analysis, protection of vulnerable populations, dual-use scrutiny, benefit sharing, and transparency.

8. **Synthetic biology** has large potential benefits and large potential risks. Biocontainment, international coordination, and public engagement are essential.
