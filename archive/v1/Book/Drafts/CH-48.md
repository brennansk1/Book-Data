## The Premise of a Reading Path

The framework is not a solo achievement. Every argument in this book stands on the shoulders of other books. A reader who wants to engage the framework at the level of primary sources rather than summaries needs a path through the literature.

This chapter provides that path -- a **reading list organized by the framework's architecture**: foundations, diagnosis, architecture, applied chapters, and defense, with the rival systems represented by their strongest primary sources. A serious reader who completes this syllabus will have read most of what the framework draws on and a substantial sample of what it engages with.

It is not a minimum requirement. The book is self-contained. But a reader who wants to argue with the framework on equal terms, or build on it, or teach it, should eventually read from this list.

The syllabus is organized into **six tracks**, corresponding to the book's architecture. Each track has a **core** (read this first, it is load-bearing) and a **further reading** section (read this when you have time and interest). Within each track, works are ordered roughly from more accessible to more difficult.

## Track 1: Epistemology and Rationality (Foundations for Book I)

Before you can reason well about morality, you have to reason well at all. This track is the precondition for everything else.

### Core

1. **Julia Galef, *The Scout Mindset* (2021).** The most accessible introduction to the distinction between belief-formation as inquiry vs. belief-formation as identity defense. Read this first, even if you have already read work on bias.

2. **Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).** The definitive summary of four decades of research on cognitive biases and the dual-process structure of human cognition. Long but worth every chapter. Skip the optional sections on your second read.

3. **Kevin Simler and Robin Hanson, *The Elephant in the Brain* (2018).** The case that most of what we do is motivated by reasons we hide from ourselves. Essential for understanding why epistemic duty is hard.

### Further reading

- Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting* (2015) — on calibration and forecasting practices.
- Eliezer Yudkowsky, *Rationality: From AI to Zombies* (the Sequences) — the deepest online resource on applied Bayesian reasoning; selective reading recommended.
- Karl Popper, *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) — the philosophical background for fallibilism and error-correction.

### What you should be able to do after this track

- Spot the most common biases in your own reasoning.
- Distinguish calibrated belief from overconfident belief.
- Evaluate evidence in a way that updates rather than anchoring.
- Recognize motivated reasoning, including your own.

## Track 2: Evolution, Mind, and Moral Psychology (Foundations for Chapters 3–5)

The framework's position on consciousness, values, and moral psychology rests on a specific empirical picture of human beings. This track builds that picture.

### Core

1. **Richard Dawkins, *The Selfish Gene* (1976, 40th anniversary edition).** The classic exposition of gene-centered evolution. Essential for understanding why human moral instincts exist and what they were selected for.

2. **Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind* (2012).** The Moral Foundations Theory account of political and moral psychology. Essential for Book I's treatment of the Alien God and the reflective rebellion.

3. **Peter Singer, *Practical Ethics* (3rd ed., 2011).** Singer is a better introduction to contemporary applied ethics than any textbook. Read it to see how a careful utilitarian reasons — Book VIII is in conversation with this book.

### Further reading

- Antonio Damasio, *Descartes' Error* (1994) — on emotion as moral cognition.
- David Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996) — the classic statement of the hard problem of consciousness.
- Frans de Waal, *Primates and Philosophers* (2006) — the animal roots of moral behavior.

### What you should be able to do after this track

- Explain why moral intuitions exist without being normatively authoritative on their own.
- Distinguish what humans are inclined to from what humans should do.
- Hold the hard problem of consciousness open while still acting on valence realism.

---

## Track 3: Game Theory and Coordination (Foundations for Book II)

The framework's diagnosis of moral problems depends on specific formal tools. This track is short but load-bearing.

### Core

1. **Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984, revised edition).** The single most important book for the framework's theory of morality-as-coordination-technology. Read it cover to cover. Every argument in Book II depends on it.

2. **Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960).** Schelling's treatment of tacit bargaining, focal points, and credible commitment. More formal than Axelrod but worth the effort.

3. **Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990).** The case studies and eight design principles that defeat the Tragedy-of-the-Commons argument. Essential for Chapter 8 and every chapter on mechanism design.

### Further reading

- James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998) — the complementary argument about what states cannot do.
- Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) — the classical treatment of why collective action fails.
- Herbert Gintis, *The Bounds of Reason* (2009) — game theory formalized for the social sciences.

### What you should be able to do after this track

- Identify the game-theoretic structure of a social problem.
- Distinguish a Prisoner's Dilemma from a Stag Hunt from an Assurance Game.
- Evaluate whether a proposed mechanism matches the structure of the problem.
- Recognize the conditions under which self-governance works.

## Track 4: The Moral Architecture (Foundations for Book III)

### Core

1. **Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Antifragile* (2012).** Taleb is the best modern source on why rules, conventions, and inherited constraints should be respected under uncertainty. Chapter 9's Chesterton Fence is in conversation with him.

2. **Harry Frankfurt, *The Reasons of Love* (2004) and "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971).** Short, deep, on the structure of caring and commitment. Important for Mode A.

3. **Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981).** The most serious modern critique of detached moral theorizing. You need to know his argument even though the framework partially rejects it.

### Further reading

- Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* — virtue ethics in its original form.
- Martha Nussbaum, *Upheavals of Thought* (2001) — emotions and moral cognition.
- Owen Flanagan, *Varieties of Moral Personality* (1991) — the empirical constraints on moral theories.

### What you should be able to do after this track

- Explain why pre-committed rules are game-theoretically necessary.
- Apply the Chesterton Fence principle without using it as cover for traditionalism.
- Distinguish virtues from feelings, from rules, and from one-off actions.

## Track 5: Political and Economic Philosophy (Foundations for Books V–VI)

This is the largest track, because political and economic philosophy is the applied heart of the framework.

### Core

1. **The Federalist Papers** (Hamilton, Madison, Jay, 1787–88). The classical text on constitutional design for error-correction. Read 10, 14, 15, 39, 46, 47, 48, 51, 62, 63, 68, 78, 84 at minimum.

2. **Friedrich Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945)** and **The Constitution of Liberty (1960)**. The essential Hayekian argument for markets as information systems.

3. **Thomas Sowell, *Basic Economics* (4th ed.) and *A Conflict of Visions* (1987).** Sowell is the most accessible modern writer on economic reasoning and political epistemology. Essential for Book VI and for the diagnosis of why political disagreements persist.

4. **David Friedman, *The Machinery of Freedom* (3rd ed.).** The strongest modern anarcho-capitalist argument. You need the contrast to understand why the framework does not go there.

5. **Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999).** The capability approach. Essential for the safety-net position in Chapter 28.

6. **Bryan Caplan, *The Myth of the Rational Voter* (2007).** The empirical case for rational irrationality in democratic electorates. Essential for Chapter 20.

7. **Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith, *The Dictator's Handbook* (2011).** The selectorate theory of political survival. Essential for understanding why good governance is hard.

### Further reading

- John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971) — the most important work of contemporary political philosophy.
- Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) — the libertarian counterpoint to Rawls.
- John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859) — still the best treatment of free speech and the harm principle.
- Ronald Coase, "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960) — the origin of much modern externality theory.

### What you should be able to do after this track

- Analyze a policy using the seven-step method (Chapter 24) with Hayekian and Pigovian tests applied.
- Distinguish market failures from government failures in specific cases.
- Evaluate institutional design proposals against Ostrom's principles.
- Engage Rawls, Nozick, and Hayek on their own terms.

## Track 6: Rival Moral Systems (Foundations for Book VIII)

Book VIII engages rival systems, but the engagement is only as strong as your familiarity with the primary sources.

### Core

1. **Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785).** Short, dense, essential. The canonical statement of deontology.

2. **John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism* (1863).** The classical defense of utilitarianism, including the responses to standard objections.

3. **Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*.** The canonical statement of virtue ethics. Books I, II, VI, and X are the most important.

4. **Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*, I-II, Questions 90–97 (the Treatise on Law).** The canonical statement of natural law theory. Short compared to the whole *Summa*.

5. **John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971).** You cannot seriously engage contemporary political philosophy without this book.

6. **Confucius, *The Analects*.** The central document of Confucian ethics. Pair with a modern commentary (Slingerland's translation includes selected traditional commentary).

7. **The *Dhammapada*.** The most accessible primary text of Buddhist ethics. Short verses; read slowly.

### Further reading

- Carol Gilligan, *In a Different Voice* (1982) — the origin of care ethics.
- Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981) — already on Track 4.
- Robert Merrihew Adams, *Finite and Infinite Goods* (1999) — the sophisticated modern defense of theistic ethics.
- Karl Marx, *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* — the humanist Marx. Read as philosophy, not economics.
- Johann Broodryk or Mogobe Ramose on Ubuntu philosophy.
- Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984) — the most important modern work on personal identity and population ethics.

### What you should be able to do after this track

- Present each rival system in its strongest form before criticizing it.
- Identify specific arguments in primary sources, rather than relying on summaries.
- Engage readers who come from these traditions in their own terms.

## How to Read This Syllabus

A few practical notes.

**1. You do not need to read all of this before engaging the framework.** The book is self-contained. The syllabus is for deepening, not for gatekeeping.

**2. Read tracks in parallel if that suits you.** A reader might read Haidt (Track 2), Axelrod (Track 3), and Sowell (Track 5) simultaneously and gain from all three. The tracks are organizational, not sequential requirements.

**3. Keep notes.** Serious reading without note-taking is mostly forgotten. Maintain a simple file per book with the key arguments, the strongest passages, and your own objections. These notes become the raw material for your own thinking and for engaging others.

**4. Read in conversation with the framework.** Ask, for each book: where does this agree with the framework? Where does it disagree? Does the framework need to update? Does this author need updating? The goal is not to confirm the framework but to test it against the best available alternatives.

**5. Expect to disagree with this syllabus.** A serious reader will, over time, develop their own sense of what belongs on the list and what doesn't. The syllabus is a starting point, not a canon. If you come to believe a book is missing, add it. If you come to believe a book does not deserve its place, skip it.

**6. Set a realistic pace.** A book a month is fast; a book every three months is sustainable for most readers with other responsibilities. In five years, you will have read thirty to sixty books, and you will be a serious thinker about moral, political, and economic questions whether or not you agree with the framework.

## The Case for Reading at All

A final note, because the case for sustained reading is no longer self-evident in a culture of instant information access.

The framework depends on **durable, structured understanding** -- the kind that comes from spending many hours with carefully argued books, following long chains of reasoning, returning to difficult passages and working them out. Short-form information (articles, podcasts, videos) can introduce ideas, but it cannot give you the deep working memory you need to hold complex arguments while evaluating them.

Reading -- serious, extended, conversational reading -- is what produces the kind of mind the framework asks you to have. A mind that can hold seven premises, follow them to a conclusion, check the conclusion against independent considerations, and notice when a premise needs revision. A mind that can argue with itself productively rather than reaching for the nearest tribal affiliation.

This mind is rarer than it used to be and more needed than ever, because the coordination problems the framework addresses are getting harder and the stakes are getting higher.

The syllabus is one path. Other paths exist. What matters is that you take some path.

## Summary

1. **The syllabus is organized into six core tracks plus one optional track**, corresponding to the framework's architecture: epistemology, moral psychology, game theory, moral architecture, political/economic philosophy, rival systems, and frontier problems.

2. **Each track has a core** (load-bearing reading) and **further reading** (for depth).

3. **The purpose is to enable primary-source engagement** — with the framework, with its rivals, and with the problems the framework aims to address.

4. **The syllabus is a starting point, not a canon.** Add books you find essential. Skip books you judge redundant. The point is the development of the reading practice, not loyalty to this particular list.

5. **Serious reading is what produces the kind of mind the framework asks for.** Short-form information is a supplement, not a substitute.

The next (and final) chapter is the commitment — what it means to take the framework as your own. The reading is how you earn the right to make that commitment honestly. The commitment is what the reading is for.
