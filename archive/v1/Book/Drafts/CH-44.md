# Chapter 44: Against Marxist and Critical Theory Ethics

---

> *"The philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways; the point, however, is to change it."*
> — Karl Marx, *Theses on Feuerbach*

---

## The Challenge

Marxism is often dismissed by mainstream moral philosophy, especially in the English-speaking world, where it is associated with twentieth-century political disasters. This dismissal is a mistake. Marx himself was a serious thinker whose analysis of capitalism, alienation, exploitation, and ideology contains insights that any comprehensive moral theory should engage with. The same applies to the broader tradition of Critical Theory (Frankfurt School, Marcuse, Habermas) and to contemporary critical traditions.

These traditions share several commitments:

1. **Material conditions shape consciousness.** How people think about ethics, politics, and justice is influenced (not determined, but shaped) by their position in material and social structures.

2. **Ideology obscures reality.** Dominant ideas in a society often serve the interests of the dominant group, while appearing to be neutral truths. Critical analysis exposes these ideas as ideology.

3. **Power is pervasive.** Moral and political questions cannot be separated from questions of power — who has it, how it is exercised, who benefits, who is harmed.

4. **Structures matter more than individual intentions.** Systems produce outcomes that no individual participant intended and may not even be aware of. Focusing on individual morality while ignoring structural dynamics is incomplete.

5. **Theory should be practical.** The point of analysis is not just to understand the world but to change it. Theory divorced from practice is impoverished.

The framework must engage with these claims. Some of them, we already endorse. Others, we reject or modify. This chapter works through the engagement carefully, because the stakes are high — if the critical traditions are right about things the framework misses, the framework is incomplete.

## Steel-Manning Marx

Marx's theoretical edifice is too large to summarize fully, but the core claims that bear on ethics include:

**1. Capitalism is exploitative.** Under capitalism, workers produce more value than they receive in wages. The difference (surplus value) is appropriated by capitalists who did not produce it. This exploitation is structural, not accidental — it is how capitalism works, not a distortion of how it should work. Reform that leaves the structure intact cannot eliminate exploitation.

**2. Capitalism alienates workers.** Workers in capitalist production are separated from the products of their labor (which belong to others), from their own creative capacities (which are sold as labor power), from other workers (who become competitors for jobs), and from their own human potential (which is reduced to what the market will pay for). Alienation is not psychological malaise but a structural feature of capitalist production.

**3. Ideology serves class interests.** The dominant ideas in a capitalist society — liberal rights, market efficiency, meritocracy, individual responsibility — are not neutral. They are the ideas that serve the interests of the owning class by legitimating the existing order and obscuring its injustices. Critical analysis exposes this.

**4. History has direction.** Societies develop through predictable stages driven by contradictions in their economic structures. Capitalism will generate crises that will eventually produce its own supersession by a different mode of production (Marx thought socialism, then communism). This historical analysis is not mere prediction; it is the basis for understanding current conditions and strategy.

**5. Class struggle is the motor of change.** The conflicts between those who own means of production and those who work them (along with related conflicts) are what drive historical change. Political action that ignores class dynamics misses the fundamental force shaping society.

**6. Morality is (partly) ideology.** Moral claims are not purely about objective right and wrong; they reflect the interests and perspectives of particular classes. "Bourgeois morality" — with its emphasis on private property, individual responsibility, and liberal rights — is the morality that serves capitalist interests, presented as universal ethics.

**7. The goal is human emancipation.** Beyond fixing specific injustices, the Marxist project aims at liberating humans from conditions that distort their development and prevent their full flourishing. Even in his earliest writings, Marx framed freedom as constitutive of the human rather than optional:

> *"Freedom is so much the essence of man that even its opponents implement it while combating its reality; they want to appropriate for themselves as a most precious ornament what they have rejected as an ornament of human nature."*
> — Karl Marx, "Debates on Freedom of the Press" (1842), *Collected Works* Vol. 1

And his question about whether freedom is "a privilege of particular individuals or whether it is a privilege of the human mind" — whether rights are for the few or for all — captures the universalist impulse that drives the tradition from its earliest days through the mature critique of Capital.

This is a powerful vision. It identifies real problems, offers a systematic account of their causes, and points toward a normative ideal that is appealing even if specific prescriptions are questionable.

## What Marx Gets Right

Several Marxist insights are, in the framework's view, correct:

**1. Structural analysis is essential.** You cannot understand moral and political problems by looking only at individual choices. Structures — economic arrangements, institutional designs, power relationships — shape what choices are available, what incentives operate, and what outcomes occur. The framework's coordination analysis (Book II) and mechanism design approach (Chapter 8) agree with this. We just call it different things.

**2. Ideology is real.** Dominant ideas often serve dominant interests while appearing neutral. The framework has its own version of this insight in Chapter 2 (motivated reasoning, Chapter 2's warnings about self-serving reasoning) and Chapter 4 (the Alien God's influence on moral intuitions). Marx was ahead of much of his time in identifying this phenomenon.

**3. Exploitation is a real category.** When one person produces value that another appropriates without compensation or consent, something morally significant is happening. The framework agrees that this can be wrong, even in voluntary transactions when the voluntariness is undermined by power asymmetries (Chapter 26 discussed this in the context of exchange).

**4. Alienation captures something real.** People can be disconnected from their work, from their capacities, from their communities, from themselves. The framework's treatment of meaningful work (Chapter 30) and human flourishing (Chapter 15) acknowledges this. Marx identified it clearly and named it usefully.

**5. Material conditions matter.** What people think and how they live is heavily influenced by their economic circumstances. Moral theory that ignores material conditions is airy and disconnected from reality. The framework's political and economic chapters take material conditions seriously.

**6. Power must be analyzed.** Who has power, how it is exercised, who benefits, who is harmed — these questions are central to political and moral analysis. Frameworks that ignore power relations are incomplete. The framework's engagement with institutional design, rights, and the state all involve power analysis.

**7. Theory should inform practice.** The point of moral and political analysis is not just intellectual understanding but to affect how people live and how societies are organized. The framework agrees — we have been applying theory to practice throughout Books IV-VII.

These are substantial concessions. The framework takes Marxist insights seriously and incorporates many of them. The disagreements are about what to do with these insights, not about whether they are valid.

## Where the Framework Disagrees with Marx

Despite the agreements, the framework differs from Marxism on several specific points:

### Disagreement 1: The Labor Theory of Value

Marx's economic analysis rests on the labor theory of value — the claim that the value of a commodity is determined by the socially necessary labor time required to produce it. From this, the theory of surplus value and exploitation follows. If labor is the source of value, and workers receive less than the value they produce, they are being exploited.

The problem: **modern economics has largely rejected the labor theory of value**. Value is better understood as determined by marginal utility and scarcity. Labor is one input among several (capital, land, entrepreneurship, technology, natural resources), and the marginal revolution (Menger, Jevons, Walras) provided a more accurate account of how prices form.

This matters because much of Marx's moral analysis depends on the labor theory. If labor is not the sole source of value, then the extraction of "surplus value" is not the theft it appears to be. Capitalists, entrepreneurs, and workers all contribute to value, and each can reasonably claim some share of the result.

The framework's response: **exploitation is a real category, but it should be redefined**. Exploitation, in the framework's usage, is when one party extracts value from another through coercion, fraud, or severe power asymmetries that undermine genuine consent. This happens in some employment relationships, but not in all. A voluntary exchange between a worker and an employer, under conditions of reasonable labor markets with multiple employers and some worker mobility, is not exploitation in the problematic sense. An exchange under conditions of severe power asymmetry (monopsony, coercion, desperation) might be.

This is a weaker claim than Marx's, but it is more defensible empirically. And it points at specific cases where intervention is warranted without condemning all capitalist production as inherently exploitative.

### Disagreement 2: Historical Materialism and Prediction

Marx's predictions about historical development -- the intensification of class struggle, the immiseration of workers, capitalism's replacement by socialism -- have mostly not come true. Capitalism has proven more adaptive than Marx expected. Workers in wealthy capitalist countries are generally better off than their ancestors. Class struggle has been moderated by labor laws, welfare states, and the expansion of the middle class. Where the transition to socialism was attempted, it produced economic failure and authoritarianism.

This does not refute everything Marx said, but it refutes his specific historical predictions, suggesting that the theoretical machinery generating them has problems.

The framework's response: **structural analysis is valuable, but historical prediction is harder than Marx thought**. Societies evolve in ways that depend on many variables, and linear predictions from current structures to future outcomes are unreliable. The framework uses structural analysis as a tool for understanding specific problems, not as the basis for sweeping predictions about the direction of history.

### Disagreement 3: The Critique of Morality Goes Too Far

Marx argued that morality is partly ideology, serving the interests of dominant classes. This is true in part. But Marx sometimes extended the claim to a general skepticism about moral reasoning, treating moral claims as mere reflections of class position and material interests.

The framework's response: **ideology is a real phenomenon, but it does not discredit all moral reasoning**. Some moral claims are objective — grounded in valence and coordination considerations that are not reducible to class interests. Some moral claims are ideology — serving particular interests while claiming universality. The skill is to distinguish the two, which requires moral reasoning that does not collapse into pure critique.

If Marx were right that all moral reasoning is ideology, then his own moral claims (about exploitation, alienation, emancipation) would also be ideology, and the theory would be self-undermining. Marx himself was not entirely consistent on this; he sometimes wrote as if his critique provided access to genuine moral truth that bourgeois morality obscured. The framework is more consistent: moral reasoning can track genuine features of reality, while also being subject to distortion by interest and ideology.

### Disagreement 4: Revolution as Solution

Marx argued that the structural problems of capitalism cannot be solved by reform — the system must be replaced. Revolution is the mechanism. This commits Marxism to radical political action and, in the 20th century implementation, to violent seizure of state power.

The framework rejects this prescription on several grounds:

**1. Historical track record.** Revolutions aiming at the replacement of capitalism with socialism have typically produced regimes that were worse than what they replaced. The Soviet Union, Mao's China, Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, Venezuela in recent decades. This is not random misfortune; it reflects something about the difficulty of replacing complex systems with untested alternatives and the susceptibility of revolutionary movements to authoritarian capture.

**2. Error-correction.** The framework's commitment to error-correction primacy (Chapter 18) suggests that gradual reform is preferable to revolutionary replacement. Reforms can be tested, adjusted, and reversed if they fail. Revolutions produce sudden transitions that cannot easily be reversed, and when they fail, the costs are enormous.

**3. Reformist alternatives have worked.** Social democracy — reformed capitalism with strong welfare states and labor protections — has produced outcomes significantly better than both pure capitalism and revolutionary socialism. The Nordic countries, while not perfect, demonstrate that reformist paths can achieve many of the goods that revolutionary Marxism aimed at.

**4. The assumption of radical replacement is flawed.** Marx assumed that capitalism could be replaced with a wholly different system that would avoid its pathologies without introducing new ones. This is speculative. The framework is more cautious: gradual change within a functioning system is safer than radical replacement, and specific reforms can address specific problems without requiring wholesale system change.

This is not a defense of the status quo. The framework supports significant reforms — Chapter 28 on the safety net, Chapter 29 on labor, Chapter 30 on fiscal policy, Chapter 27 on externalities all argue for substantial changes. But reform rather than revolution is the framework's preferred mechanism, and the historical evidence supports this preference.

### Disagreement 5: Class as the Primary Category

Marx treated class — defined by relationship to the means of production — as the primary social category. Other divisions (race, sex, nationality, religion) were secondary, reducible to class in the last analysis.

This is not supported by the evidence. Class matters, but so do other divisions, and they are not reducible to class. Sex-based oppression exists in both capitalist and non-capitalist societies. Racial hierarchies are not mere functions of class positions. Religious conflicts have dynamics independent of economics. The irreducibility of these divisions is a significant problem for classical Marxism.

Contemporary critical theorists have addressed this by expanding the categories — intersectionality, critical race theory, feminist critique, postcolonial analysis. These developments are genuine improvements on classical Marxism's reductionism. But they also change the theory significantly, and the result is no longer simply Marxism — it is a broader critical tradition that includes Marxism as one component.

The framework's response: **multiple forms of power and oppression exist, and the specific analysis depends on the specific case**. Economic class is one important dimension. Race is another. Sex is another. Nationality is another. These interact in complex ways, and no single category is primary across all situations. The framework uses the analytical tools relevant to specific questions rather than privileging one category over others.

## Critical Theory and Its Successors

The Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer, Marcuse, Habermas) developed Marxist insights in new directions, often engaging with questions Marx himself did not address in depth: culture, psychology, communication, reason, and the Enlightenment project.

Some of their insights the framework endorses:

**1. Instrumental reason is dangerous when unchecked.** Pure means-ends rationality, applied to everything, can produce monstrous outcomes — efficient extermination camps, optimized surveillance states, maximized quarterly profits at the expense of everything else. Reason must be accompanied by values it does not itself generate. The framework agrees (Chapter 2 explicitly distinguishes epistemic rationality from moral foundations).

**2. Mass culture can produce conformity and passivity.** When cultural production is dominated by a small number of corporations optimizing for engagement, the result can be cultural impoverishment. The framework's treatment of the digital environment (Chapter 36) addresses related concerns.

**3. Language shapes thought.** How we talk about things affects how we think about them. Ideological framings embedded in language influence how we reason. Critical analysis of language is a legitimate tool, even if it is sometimes overused.

**4. Communicative rationality matters (Habermas).** The conditions under which genuine dialogue can occur — equality of speakers, absence of coercion, mutual recognition — are morally and politically important. The framework's treatment of the epistemic commons (Chapter 21) and democratic deliberation (Chapter 20) reflects related concerns.

Contemporary critical traditions (critical race theory, feminist critical theory, postcolonial theory, queer theory) have extended these insights to specific forms of oppression. The framework engages with these as it does with classical Marxism — taking the insights seriously, questioning the specific claims where they seem overstated, and integrating what works with the broader framework.

The most valuable contributions of contemporary critical traditions:

- **Attention to specific historical and structural injustices** that liberal frameworks sometimes overlook.
- **Centering the perspectives of the marginalized**, whose experiences provide evidence that dominant groups often lack.
- **Analysis of how neutral-sounding institutions can produce unequal outcomes**, even in the absence of explicit discrimination.
- **Recognition that formal equality is insufficient** if substantive inequalities persist.
- **Critique of the universalist pretenses of particular traditions**, reminding us that all frameworks are situated.

The framework absorbs these insights while resisting some of the more radical claims of contemporary critical theory:

- **We do not accept** that all social categories are purely constructed with no basis in reality.
- **We do not accept** that power analysis replaces all other forms of moral analysis.
- **We do not accept** that truth is purely a function of power (this would self-refute like the relativism we rejected in Chapter 40).
- **We do not accept** that liberal institutions are fundamentally unreformable and must be replaced.
- **We do not accept** that rigorous reasoning is itself a form of oppression.

The framework is not post-modern, not purely structural, and not anti-liberal. It is liberal in the broad sense (committed to individual rights, democratic institutions, open discourse, and the possibility of reform) while being informed by the critical insights about how these institutions can produce unjust outcomes when left unchecked.

## What the Framework Owes the Critical Traditions

Putting the engagement together, the framework owes specific debts to Marxism and critical theory:

**1. The analysis of structural injustice** — the recognition that bad outcomes can be produced by systems rather than by individual malice, and that fixing them requires structural change rather than moral exhortation.

**2. The concept of ideology** — the awareness that dominant ideas can serve dominant interests while appearing neutral. The framework's epistemic humility (Chapter 2) has Marxist ancestry.

**3. Attention to exploitation and power asymmetries** — even when we narrow the definition of exploitation, the underlying concern is legitimate and important.

**4. The insight that material conditions shape consciousness** — what people think is not independent of how they live, and serious moral philosophy must account for this.

**5. Analysis of alienation and meaningful work** — the framework's treatment of labor and flourishing draws on this.

**6. The importance of specific historical injustices** — slavery, colonialism, patriarchy, discrimination. These shaped current conditions and cannot be dismissed as "past" issues.

**7. The reminder that theory should serve practice** — moral philosophy divorced from how people actually live is impoverished.

**8. The critique of liberal self-satisfaction** — liberal societies have real achievements but also real failures, and the critical tradition keeps the failures visible.

These are substantial debts. The framework is not "anti-Marxist" in the sense of dismissing everything Marxists said. It is selectively engaged, agreeing with some Marxist insights, modifying others, and rejecting some specific claims.

## The Practical Upshot

What does this chapter imply for how the framework actually operates?

**1. Economic and political analysis should include structural and power considerations.** Pure individual-choice analysis misses too much. When analyzing specific situations, ask: who has power here? How is it exercised? Who benefits? Who bears costs? These questions should be routine, not optional.

**2. Exploitation, even narrowly defined, is a real category and warrants attention.** Workers facing monopsonistic employers, debtors facing usurious lenders, citizens facing captured regulators — these are situations where power asymmetries can produce unjust outcomes even in "voluntary" transactions.

**3. Structural reform is legitimate and sometimes necessary.** Not everything can be fixed by individual moral choices or by small adjustments within existing structures. Some problems require institutional change. The framework's policy analysis (Chapter 24) supports this.

**4. Beware of ideology, including your own.** Your moral and political views are partly shaped by your interests and position. Take this seriously. Engage with critics whose perspectives differ from yours. Notice when your conclusions conveniently align with your interests.

**5. Don't dismiss critiques that come from unfashionable sources.** Marxists have real insights even when their political projects have failed. Critical theorists have real insights even when their rhetoric is sometimes excessive. Engagement with hostile critics is often more valuable than engagement with friendly ones.

**6. Reform, not revolution.** The framework commits to gradual, error-correcting change rather than radical replacement. This commits us to patience and to working within existing institutions, while also insisting that the institutions must change when they are producing unjust outcomes.

## Summary

1. **Marxism and critical theory** have been dismissed too easily by mainstream moral philosophy. The traditions contain real insights.

2. **Marx's core claims** include exploitation, alienation, ideology, historical materialism, class struggle, and human emancipation. Some are valuable; some are problematic.

3. **The framework agrees** on structural analysis, ideology critique, the reality of exploitation (narrowly defined), the importance of alienation, material conditions, power analysis, and practice-oriented theory.

4. **The framework disagrees** on the labor theory of value (rejected), historical predictions (mostly failed), the scope of the critique of morality (too broad), revolution as solution (bad track record), and class as the primary category (not supported by evidence).

5. **The Frankfurt School and contemporary critical theory** extended Marx's insights in valuable directions while adding specific critiques of instrumental reason, mass culture, language, and specific forms of oppression.

6. **The framework absorbs** attention to structural injustice, ideology, exploitation, material conditions, historical injustices, and the limits of liberal self-satisfaction.

7. **The framework rejects** radical social constructionism, the reduction of truth to power, wholesale anti-liberalism, and the rejection of rigorous reasoning as oppression.

8. **The practical upshot**: include structural and power analysis in moral reasoning, take critique seriously including from unfashionable sources, commit to reform rather than revolution, beware of your own ideological biases.
