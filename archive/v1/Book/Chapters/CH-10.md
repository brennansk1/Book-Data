# Chapter 10: The Crisis Override — When to Break the Rules

---

> *"Every exception to a rule is a potential loophole; and every loophole, sooner or later, is exploited."*
> — Anonymous, but uncomfortably close to the truth.

---

## The Hardest Chapter

This is the chapter in which the framework is most vulnerable.

Chapter 9 made the case for Mode A — the rule-following default. Rules are bias-resistant, cognitively cheap, predictability-producing, and wisdom-encoding. But rules fail. They can be wrong, they can conflict, and they can produce outcomes so catastrophic that following them becomes a moral horror. "Don't lie" to the Nazi at your door is not the right move if you are hiding refugees in your basement.

So we need an override. Mode B is that override: explicit consequentialist calculation, invoked only when Mode A fails catastrophically.

Here is the problem: **every consequentialist thinks their case is the special exception.** The businessman who cheats on his taxes "just this once." The spouse who tells a "kind lie." The politician who breaks a promise because "the situation changed." Each invokes Mode B. Each feels justified. Each may be morally wrong in ways they cannot see because the elephant is steering the rider.

Allow Mode B without constraint, and rules erode through a sequence of motivated particular judgments. Forbid it entirely, and you get moral catastrophes: rigid rule-following in cases where the rules produce atrocity.

We need an override that works only when truly needed. This chapter attempts to design it.

---

## What Mode B Is For

Let us be precise about what we are trying to build.

Mode B is not "use consequentialism whenever you think the stakes are high." That is the slippery slope. Mode B is specifically for cases where:

1. **Following the rule would produce catastrophic, irreversible harm.** Not "bad consequences" in the ordinary sense. Not "worse than breaking the rule." *Catastrophic* — meaning the kind of harm that a reasonable observer would agree is of a qualitatively different order from the harm the rule was designed to prevent.

2. **The catastrophic harm is clearly attributable to following the rule, not merely coincident with it.** You cannot invoke Mode B because following the rule is associated with bad consequences; you must invoke it because the rule-following itself produces the consequences.

3. **No other option preserves both the rule and the avoidance of catastrophe.** Mode B is a last resort, not a first option. If you can find a way to honor the rule while avoiding the catastrophe, you must do that.

4. **You can articulate the override to a skeptical third party who does not share your interests.** This is the anti-gaming mechanism, and we develop it at length below.

5. **Your self-interest is not in play.** Or if it is in play, you have additional burden of demonstrating that you would still invoke the override if the self-interest were reversed.

Most claimed cases of "the rule shouldn't apply here" fail at least one of these conditions. The businessman cheating on his taxes fails 1, 2, 4, and 5. The spouse telling the kind lie fails 1, 3, and 4. These are not Mode B invocations. They are rationalizations dressed in Mode B language.

True Mode B invocations are rare: the Nazi at the door, refusing to return a murder weapon to its owner (Plato's original example), disobeying an order that would cause a massacre, lying to a kidnapper. These are characterized by features that make the override obvious to any reasonable person — not just to the person performing it.

## The Five Conditions in Detail

Let us examine each of the five conditions, because the whole Mode B architecture depends on them being understood precisely.

### Condition 1: Catastrophic, Irreversible Harm

Not every bad consequence triggers Mode B. Mode A already accounts for ordinary bad consequences — the rule "don't lie" was selected because, on average, lying produces bad consequences. You don't need Mode B to reject lying. You need Mode B only when following the rule would produce harm qualitatively worse than the ordinary bad consequences the rule was designed to prevent.

What counts as "catastrophic"? Death, permanent disability, massive suffering, irreversible destruction. Lying to spare someone's feelings does not qualify. Lying to prevent a genocide does.

The threshold is not "more bad than good" — that is ordinary consequentialism. The threshold is "categorically different order of harm," a recognition that the rule's normal domain does not include this case. Irreversibility matters too: normal situations allow for correction of mistakes over time, but irreversible harm — death, destruction of the irreplaceable — forecloses the ordinary correction mechanism.

### Condition 2: Attributable to Rule-Following

The second condition is subtle but essential. Mode B applies only when the catastrophic harm is caused *by following the rule*, not merely when it is happening *while* the rule is being followed.

Example: A doctor is following the rule "don't betray patient confidences." She learns that her patient is planning to harm someone. If she says nothing, the harm occurs. *This is Mode B territory* — the catastrophic harm is directly caused by her following the rule, and breaking it (warning the victim) would prevent the harm.

Contrast: A doctor is following the rule "don't betray patient confidences." She also learns, through unrelated channels, that there will be a famine next year. *This is not Mode B territory* — the famine is not caused by her following the confidence rule. Whatever her obligation to prevent the famine, it does not involve overriding the confidence rule, because the rule is not the relevant cause.

This prevents a specific kind of slippery-slope reasoning: "bad things are happening, and I'm following rules, so maybe I should break the rules to address the bad things." No. The rules you break must be causally connected to the harm you are trying to prevent.

### Condition 3: Last Resort

Mode B is the final option, not the first. Before invoking it, you must exhaust the alternatives that preserve the rule.

Can you refuse to answer the Nazi at the door without lying? (Many traditional moral theorists argued that silence is permissible even when speech would require lying.) Can you find a different way to save the child without stealing? Can you warn the potential victim without breaking confidentiality by raising general concerns that trigger their vigilance? Can you report the impending massacre without disobeying your superior, by going to a different authority?

These alternatives are often available. The morally serious person looks for them before concluding that the rule must break. Mode B is reserved for cases where the search has been sincere and thorough, and no rule-preserving path exists.

This is a significant constraint in practice. Most situations that feel like "I must break this rule" dissolve on inspection into "I could have preserved the rule by being more creative or more patient." The requirement to exhaust alternatives is how we filter out the false positives.

### Condition 4: Articulable to a Skeptical Third Party

This is the most important condition, and the most powerful anti-gaming mechanism in the system.

When you invoke Mode B, imagine you must justify your decision to someone who:

- Does not share your interests in the outcome
- Does not grant you the benefit of the doubt
- Is willing to scrutinize your reasoning for self-serving bias
- Will judge you by the argument you can *articulate*, not by the one you *feel*

This is the **public reasoning test**. Can you explain, in words, why following the rule would have produced catastrophic irreversible harm, why that harm was attributable to the rule, why no alternative existed, and why your self-interest did not influence the calculation? If you can — if the argument would survive critical scrutiny by an opponent — then the override is defensible. If you cannot — if your justification collapses the moment someone skeptical pushes on it — then you are not invoking Mode B; you are rationalizing.

This test blocks almost all false positives. The businessman cannot publicly justify his tax cheating. The politician cannot publicly justify breaking the promise. The spouse cannot publicly justify the "kind lie."

The test works because motivated reasoning is much easier in private than in public. Your elephant can supply convenient answers in your own head. It cannot supply them against a skeptical interlocutor actively looking for holes. The *articulation requirement* forces the elephant to work in public, where its biases are visible.

If you cannot explain why you broke the rule, you did not have Mode B justification for breaking it. "It felt like the right thing to do" is not a Mode B argument.

### Condition 5: Self-Interest Absent or Corrected For

The final condition is the most demanding. If your own interests are served by breaking the rule, you should be extremely skeptical of any Mode B argument you construct, because your elephant is almost certainly steering.

The test: *would you invoke this override if the self-interest were reversed?* Would you break the rule if doing so cost you personally, rather than benefited you? Would you defend the same argument if you were the one who would lose by it?

If the answer is no — if your willingness to break the rule tracks suspiciously with your interests — then you are not doing Mode B. You are doing self-serving reasoning in Mode B clothing.

If the answer is yes — if you would genuinely invoke the override even against your own interests — then you have passed the hardest test. The override may still be wrong on other grounds, but it is at least not a product of simple self-interest.

This condition does not forbid Mode B in cases where you have any personal stake. Most moral situations involve some personal stake; the condition would be unworkable otherwise. What it requires is that you *recognize* the stake, *account* for it in your reasoning, and *verify* that your conclusion would survive its reversal. This is a discipline, and like all disciplines, it is difficult. It is also essential.

## The Gating Protocol: Procedural Safeguards Against the Override Reflex

The five conditions above define *what justifies* a Mode B override. But they leave open a procedural question that any serious opponent will press: **Who decides when the conditions are met?** If the agent evaluates their own situation against the conditions, the fox guards the henhouse — motivated reasoning colors the assessment before the conditions are even consulted. If someone else decides, you face a regress: who watches the watcher?

This is not a hypothetical objection. It is the most dangerous structural vulnerability in any dual-process system. Every consequentialist who has ever rationalized a bad action believed, at the moment of action, that their case met the threshold. The conditions alone are necessary but not sufficient. We need a **procedural architecture** — a gating protocol — that makes it harder for the override reflex to hijack the evaluation process.

### The Three Gates

Before the five conditions are even consulted, three procedural gates must be passed. These are not additional moral tests — they are *epistemic checkpoints* that reduce the probability that the agent is evaluating the situation under the influence of exactly the biases the override is designed to protect against.

**Gate 1: Bright-Line Trigger Check.** Mode B consideration is activated *only* when one of three categories of situation is present:

- **(a) Imminent large-scale loss of life.** Not hypothetical future risk, not probable downstream harm, but identifiable people facing death or severe irreversible harm within a timeframe that forecloses normal institutional response.

- **(b) Irreversible systemic damage.** Environmental collapse, destruction of irreplaceable institutions, elimination of future option-value on a scale that cannot be undone. The key word is *irreversible* — if the harm can be corrected through normal error-correction channels (elections, legal reform, market adjustment), Mode B is not yet warranted.

- **(c) Institutional capture that disables normal error-correction.** When the institutions designed to correct errors have themselves been captured — when reporting the problem through channels is impossible because the channels are the problem — Mode B consideration becomes available. This is the whistleblower's gate: the system's own correction mechanisms have been compromised.

These are **necessary conditions for even considering the switch**, not sufficient conditions for making it. An agent who cannot identify which bright-line trigger applies has not entered Mode B territory. They may be facing a genuine moral dilemma, but it is a Mode A dilemma — to be resolved within the rule system, not by overriding it.

The bright-line triggers serve a specific cognitive function: they force the agent to *categorize* the situation before *evaluating* it. This interrupts the emotional urgency that makes motivated reasoning feel like genuine moral insight. "This feels like a crisis" is not a bright-line trigger. "Identifiable people will die if I follow this rule" is.

**Gate 2: Mandatory Deliberation Period.** Except in cases of imminent physical danger where delay itself would cause the catastrophic harm (Gate 1a with a tight timeline), Mode B activation requires a deliberation period before action:

- **Personal decisions:** minimum 24 hours.
- **Institutional decisions:** minimum proportional to the scope of the override (days to weeks).
- **Immediate physical danger:** exempt from the delay, but subject to enhanced post-hoc review (Gate 3).

The deliberation period exists because of a well-documented cognitive phenomenon: emotional urgency mimics genuine crisis. Kahneman's System 1 generates a feeling of *this must be decided now* that is often false. The 24-hour rule forces the agent to discover whether the urgency survives a night's sleep, a conversation with someone uninvolved, and the passage of the acute emotional state. Many "obvious" Mode B cases dissolve under this test — the situation that seemed to demand immediate rule-breaking turns out to have rule-preserving alternatives that were invisible under emotional pressure.

This is not a bureaucratic procedural requirement. It is a *debiasing mechanism*. The research on temporal discounting, hot-cold empathy gaps, and the planning fallacy all converge on the same finding: decisions made under emotional urgency are systematically biased toward action, toward overconfidence, and toward underweighting the costs of deviation. The deliberation period is the system's equivalent of "sleep on it" — and like "sleep on it," it catches an extraordinary number of errors.

**Gate 3: Mandatory Post-Hoc Review.** Every Mode B activation — whether it turns out to have been correct or incorrect — must be documented and subjected to retrospective analysis. The documentation includes:

- The bright-line trigger that was identified
- The five conditions as assessed at the time of decision
- The action taken
- The outcome
- A retrospective assessment: with the benefit of hindsight, was the override warranted?

This serves two functions. First, it creates accountability. An agent who knows their override will be reviewed is less likely to invoke Mode B casually. Second, and more importantly, it creates *pattern data*. If an agent — or an institution — consistently invokes Mode B, this pattern is itself diagnostic. Consistent override invocation is evidence of one of two things: either the agent faces genuinely exceptional circumstances (possible but unlikely to persist), or the agent has developed a habit of rationalized rule-breaking that the five conditions alone are not catching. The pattern becomes data for the Position Registry: it may indicate that a Mode A rule needs revision (if the overrides keep being warranted) or that the agent's Mode B judgment needs recalibration (if they keep being unwarranted).

Eliezer Yudkowsky's concept of the "Reasonable Reviewer" from AI alignment research provides the right frame here. The question is not "would I approve of this override?" — that is the question the biased agent is already asking. The question is: "Would a reasonable reviewer — someone intelligent, well-informed, and predisposed to rule-following — agree that this override was warranted given what was known at the time?" If you cannot predict that a reasonable reviewer would approve, you have not met the bar.

### Why Mode A Creates the Conditions for Mode B: The Concept of Slack

Zvi Mowshowitz introduced a concept essential to understanding why the dual-process architecture works: **slack** — the capacity to deviate from locally optimal behavior in order to maintain strategic flexibility.

An agent with no slack — fully optimized, operating at maximum efficiency, with no surplus resources of time, attention, or energy — cannot switch modes. They are locked into whatever behavior pattern currently maximizes their local metric. They cannot step back, deliberate, consider whether the rules still serve their purpose, or invoke an override. They are, in the language of Chapter 6, fully captured by Moloch.

Mode A rules *create slack* by preventing constant consequentialist recalculation. The agent who follows rules does not spend every moment optimizing; they spend most moments on autopilot, executing pre-committed responses. This conserves cognitive resources. And those conserved resources are precisely what makes Mode B possible: when a genuine crisis arises, the agent has the attentional surplus to recognize it, the deliberative capacity to evaluate it, and the emotional stability to respond to it without panic.

A system without Mode A would have no slack and therefore no capacity for Mode B. A system without Mode B would have slack but no mechanism to use it. The dual-process architecture requires both: the rule-following default that creates cognitive and emotional slack, and the override mechanism that deploys that slack when the situation genuinely demands it.

This is why the Gating Protocol's mandatory deliberation period is not merely a procedural hurdle — it is a *slack-creating mechanism*. It forces the agent to step out of the urgency, recover the cognitive surplus that the crisis consumed, and re-engage from a position of deliberative capacity rather than reactive depletion.

### When to Trust Yourself vs. Defer to Consensus

The Gating Protocol raises a deeper question: when is it rational to trust your own judgment over the default rules and the conventional wisdom that supports them?

Yudkowsky's *Inadequate Equilibria* (2017) provides useful criteria. You should trust your own deviation from consensus only when you can identify the *specific mechanism* producing the inadequacy — the specific coordination failure, incentive misalignment, or information asymmetry that makes the default response wrong in this case. "I think I know better" is not sufficient. "I can identify the specific reason the system is producing a bad answer here, and that reason is verifiable by others" is the bar.

This directly supports Mode B logic. You should deviate from rules (including the meta-rule "defer to established norms") only when you can identify the specific failure mode that makes the default response catastrophic — and you can articulate that failure mode to someone who starts skeptical. The Gating Protocol ensures this identification happens before action, not after.

## Historical Test Cases

Let us test the framework against history. Would these conditions, applied honestly, have produced the right answers in the hard cases?

### Case 1: Hiding Refugees from Nazis

Setup: You are sheltering Jewish refugees during WWII. A Nazi officer asks if anyone is hiding in your house.

Applying the conditions:

1. **Catastrophic harm from rule-following?** Yes. Telling the truth would result in the deaths of innocent people. This is as clear a case of catastrophic, irreversible harm as exists.

2. **Attributable to the rule?** Yes. The killing is directly caused by your truthful answer. No truthful answer, no killing (in this interaction).

3. **Last resort?** Silence would fail (the Nazi will search). Evasion might fail. Lying may be the only option that saves the refugees.

4. **Articulable to a skeptical observer?** Yes. Any reasonable person, including skeptical ones, would immediately recognize the override as justified. "I lied to save innocent lives from murderers" is a public argument that survives all scrutiny.

5. **Self-interest absent?** The sheltering itself is at great personal risk. Self-interest points *against* lying (because being caught means punishment), not in favor of it. The Mode B invocation is not explained by self-interest.

Verdict: Clear Mode B case. Lie.

### Case 2: The Ticking Time Bomb (Torture)

Setup: You have captured a terrorist who knows the location of a bomb that will kill many. Should you torture him to extract the information?

Applying the conditions:

1. **Catastrophic harm from rule-following?** Only if the bomb scenario is real, the suspect actually knows, and torture would actually produce reliable information. These are strong empirical claims.

2. **Attributable to the rule?** Only if torture actually would have prevented the harm. Empirically, torture is a poor information-extraction technique — it produces false confessions more reliably than true information. The Senate Intelligence Committee's 2014 study of the CIA's post-9/11 interrogation program — the most comprehensive empirical assessment of torture's effectiveness ever conducted — concluded that "the CIA's use of its enhanced interrogation techniques was not an effective means of acquiring intelligence." The 6,700-page report found that the CIA systematically overstated the value of information obtained through coercion and that actionable intelligence was obtained through standard interrogation, not torture.

3. **Last resort?** This requires that no other method (offers, deception, patient interrogation, intelligence work) could extract the information in time. Almost always, this condition fails.

4. **Articulable to a skeptical observer?** Here the case collapses. A skeptical observer will immediately challenge: how do you know the bomb is real? How do you know the suspect knows? How do you know torture will work? How do you know other methods would fail? The answer to all of these is usually "I don't, I'm guessing, and the guess is colored by my emotional state." The articulation fails.

5. **Self-interest absent?** The torturer's reputation, their career, their sense of themselves as a hero-in-crisis — all of these create motivated reasoning toward torture. The self-interest is not absent.

Verdict: Not a Mode B case. The public articulation test fails. The torture debate, under this framework, collapses: the "ticking time bomb" cannot satisfy the Mode B conditions because the empirical premises cannot be verified in real time and the motivated reasoning is pervasive. **Don't torture.**

This is a striking result, because the ticking time bomb is the single most cited case for consequentialist override. The Mode B framework, correctly applied, refuses it. This is not an accident. The conditions were designed to filter out exactly this kind of case — where high stakes and urgent emotion combine to make people think their rule-violation is justified, when in fact it is rationalized.

### Case 3: Civil Disobedience

Setup: A just cause (civil rights, anti-apartheid, anti-slavery abolitionism) requires breaking unjust laws. Does Mode B justify this?

Applying the conditions:

1. **Catastrophic harm from rule-following?** Yes, if the unjust law is itself producing massive suffering (Jim Crow, apartheid). Following the law means participating in the catastrophe.

2. **Attributable?** Yes. The law is the direct mechanism of the catastrophe.

3. **Last resort?** Usually not at the individual level — other paths (political organizing, legal challenges, public advocacy) often exist. But civil disobedience is often a *supplement* to these other paths, not a replacement, and its role is to draw attention to the injustice and force political response.

4. **Articulable to a skeptical observer?** Yes, and famously so. Martin Luther King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail" is a model of Mode B articulation — explaining exactly why the law must be broken, what the alternatives were, and why the injustice justifies the override. The argument survives critical scrutiny.

5. **Self-interest absent?** Civil disobedience is typically extremely costly to the disobeyer — jail, beatings, loss of career. Self-interest points against it, not toward it.

Verdict: Mode B case. Civil disobedience is justified when the conditions are met (as in the civil rights movement), and it is powerfully legitimized by meeting the public articulation test in ways that ordinary rule-breaking cannot.

### Case 4: Whistleblowing

Setup: You discover that your employer is committing serious fraud that harms many people. Your employment contract requires confidentiality. Should you break it?

Applying the conditions:

1. **Catastrophic harm?** Depends on the fraud's scale. Fraud that harms millions of people or poses severe risks certainly qualifies. Minor fraud probably does not.

2. **Attributable?** Yes — your continued silence enables the continuation of the fraud.

3. **Last resort?** Have you tried internal channels? Reported to regulators without breaching confidentiality? Exhausted options that preserve the contract? If not, you have not yet met this condition.

4. **Articulable?** Usually yes, if the fraud is real and serious. The whistleblower can publicly justify the breach by reference to the harm being prevented.

5. **Self-interest absent?** Often — whistleblowers typically suffer enormous personal costs. But if whistleblowing comes with a reward (legal settlements, media fame), the self-interest condition requires more careful scrutiny.

Verdict: Mode B case, if the conditions are met. The framework supports whistleblowing when the fraud is serious, internal channels are exhausted, and public articulation is possible.

## The Skeptical Third Party, Formalized

The fourth condition — articulability to a skeptical third party — deserves further development, because it is doing most of the work in this framework.

The idea is not merely that you should be able to explain your reasoning. It is that your reasoning must survive a specific kind of scrutiny — the scrutiny of someone who is actively looking for flaws, who does not share your interests, and who is willing to conclude that you are wrong.

We can formalize this as a two-person imagined dialogue:

**You**: "I am going to break rule X because of situation Y."

**Skeptical Observer**: "Before you do that, let me ask some questions. First, what exactly is situation Y, and how confident are you in your description of it?"

**You**: "[Description]"

**Skeptical Observer**: "Your description assumes several empirical claims. How do you know those are true? Could you be wrong about any of them?"

**You**: "[Response]"

**Skeptical Observer**: "Second, you say following rule X would produce harm. How much harm? Compared to what the rule normally produces when followed? Compared to what breaking the rule might produce in unintended consequences?"

**You**: "[Response]"

**Skeptical Observer**: "Third, have you considered alternatives that preserve the rule? Did you spend at least as much time looking for those as you spent justifying the override?"

**You**: "[Response]"

**Skeptical Observer**: "Fourth, what is your self-interest in this situation? How might it be influencing your reasoning? If you had the opposite interest, would you still conclude the override is justified?"

**You**: "[Response]"

**Skeptical Observer**: "Fifth, are you willing to be publicly identified as the person who made this decision, and to defend it to a broad audience including people who disagree with you?"

If, after this dialogue, you can still answer "yes, the override is justified" — and if your answers would convince not yourself but the skeptical observer — then you have a defensible Mode B case. If any of your answers weakens under the skeptical probing, then your case is not defensible, and you should follow the rule.

This dialogue should be practiced. Seriously. The goal is not to produce a particular answer; the goal is to train yourself to *genuinely* imagine the skeptical observer and to let them push back hard. Most people, when they first try this, find that their Mode B invocations collapse quickly. This is the system working — most invocations *should* collapse, because most of them are rationalizations.

## The Public Justification Standard

The skeptical-observer test has a stronger cousin: the **public justification standard**. This is the requirement that a Mode B invocation must be one you are willing to publicly defend, to actual humans who disagree with you, not just imagined skeptical observers.

There is a reason this matters. Motivated reasoning works best in private. Under public pressure, against real interlocutors, many rationalizations that felt solid in your head collapse. The person who was certain they had to break the rule often discovers, in the course of explaining it, that their reasoning does not hold up.

Public justification also introduces accountability. If you invoke Mode B and are wrong, the public justification means that others can call you out. It creates consequences for bad Mode B invocations — reputational consequences, at minimum, and sometimes legal or social ones. This changes the incentives. The person who knows they will have to publicly defend their choice is less likely to make a bad choice.

Not every Mode B invocation can be public *immediately*. Hiding refugees, whistleblowing before you're ready, lying to a kidnapper — these sometimes require initial secrecy. But they should be ones you are willing to defend publicly *after the fact*, once the danger has passed. If you would be ashamed to explain what you did in retrospect, your Mode B invocation was not justified.

The parallel to Kant's categorical imperative is deliberate. Kant asked: can you universalize the maxim of your action? We ask something weaker but more practical: can you *publicly defend* the override as an appropriate response to this specific case? The public defense does not need to generalize to all similar cases (that would be too strong), but it needs to survive genuine scrutiny by others.

## The Residual Vulnerability

Even with all these conditions, the Mode B architecture remains the framework's most vulnerable position.

The fundamental problem is that human psychology is adversarial to this architecture. We are *designed* (by evolution) to rationalize self-serving choices as moral exceptions. We are *rewarded* (by social dynamics) for being clever enough to justify our preferred actions. We are *blinded* (by motivated reasoning) to our own biases in exactly the cases where the biases are strongest.

The five conditions raise the bar substantially. They filter out most bad invocations. But they cannot eliminate the risk entirely, because any rule-set can be gamed by sufficiently motivated reasoners. Someone determined to justify a bad override will construct answers to all five conditions, and those answers may be wrong in ways that person cannot see.

What protects the system from this vulnerability?

1. **The high bar**: The five conditions are demanding. Many bad invocations fail them, and those that survive are much smaller in number than would survive without them.

2. **The public test**: Forcing articulation to skeptical observers blocks most motivated reasoning, because motivated reasoning is harder in public than in private.

3. **The default**: Mode A is the default, and shifting out of it requires positive justification. Inertia favors rule-following.

4. **Institutional support**: A culture that takes rules seriously, that scrutinizes claimed overrides, and that punishes bad invocations, reinforces the individual discipline required for the system to work.

5. **Honesty about the limit**: We are explicit that the system can fail. This is the Registry's role — it tracks our knowledge of weaknesses and updates as we learn more.

6. **The asymmetry of errors**: False negatives (failing to invoke Mode B when we should have) are usually less catastrophic than false positives (invoking Mode B when we should not have). If you are uncertain, err on the side of the rule. The rule was designed for the normal case; the deviation is the exceptional case. When in doubt, default.

None of these protections are perfect. The Mode A/B architecture remains vulnerable, and we acknowledge it. What the architecture provides is the *best available* compromise between the failure modes of pure rule-following and pure calculation — not a perfect solution, but one that is less bad than the alternatives.

## A Note on Catastrophic Cases and Moral Luck

One more wrinkle deserves mention. The framework so far has focused on the *decision*: when is it right to invoke Mode B? But there is a related question about *moral evaluation after the fact*: how do we judge someone who invoked Mode B and turned out to be wrong?

Consider: a person in occupied France hides refugees and lies to the Nazis about it, but the lies are discovered, everyone dies, and the lies accomplished nothing except prolonging the danger briefly. Was their Mode B invocation correct?

Our answer: yes, if they satisfied the five conditions at the moment of decision. Moral evaluation tracks the quality of the decision given the information available, not the outcomes that in fact resulted. This is not moral relativism — there are still right and wrong answers. It is recognition that human agents cannot be held responsible for consequences they could not have predicted.

This has an important corollary. When we evaluate others' Mode B invocations, we should ask: did they satisfy the conditions *given what they knew at the time*? Not: did everything work out? The latter question introduces a distortion called outcome bias (Chapter 2) — judging decisions by results rather than by the quality of the reasoning that produced them.

Similarly, we should not excuse bad Mode B invocations just because they happened to produce good outcomes. The politician who broke a promise and was lucky enough to avoid the usual bad consequences still invoked Mode B badly; the fact that it worked out is moral luck, not vindication.

## Summary: The Override, Stated Clearly

**Before the five conditions are consulted, three procedural gates must be passed:**

1. **Bright-line trigger**: One of three categories must be identifiable — imminent large-scale loss of life, irreversible systemic damage, or institutional capture disabling error-correction. If no trigger is present, Mode B is not available.

2. **Deliberation period**: Minimum 24 hours for personal decisions, longer for institutional ones. Exempt only when delay itself causes the catastrophic harm.

3. **Post-hoc review commitment**: The agent commits in advance to documenting the override and subjecting it to retrospective analysis.

**Mode B may be invoked only when all five conditions are met:**

1. **Catastrophic harm**: Following the rule would produce a qualitatively worse outcome than the rule was designed to prevent, of an order magnitudes beyond ordinary bad consequences.

2. **Attributable to rule**: The catastrophic harm is directly caused by rule-following, not merely coincident with it.

3. **Last resort**: No alternative exists that preserves both the rule and the avoidance of catastrophe.

4. **Articulable publicly**: You can explain the override to a skeptical third party — a Reasonable Reviewer predisposed to rule-following — in terms that survive critical scrutiny, without relying on private intuitions or motivated reasoning.

5. **Self-interest corrected**: Your decision would survive reversal of self-interest; you would invoke the override even if it cost you.

**Defaults and asymmetries:**

- Mode A is the default. Mode B requires positive justification.
- When in doubt, follow the rule. The burden of proof is on the override.
- Errors of rule-following are usually less catastrophic than errors of false override, so asymmetric caution favors the rule.

**Accountability:**

- Every Mode B invocation is documented and reviewed retrospectively.
- Patterns of repeated invocation are themselves diagnostic data.
- You must be willing to publicly defend the override after the fact, even if it had to be private at the time.
- Your reasoning should be the kind that improves your future judgment, not the kind that justifies your current convenience.
- Bad Mode B invocations are a moral failure, even if the outcomes happened to be good.

This is the override. It is demanding. It is unreliable. It is the best we can do.

## Looking Forward

This chapter closes the argument for the dual-process architecture: Mode A as the default, Mode B as the carefully-constrained override. Chapters 9 and 10 together make the case for why this architecture handles the failure modes of both pure rule-following and pure calculation better than either alternative.

Chapter 11 addresses a different question: *what character traits does this architecture require of the agent running it?* The answer is the virtues — specific habits of thought and action that make both Mode A and Mode B work. The twelve virtues of rationality that the Framework introduced now have a clear function: they are the firmware that the dual-process architecture runs on.

Chapter 12 addresses how this character is built — the process of moral development from instinct to reflection to habit, drawing on developmental psychology and the traditions of moral formation.

With Book III complete, we will have the full moral architecture: foundations (Book I), diagnosis (Book II), decision framework (Book III). Books IV through VII will apply it to specific domains — personal ethics, politics, economics, the frontier — and Book VIII will defend it against rival systems.
