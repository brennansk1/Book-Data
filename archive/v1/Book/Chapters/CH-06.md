# Chapter 6: Moloch — The God of Coordination Failure

## July 2, 1992

According to the accounts that survive, when John Cabot's crew reached the waters off Newfoundland in 1497, they reported a sea so thick with cod that the fish could be brought up in weighted baskets, no bait required. Whatever the exact truth of that story, the fishery it describes was real, and it endured for five centuries. Basque, Portuguese, French, and English boats worked the Grand Banks for generations. Entire towns on Newfoundland's coast were built on it, fed by it, buried their dead because of the boats it swallowed, and never once, in five hundred years, ran out of cod. Then, in the space of about thirty years, it ended.

The change began with a technology: the factory freezer trawler, a vessel that could catch, process, and freeze fish continuously, staying at sea for months instead of days. The first entered service in the 1950s, and by the 1960s distant-water fleets from the Soviet Union, Spain, Portugal, and West Germany were working the Grand Banks around the clock. Catches spiked, then began to fall — not because demand had dropped, but because the fish were being taken out faster than they could replace themselves. By 1977, alarmed at what foreign fleets were doing to a resource on its doorstep, Canada declared a two-hundred-mile Exclusive Economic Zone, pushing most of the foreign boats out and, for a few years, giving the northern cod stock room to recover.

What happened next is the part of the story that matters. Canada did not leave the fishery alone. It subsidized its own fleet to expand into the space the foreign trawlers had vacated, equipping Canadian boats with the same efficient technology that had done the damage in the first place. Government scientists set annual quotas using population models that, reconstructed after the fact, had been overestimating how much cod remained for years — and every autumn, fishing companies and the coastal communities that depended on them argued for quotas at the top of what the models could justify, because a smaller quota meant real hardship this year in exchange for a healthier stock that someone else, later, might be the one to benefit from. By the late 1980s, scientists inside Canada's Department of Fisheries and Oceans were raising a chilling alarm: catch rates were holding steady not because the population was healthy, but because sonar and factory processing had gotten so good that the fleet could still find and process the last fish as efficiently as it had once found the first. The stock could be nearly gone and the numbers would not show it until the moment it collapsed outright.

No minister wanted to be the one who shut down a fishery that supported, directly or indirectly, something like one job in seven in Newfoundland — not without certainty, and by the time certainty arrived, it was too late to matter. On July 2, 1992, Fisheries Minister John Crosbie announced a moratorium on northern cod fishing. The spawning biomass, by the best reconstructions, had collapsed to roughly one percent of its levels from three decades earlier. Something like thirty thousand fishermen and plant workers lost their jobs in a single day — the largest mass layoff in Canadian history to that point. More than three decades later, the fishery has never come close to recovering; the moratorium, meant to be temporary, is in most areas still in effect.

No single captain sank the Grand Banks. Each one was doing something locally sensible: feeding a family, meeting a quota, keeping a boat afloat, keeping a town's only cannery open one more season. Multiply that sensible local decision by thousands of boats across three decades and the result is a five-hundred-year fishery reduced to wreckage in a single generation — a collapse nobody wanted, nobody voted for, and nobody, acting alone, could have prevented. This is not a story about greed overpowering virtue. It is a story about arithmetic. And the same arithmetic keeps producing the same kind of ruin, in different oceans and different currencies, no matter how decent the people running the numbers happen to be.

## The Logic That Traps Good People

Ask someone why the world contains so much unnecessary suffering, and you will usually get one of two answers.

The religious answer says: evil exists. There are bad people, bad choices, bad spirits — sometimes Satan, sometimes human depravity, sometimes the inherited stain of some ancestral fall. Suffering is the signature of wickedness, and reducing it is a matter of reducing wickedness. The cynical answer says: people are selfish, full stop. They pursue their own advantage, and the wreckage is what's left over. If people were only nicer, less greedy, more cooperative, most of the suffering would disappear.

Both answers are wrong, in a way that matters a great deal if we build a moral system on top of them. The religious answer fails because most suffering is not caused by bad people. It is caused by ordinary people — who love their children, pay their taxes, and follow the rules of their communities — making choices that are individually reasonable and collectively catastrophic. The trawler captain who takes his full quota is not a villain; the consumer who buys the cheapest fish is not a sadist. They are you and me, responding to the incentives in front of us. The cynical answer fails for a subtler reason: even a fleet staffed entirely by saints, each one sincerely wanting the cod to survive, produces the same collapse if the situation rewards taking your share today over trusting everyone else to hold back. Replace every captain with a monk and the biomass still hits one percent. The problem is not in the hearts of the fishermen. It is in the logic of the situation they share.

This book has a name for that logic, borrowed from Allen Ginsberg's 1955 poem "Howl," in which Moloch — the Canaanite god to whom parents once sacrificed their children — becomes a figure for the systems that demand the destruction of what we love in exchange for advantages none of us actually sought and none of us can unilaterally refuse. Moloch is not a person and not a conspiracy. Moloch is the name for what happens when individually rational choices combine to produce a collectively irrational result — when everyone playing their own hand correctly still adds up to a table nobody would have chosen. Moloch is the god of bad equilibria, and a very large share of the world's unnecessary suffering is his handiwork, not sin's.

## Bad Equilibria Can Be Stable

Game theory gives this intuition a precise shape through the idea of a **Nash equilibrium**: a situation in which no player can improve their own outcome by unilaterally changing strategy, given what everyone else is doing. It is a resting point — a place a system falls into and stays, because no single participant has a reason to move first.

The crucial fact, easy to state and easy to underestimate, is that Nash equilibria are not always good. A situation can be perfectly stable even when everyone trapped in it would be better off in some other arrangement, because no individual can reach that better arrangement by moving alone. The classic illustration is the **Prisoner's Dilemma**. Two suspects are interrogated separately, each choosing to stay silent or to confess. If both stay silent, both serve a light sentence; if both confess, both serve a heavy one; if one confesses and the other stays silent, the confessor goes free while the silent partner takes the harshest sentence of all. Individual rationality says: confess. Whatever your partner does, you do better by talking. Both prisoners reason this way. Both confess. The result is the heavy sentence for both — strictly worse for each than the outcome cooperation would have produced.

> *"Individual rationality leads to a worse outcome for both than is possible. Hence the dilemma."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

Rationality is not enough. Two agents, each maximizing only their own outcome, can be led by that very rationality into a result both would reject if they could simply choose together. The structure of the situation traps them, not any defect in their reasoning. Axelrod, whose tournaments in the late 1970s made the Prisoner's Dilemma the central experimental object of modern cooperation theory, states the reach of the pattern without exaggeration:

> *"Richardson's model of the arms race is based on an interaction which is essentially a Prisoner's Dilemma... Oligopolistic competition can also be modeled as a Prisoner's Dilemma... The ubiquitous problems of collective action to produce a collective good are analyzable as Prisoner's Dilemmas with many players.... In fact, many of the best-developed models of important political, social, and economic processes have the Prisoner's Dilemma as their foundation."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

> *"The iterated Prisoner's Dilemma has become the E. coli of social psychology."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

It is worth sitting with how wide that reach is: arms races, price wars, pollution, antibiotic overuse, the credential inflation that turns a bachelor's degree into a minimum requirement for jobs that never used to need one. In every case the structure is identical — individually rational behavior producing a collectively catastrophic outcome that persists because no actor can break out of it without absorbing the entire cost alone.

## The Tragedy of the Commons, Revisited

The many-player version of this same trap has its own name, coined by the biologist Garrett Hardin in 1968: the **Tragedy of the Commons**. Picture a pasture shared among a group of herders, each free to add cattle at no direct cost to himself. The grass is finite; below some threshold of total cattle the pasture regenerates indefinitely, above it the pasture is destroyed. From any single herder's vantage point, adding one more cow delivers the full benefit to him while spreading the cost across every herder on the commons — with ten herders sharing the pasture, each captures the whole benefit of an extra cow while bearing only a tenth of its cost. The arithmetic recommends adding the cow. Every herder does the same arithmetic. Every herder adds the cow. The pasture dies, and no one, by the standards of ordinary self-interest, did anything wrong.

This is not a parable invented to make a point; it is a description of what happened on the Grand Banks. The ocean was the commons. Each boat's additional day of fishing delivered its full benefit to that boat's owner while spreading the cost — a slightly smaller stock for everyone, a slightly harder season next year — across every other boat on the water and every fisherman not yet born. Hardin's original formulation was somewhat too pessimistic: Chapter 8 will show, through Elinor Ostrom's research into community-managed resources, that commons are not doomed by definition, and groups have governed shared resources successfully for centuries without privatizing them or handing them to a central authority. But the underlying mechanism holds wherever concentrated benefits meet distributed costs, and the Grand Banks had no Ostrom-style institution to interrupt it. Climate negotiations run on the same arithmetic — each nation's emissions benefit that nation while the atmospheric cost falls on everyone, which is why every country has some incentive to let others cut first. So does antibiotic overuse, where each patient's marginal prescription helps that patient while the resistance it breeds becomes everyone's problem for a generation. Different resource, same trap, same innocence of any single participant.

## The Stag Hunt and the Fragility of Trust

Jean-Jacques Rousseau's second game captures a related but distinct face of the same problem: the **Stag Hunt**. Two hunters can bring down a stag together, a large reward, but only if both commit and neither breaks off. Alternatively, either hunter can go it alone after a rabbit — smaller, but guaranteed. The best joint outcome is two hunters on the stag. But if you commit to the stag and your partner peels off for the rabbit, you come home with nothing. The safe strategy is always the rabbit. The optimal strategy is the stag, but only on the condition that you trust your partner to choose it too.

Unlike the Prisoner's Dilemma, the Stag Hunt has two stable resting points — (stag, stag) and (rabbit, rabbit) — and both are genuine Nash equilibria. The first is better for everyone; the second is where groups get stuck the moment trust fails, even though nothing about the payoffs themselves has changed. This is the shape of most real cooperation problems: a founding team only succeeds if everyone commits fully, and any one defector can sink the venture; a marriage requires both partners to invest without a guarantee the other will match it; a nonviolent political movement needs enough citizens willing to take a risk simultaneously, because the citizen who shows up alone gets arrested or shot. The technology of cooperation is already understood in every one of these cases. What's missing is not knowledge but assurance — confidence that the other party will hold up their end if you hold up yours. This is a large part of why Moloch is so hard to dislodge: people can see the better equilibrium, describe it accurately, and still be unable to reach it, because reaching it requires simultaneous movement, and simultaneous movement without trust is not something individual virtue can manufacture alone.

## The Arms Race and the Shadow It Cast

Consider a case with no fish and no pasture at all: the nuclear arms race between the United States and the Soviet Union. From the late 1940s onward, each side reasoned in a way that was individually airtight — fail to match the other's arsenal and you invite a first strike or coercive blackmail, therefore you must build. The other side reasoned identically, for the identical reason. Neither government wanted a world stocked with tens of thousands of warheads — by the mid-1980s the two arsenals together held something on the order of seventy thousand — but each rational step in getting there was locally correct given what the other side had just done. This is precisely the interaction Axelrod points to when he cites Lewis Fry Richardson's mathematical model of arms races as a Prisoner's Dilemma among nations: mutual restraint would have left both countries safer and immeasurably richer, but unilateral restraint looked, and often was, suicidal.

What makes the Cold War instructive rather than merely bleak is that it also shows Moloch's logic being partially broken by exactly the mechanism the next chapter develops. The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 brought both governments to a point where the abstract mathematics of mutual destruction became, for thirteen days, a vivid and personal fear for John F. Kennedy and Nikita Khrushchev, who had to decide in real time whether to keep escalating. What emerged from that crisis was not disarmament — the arms race continued for another quarter century — but the first serious machinery of arms control: a Washington–Moscow hotline, and within a year the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963. Neither side stopped competing, but both began, however haltingly, to build channels through which today's restraint could be recognized, verified, and rewarded with tomorrow's reciprocal restraint. That is the shadow of the future starting to fall across a game that had, until then, been played as if there were no tomorrow at all — and it is the subject of Chapter 7.

## The Attention Economy's Perpetual Escalation

A newer version of the same trap runs on servers instead of oceans. Once social platforms began ranking content by an algorithm optimized for engagement — time on app, shares, replies — every platform found itself competing for a strictly finite resource: the attention of the same population of humans, who sleep, work, and can only look at one screen at a time. A platform that unilaterally made its feed calmer or less emotionally provocative would lose users to competitors that hadn't, because the more inflammatory feed reliably captures more attention in the short run. In 2021, internal research disclosed by the Facebook whistleblower Frances Haugen reportedly found that the company's own studies linked Instagram use to worsened body image and mental health among some teenage users — a finding that did not, on its own, change the underlying dynamic, because Instagram's leadership still faced the pressure every platform faced: slow the feed down and the next platform absorbs the attention you gave up. No single company's engineers are villains here; each is optimizing correctly for the game the market placed them in. A shortened public attention span, a coarsened discourse, a generation that reports feeling worse the more time it spends on the platforms built to keep it there — this is a casualty of the arithmetic, not of anyone's malice.

## Why Moloch Outmatches Malice

Malice is, in an important sense, the easier enemy. If suffering were produced by bad people making bad choices, the fix would be straightforward: identify the wrongdoers, stop them, punish them, or replace them with better people. Most moral traditions have implicitly modeled the problem of evil this way, generating a predictable repertoire — wars against enemies, purges of heretics, revolutions against oppressors, crusades against the wicked. When the actual cause is Moloch, every one of these responses fails, and several make matters worse.

Replacing the individuals accomplishes nothing, because the incentive that produced the bad behavior survives the personnel change intact. Swap the trawler-company owner for a saint, and the saint faces exactly the competitive pressure the owner did: fish at capacity or lose the company to someone who will. The saint may feel worse about it; the cod population will not notice the difference. Moral exhortation fares no better: telling people to simply cooperate in a Prisoner's Dilemma does not alter the payoffs, and the rare few who take the sermon seriously are outcompeted by everyone who didn't. Even revolution, the most drastic remedy available, routinely fails for the same reason: new leadership atop an unchanged structure inherits the unchanged incentives. The French Revolution replaced a king with the Terror; the Russian Revolution replaced a Tsar with Stalin; the Iranian Revolution replaced a Shah with a Supreme Leader answerable to no electorate. The problem was never fundamentally the person at the top; it was the game that position forced its occupant to keep playing.

This is why the name is not hyperbole. The experience of living inside a coordination failure feels precisely like being extorted by an intelligent, malevolent will — something extracting sacrifices, converting decent intentions into rotten outcomes, making individual effort feel futile — even though the "entity" doing the extracting is nothing more than the mathematics of the interaction, running itself out to its equilibrium regardless of who is standing where.

## Moloch Is Not Inevitable

If Moloch were simply the shape of reality itself — an inescapable feature of any world with more than one agent in it — this chapter would be a counsel of despair. It is not. Moloch has been beaten before, repeatedly, in settings we can study and reproduce. The central finding, established by Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments in the late 1970s, is that when interactions repeat over a long horizon, cooperation becomes achievable without any central authority to enforce it. The mechanism is what Axelrod calls the shadow of the future: the possibility that today's opponent will be tomorrow's opponent again, so today's defection invites tomorrow's retaliation.

> *"What makes it possible for cooperation to emerge is the fact that the players might meet again. This possibility means that the choices made today not only determine the outcome of this move, but can also influence the later choices of the players. The future can therefore cast a shadow back upon the present and thereby affect the current strategic situation."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

Chapter 7 develops the mechanics of that shadow in full. For now, the point is only that it is real, and Axelrod's most striking evidence for it comes from an unlikely source: the trenches of the First World War.

> *"During World War I, the Western Front was the scene of horrible battles for a few yards of territory. But between these battles, and even during them at other places along the five-hundred-mile line in France and Belgium, the enemy soldiers often exercised considerable restraint."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

A British staff officer, touring the front, reported his astonishment at seeing German soldiers walking about within rifle range of British lines, apparently unmolested — "these people evidently did not know there was a war on. Both sides apparently believed in the policy of 'live and let live.'" Opposing units, ostensibly committed to killing one another, developed tacit truces: artillery fired at the same predictable hour toward the same predictable target, day after day, so reliably that one English officer described a German shell that arrived "at seven... so regularly that you could set your watch by it.... It always had the same objective." This was not incompetence. It was the most hostile environment imaginable producing the least likely outcome imaginable: stable cooperation, built from the bottom up, with no commander's order behind it.

More striking still is the Saxon incident, in which German artillery mistakenly caused British casualties and a German soldier climbed onto the parapet to apologize: "We are very sorry about that; we hope no one was hurt. It is not our fault, it is that damned Prussian artillery." Axelrod's comment is the one worth keeping:

> *"This Saxon apology goes well beyond a merely instrumental effort to prevent retaliation. It reflects moral regret for having violated a situation of trust, and it shows concern that someone might have been hurt."*
> — Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*

What eventually destroyed the live-and-let-live system was not the soldiers. It was the command structure above them, which introduced the mandatory raid — an order that could be verified as carried out or not, and punished when it wasn't, precisely because it broke the pattern of predictable, low-cost signaling the soldiers had built to sustain their truce. The men in the trenches, left to themselves, had constructed a functioning peace in the middle of the most catastrophic war Europe had yet fought; it took a deliberate institutional effort from above to unmake it. The lesson generalizes: Moloch is beatable, but never automatically. Cooperation needs specific conditions — repetition, recognizable participants, the credible capacity to retaliate against defection — and where those conditions hold, cooperation can flourish even amid open hostility. Where they don't, defection wins out even among people who would sincerely rather cooperate. Designing institutions that manufacture those conditions on purpose is the project Chapter 8 calls mechanism design — which, this book will argue, is simply moral and political philosophy correctly understood.

## Morality as Anti-Moloch Technology

This puts us in a position to state the organizing thesis of Book II: morality is the technology human beings have invented to defeat Moloch. This is not a loose metaphor; it is a claim about what the core moral rules found in nearly every human society are actually for.

Take the injunction against lying. Lying corrodes the basis of iterated cooperation, because a society where deception is common cannot solve any coordination problem that depends on honest signaling — and the trench truces above worked only because a predictable, honest signal could be trusted by the other side. The prohibition on theft functions the same way: theft is defection inside a property-rights game, and a society where it is common cannot accumulate the capital or long-horizon investment secure property makes possible; property becomes a focal point, in the economist Thomas Schelling's sense, around which peaceful coordination crystallizes without anyone renegotiating it from scratch each time. The prohibition on murder addresses a still more dangerous instability: retaliatory violence, once begun as private vengeance, is among the most destructive equilibria a society can fall into, and cultures that tolerate blood feuds can be consumed by them for generations. Promise-keeping makes credible commitment possible at all — without it, no Stag Hunt larger than two people holding hands can be sustained, and a civilization is simply a very large number of Stag Hunts stacked on top of each other. And the instinct to reciprocate kindness and punish cruelty is, as Axelrod's tournaments demonstrated directly, the single strategy most responsible for stabilizing cooperation once it exists.

Each of these familiar rules, seen through this lens, turns out to be a coordination device — an institutional answer to a particular species of Moloch problem, discovered rather than invented over millennia of cultural trial and error, as groups that stumbled onto them outcompeted groups that didn't. This reframes the question a moral system exists to answer. We no longer need to ask only whether a rule feels right, the elephant's verdict, or whether it carries legitimate authority, the theocrat's question. We can ask something more precise and harder to fake: does this rule, widely adopted, reliably move agents out of a bad equilibrium into a better one? That question is testable — not a matter of taste, and not relative in any deep sense, because it ties morality to the same evidence biology, economics, and the mathematics of strategic interaction already share.

## The Boundary of the Game

An account this useful invites a specific danger: mistaking a powerful tool for a complete one. Before this book leans on the coordination frame for the rest of Book II and much of what follows, it is worth being precise about where that frame stops working, because a system that claims more explanatory power than it has will be dismantled by the first careful reader who notices the gap. Better to mark the edge ourselves.

A mother who wakes at three in the morning to nurse a sick child is not making a move in an iterated game. She is not weighing payoffs or calculating a shadow of the future. She is responding to a vulnerability that cannot reciprocate, cannot retaliate, and cannot exit the relationship — and it is precisely that dependency, not any strategic calculation, that generates her obligation. Game theory models interactions between agents who have alternatives; an infant has none. Care ethics, developed across the work of Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Virginia Held, and Eva Feder Kittay, treats exactly this kind of asymmetric relationship as constitutive of moral life rather than an edge case handled once the real analysis is finished. Joan Tronto's account of care names four elements worth stating because each does distinct work: attentiveness, the disposition to notice needs that cannot be strategically signaled; responsibility, the willingness to answer obligations arising from proximity rather than contract; competence, the requirement that care actually help, not merely mean well; and responsiveness, the caring agent's willingness to be changed by the encounter rather than remaining at the distance strategic calculation requires. Kittay adds a political observation: those who care for the dependent become dependent themselves — economically, socially, physically — a cost no game-theoretic model of the caregiver's "choice" captures. Care, on this account, is not a special case coordination theory will eventually absorb; it is the substrate coordination stands on. A society needs caring agents, formed inside relationships of exactly this asymmetric kind, before it can have cooperating agents capable of playing any game at all — a claim Chapter 11's account of virtue and Chapter 14's account of relationships both build on directly.

A different limit appears at the other end of moral life. A stranger who dives into a frozen river for a drowning child, a doctor who volunteers a year in a war zone, a person who gives a kidney to a stranger — these acts are supererogatory: they exceed what morality requires, entering the territory of moral heroism. Game theory has no category for this. In a strictly game-theoretic frame every available action is either optimal given the agent's goals, or it is not; there is no rung above optimal. An agent who sacrifices comfort or life for a stranger is, by pure strategic calculation, simply choosing a worse payoff when a better one was available. Yet no one judges the diver this way — we call the act admirable, not suboptimal, because it expresses a commitment that exceeds any calculation of advantage. Chapter 11's virtue framework accommodates this: courage and compassion have a coordination function, stabilizing the equilibria this book describes, but also an expressive dimension that outruns that function entirely. The courageous person does not act courageously in order to stabilize an equilibrium; she acts courageously because courage has become who she is.

A third limit concerns action aimed at nothing measurable at all. A citizen votes in an election where her ballot has, for practical purposes, zero chance of changing the outcome. A mourner attends a funeral for someone who can no longer be helped by the visit. Judged by instrumental rationality alone, each act is a mistake: real cost, no measurable benefit. Geoffrey Brennan and Loren Lomasky offer the better description in their concept of expressive rationality: people often act not to produce an outcome but to declare something about who they are — the voter saying *I am the kind of citizen who shows up*, the mourner saying *this person's life mattered*. This is not equilibrium selection, and it is not irrationality either; it belongs to a different register entirely, one about meaning rather than payoff, and a theory that sees only the strategic register will misread a great deal of what moves people to act.

Together these three limits mark the honest boundary of this chapter's claim. Game theory is the right primary tool for coordination failures — the class that includes most large-scale institutional, political, and economic suffering: overfished oceans, arms races, attention markets, housing shortages, atmospheric carbon. It is not the right tool for the asymmetric dependency of care, for supererogation, or for expressive action, and this book does not pretend otherwise. The valence axiom of Chapter 3 — that conscious suffering and flourishing are what ultimately matter — grounds all four registers of moral life; game theory, virtue, care ethics, and expressive rationality are simply the tools each register calls for. A system claiming one tool could do everything would be lying about its own reach. Bounding the claim honestly is not a weakness in the argument; it is what keeps the argument standing once an opponent starts probing for the overreach.

## Would You Still Be Just, Wearing the Ring of Gyges?

The most serious objection to everything this chapter has argued is roughly twenty-four hundred years old, and it comes from Glaucon, in Book II of Plato's *Republic*. Glaucon does not deny that people behave justly. He denies that justice, on an account like this one, is ever more than a second-best strategy adopted by people too weak to get away with injustice. To make the point vivid, he imagines a ring that renders its wearer invisible — the ring of Gyges — and asks what a person who found it, and who could now take whatever he wanted and harm whomever he wished with no possibility of detection or retaliation, would actually do. Glaucon's wager is that the perfectly rational, self-interested wearer of that ring would abandon justice entirely, because on a purely instrumental account — one that grounds moral rules in their coordination function, in shadows cast by a future of possible retaliation — the ring simply switches the shadow off. Remove all possibility of being caught, and every coordination device this chapter has described should evaporate with it. If it doesn't, Glaucon's challenge concludes, that is proof that our reasons for justice were never really about coordination; they were about something the invisible man still has, and something a purely game-theoretic morality cannot locate.

This objection lands, and the framework built here has only a partial answer to it. That answer runs through Chapter 3. The valence axiom does not ground the wrongness of harm in its detectability or its strategic consequences; it grounds that wrongness directly in the suffering of the conscious being harmed, whether or not anyone — including the person doing the harming — will ever know. On this account, the invisible man who tortures a stranger for sport does something exactly as wrong as the visible man who does the same thing before witnesses, because the wrongness was never located in the witnesses. What this chapter's coordination analysis explains is not the ultimate source of that wrongness but the observable shape morality's specific rules take among agents who can see, remember, and retaliate against one another. Game theory explains the architecture of the rules; it was never meant to explain, on its own, why anyone should care about the person the rules protect — that answer was already supplied, before this chapter began, by the axiom grounding value in consciousness itself. The boundary section above supplies a second, independent piece of the answer: the fact that people act morally where no coordination benefit exists — nursing a child who can never repay them, mourning someone who will never know they came — is itself evidence that morality was never purely instrumental in the way Glaucon assumes.

Even so, honesty requires naming what remains unresolved. This is the objection the Position Registry tracks as the standing challenge to treating morality as coordination technology, and after this chapter's boundary work it is rated a low-vulnerability position rather than a defeated one — a meaningfully different thing. The residual worry: a great deal of the specific *content* of this book's rules — which promises bind, how property is individuated, what reciprocity demands in a given culture — is still derived from coordination logic, which by its nature goes quiet exactly where detection and retaliation go quiet. The axiom answers Glaucon for the deep question of why harm matters; it does not, on its own, tell the invisible man which of the thousand coordination-shaped conventions he now has no game-theoretic reason to follow. That gap is real and not fully closed, and pretending otherwise would repeat the very overreach this chapter has spent its middle section warning against.

## The Return to the Grand Banks

The cod have still not come back. More than three decades after Crosbie's announcement, the biomass on the Grand Banks remains a fraction of what it was when Cabot's crew first described a sea thick enough to walk on. No one still fishing those waters wanted this outcome; no single decision produced it. It was produced by thousands of locally rational decisions, compounding across decades, inside a structure that rewarded exactly the behavior that destroyed it — the entire lesson of this chapter compressed into one dead fishery. Most of the suffering this book is concerned with reducing is not the product of malice. It is the product of Moloch: individually rational choices, taken by people no worse than any of us, adding up to outcomes none of them chose.

That is also, properly understood, the good news. A malicious world would require us to defeat malice, which history suggests is close to impossible. A Moloch-ridden world only requires us to change the game — and games, unlike hearts, can be redesigned. Chapter 7 develops the mechanics by which trust emerges, stabilizes, and defends itself inside a repeated game. Chapter 8 develops the deliberate engineering of institutions — courts, markets, commons regimes, treaties — that manufacture those conditions on purpose, rather than waiting for them to emerge, as they did in the trenches, by accident. The enemy has a name now, and a shape precise enough to be studied. What remains is learning how to beat it.

## Chapter Summary

Most unnecessary suffering is not caused by bad people; it is caused by ordinary people trapped in situations where individually rational choices produce collectively catastrophic outcomes. The collapse of the Grand Banks cod fishery in 1992, after five centuries of abundance, shows this arithmetic in its purest form: no single captain destroyed the fishery, yet thousands of locally sensible decisions did. Game theory gives this pattern a precise structure through the Prisoner's Dilemma, the Tragedy of the Commons, and the Stag Hunt, and this book calls the pattern itself Moloch, after Allen Ginsberg's poem — not a literal god, but a name for the phenomenology of a trap that extracts sacrifices from decent people and makes their individual efforts feel futile. Moloch is not inevitable: Robert Axelrod's research, and the striking case of live-and-let-live cooperation among enemy soldiers in the First World War trenches, show that cooperation can stabilize even in the most hostile conditions once interactions repeat and defection can be recognized and punished. Morality itself is best understood as the technology humanity has built to defeat Moloch — but that technology has edges, and this chapter marks them honestly: care for the dependent, supererogatory acts of heroism, and expressive action aimed at meaning rather than outcomes all lie partly or wholly outside what coordination theory can explain. Glaucon's ring of Gyges remains the sharpest challenge to this whole account, and it is answered only partially, by grounding harm's wrongness in consciousness itself rather than in detection. The next chapter builds the machinery of trust that makes defeating Moloch possible in practice.
