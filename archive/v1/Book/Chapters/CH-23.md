## The Hardest Coordination Problem

Everything in Book II applies to international politics — the Prisoner's Dilemmas, the Stag Hunts, the Moloch traps, the mechanism design challenges — except that at the international level, almost none of the usual enforcement mechanisms exist. No world government. No impartial court with coercive power over states. No mechanism by which agreements can be enforced if one party decides to defect. International politics is what coordination looks like when the shadow of the future is long but there is no sovereign to enforce the rules.

This is not to say international politics is lawless. It has laws (international law, treaties, conventions). It has norms (the sovereignty of states, the prohibition on genocide, diplomatic protocols). It has institutions (the UN, the WTO, the ICC, regional bodies like the EU and ASEAN). It has expectations, traditions, and shared practices that most states honor most of the time. Some of these have teeth; most do not. The international order is a loose, partial coordination regime held together by self-interest, reputation, occasional enforcement, and the historical memory of what happens when it collapses.

The framework's position on war, peace, and international order is shaped by the same principles that have governed everything in Book V: coordination analysis, mechanism design, subsidiarity, error-correction, and the commitment to honest engagement with what actually works. This chapter applies those principles to the hardest case: politics between states that have no common sovereign.

## Why War Is (Usually) Irrational

Before discussing when war is justified, we should observe that war, as a practice, is usually irrational for the parties involved. This is not a pacifist cliche; it is a statistical observation.

Consider the costs of major war:
- Massive loss of life, including civilians
- Destruction of infrastructure that took decades to build
- Economic devastation lasting years or generations
- Social trauma persisting across generations
- Diplomatic isolation and reputational damage
- Loss of opportunities that would have been available under peace
- Risk of complete defeat and occupation

Against these, the benefits of war are usually:
- Territory, resources, or other tangible gains that are often smaller than the costs
- Political objectives that could often be achieved through other means
- The satisfaction of nationalism or revenge (which the framework does not count as a legitimate objective)

In most historical wars, an honest cost-benefit analysis would have rejected the war. States go to war expecting quick victory and small costs; they usually get long wars with large costs. The planning fallacy (Chapter 2) applies at the international level with catastrophic effects.

The framework's default follows: **war is presumptively irrational, and the burden of proof is very high on anyone who proposes it**. Some wars are necessary — but most historical wars would not have met this burden if applied honestly.

## When War Is Justified: Just War Theory, Updated

The tradition of Just War Theory, running from Augustine through Aquinas through Grotius through contemporary thinkers, has generated a set of conditions that purport to distinguish justified from unjustified wars. The framework mostly agrees with this tradition, while updating it in light of modern strategic realities and the coordination-focused approach we have been developing.

Traditional Just War Theory distinguishes two sets of conditions: *jus ad bellum* (justice in going to war) and *jus in bello* (justice in the conduct of war).

### Jus Ad Bellum: When to Fight

The traditional conditions, updated:

**1. Just cause.** The only genuinely just causes for war are self-defense and defense of others against grave attack. Wars of conquest, wars for resources, wars to spread ideology, wars for national glory — none of these qualify. The bar is set deliberately high: the cause must be grave enough that the alternative to war would be worse than the war itself.

**2. Legitimate authority.** War must be decided by legitimate political institutions, not by individual leaders acting unilaterally or by unauthorized factions. In democracies, this means constitutional processes, which usually require legislative authorization for anything beyond immediate self-defense. The framework strongly opposes the modern practice of executive war-making without legislative authorization.

**3. Right intention.** The purpose of the war must actually be the just cause, not some other objective dressed up as the just cause. A war for "freedom" that is actually for oil or strategic position is not morally equivalent to a war for the stated reason. This condition is hard to verify externally but matters for moral evaluation.

**4. Last resort.** War must be the last resort after non-military options have been genuinely exhausted. Diplomacy, economic measures, international pressure, negotiation — these must be sincerely attempted, not treated as box-checking exercises on the way to predetermined war.

**5. Reasonable chance of success.** War that cannot achieve its objectives causes suffering without benefit. If a cause is just but pursuing it militarily will not succeed, other means must be tried. This condition is especially important because it blocks the tempting argument "we must do something, and war is something" when the "something" will not work.

**6. Proportionality (ad bellum).** The expected good from winning the war must be proportionate to the expected costs of fighting it. A just cause does not justify unlimited suffering in its pursuit. If the cost of victory would be worse than the cost of tolerating the injustice, war is not justified.

**7. Public declaration.** Wars should be declared publicly, with stated objectives, so that citizens and the international community can evaluate them. Secret wars and covert actions that evade public scrutiny undermine democratic accountability and create moral hazard.

These conditions are not mechanical. Reasonable people applying them honestly will sometimes disagree. But they function as a serious filter when applied in good faith, and most historical wars would fail several of them.

### Jus In Bello: How to Fight

If war is occurring, what constraints apply to how it is conducted?

**1. Discrimination.** Civilians who are not participating in hostilities must be protected. Attacks must be aimed at military targets. This rules out deliberate targeting of civilians, including through indiscriminate weapons whose effects cannot be limited to combatants. The framework treats this as nearly absolute.

**2. Proportionality (in bello).** Even in attacks on legitimate military targets, the expected military benefit must be proportionate to the expected civilian harm. "Collateral damage" is not automatically acceptable; it must be weighed against the military necessity.

**3. Prohibition on certain weapons.** Weapons whose effects cannot be limited to combatants (biological weapons, certain chemical weapons, landmines in certain contexts), weapons that cause unnecessary suffering, and weapons of mass destruction against civilian populations are prohibited. These prohibitions are codified in international humanitarian law.

**4. Treatment of prisoners.** Captured combatants become protected persons and cannot be abused, tortured, or killed. This is both a moral constraint and a practical one — states that treat prisoners well create incentives for enemy forces to surrender rather than fight to the death.

**5. No perfidy.** Deception that exploits protected statuses (wearing enemy uniforms or civilian clothing to launch attacks, feigning surrender to kill soldiers who approach) is prohibited, because it undermines the protections that reduce overall harm.

**6. Accountability for violations.** Individuals who commit war crimes — on any side — should be held accountable. This is the *jus post bellum* tradition (justice after war), which has become increasingly important with the development of international criminal law.

These constraints are not always honored. They are, however, widely recognized as legitimate, and violations produce diplomatic costs, international prosecution when possible, and reputational damage that persists for generations. The norms exist, and the framework endorses them strongly.

## The Nuclear Problem

A special case that Just War Theory was not originally designed to handle: nuclear weapons. The existence of weapons that can destroy cities in seconds — and, in extreme scenarios, destroy civilization — creates strategic and moral dilemmas that older frameworks struggle with.

The framework's position on nuclear weapons:

**1. First use is almost certainly unjustifiable.** The destructiveness of nuclear weapons is so vast that the proportionality condition of just war theory is extremely difficult to satisfy. Hypothetical scenarios (stopping a genocide, preventing an imminent worse attack) can be constructed, but in practice, any realistic first use is likely to fail the just war conditions.

**2. Deterrence is a genuine dilemma.** The doctrine of mutually assured destruction (MAD) has, arguably, prevented major war between nuclear powers since 1945. This is an enormous benefit. But it depends on the willingness to use nuclear weapons in retaliation — a willingness that is morally problematic in itself, because if deterrence fails, actual use would be catastrophic. The framework acknowledges this tension without fully resolving it: nuclear deterrence has saved more lives than it has cost, and abandoning it unilaterally would be dangerous, but its continued existence is a horrifying ongoing risk.

**3. Non-proliferation is critical.** The more states that have nuclear weapons, the higher the risk of accidental use, theft, or use by a state with unstable leadership. The framework strongly supports non-proliferation efforts, even when they come at the cost of some sovereign prerogatives.

**4. Arms reduction is a moral priority.** The Cold War arsenals were vastly larger than what any conceivable deterrence purpose required. Reductions to the minimum credible deterrent level — far below current stockpiles — are achievable and would reduce existential risk substantially.

**5. The goal is eventual abolition, but the path is unclear.** If nuclear weapons could be verifiably eliminated, the world would be safer. But verification is technically very difficult, and the disarmament path has risks of its own (states cheat, imbalances emerge, the genie cannot be put back in the bottle). The framework endorses the goal while acknowledging the path is not currently available.

Nuclear weapons are the clearest case where the framework's mechanism design approach runs into the limits of what institutional design can achieve. We have not yet invented the institutions that would allow verifiable disarmament, and the cost of institutional failure could be civilizational. This is humility-inducing.

## International Order: What Holds It Together

Above the level of individual wars is the broader question of international order. What keeps the system of states from degenerating into continuous conflict? What allows trade, travel, communication, scientific cooperation, and the other benefits of international cooperation to continue? What mechanisms produce at least partial coordination in a world without a sovereign?

Several factors, none sufficient alone but collectively significant:

**1. Reputation and repeated interaction.** States interact repeatedly, and their reputations matter. A state that reneges on treaties will find future treaties harder to negotiate. A state that treats foreigners well will attract investment and tourism. A state that provides reliable trade partners will benefit economically. These reputation effects create incentives for cooperation even without enforcement.

**2. Economic interdependence.** When states are economically intertwined, war between them becomes more costly for both. This is not a guarantee of peace — interdependent countries have fought (WWI is the classic counterexample), but it raises the cost of conflict and provides an ongoing incentive toward peaceful relations.

**3. International institutions.** The UN, WTO, ICC, IMF, World Bank, and regional bodies are not world government, but they provide forums for negotiation, mechanisms for dispute resolution, and (in some cases) coordinated responses to specific problems. Their effectiveness varies enormously, but collectively they reduce friction and produce cooperation on many issues.

**4. International law.** Treaties, conventions, and customary international law create expectations about state behavior. Violations carry costs (diplomatic, reputational, sometimes legal). The law is not self-enforcing, but it shapes state behavior more than cynics acknowledge.

**5. Shared norms.** Certain prohibitions (genocide, slavery, aggressive war, targeting civilians) have become widely accepted norms that states violate only at cost. The norms emerged gradually and remain contested, but they represent real achievements.

**6. Hegemonic stabilization.** At various times, a dominant power has enforced an international order that benefits itself but also provides public goods (protected shipping lanes, reserve currency, collective security). *Pax Romana*, *Pax Britannica*, *Pax Americana*. These are imperfect and often unjust, but they are less chaotic than the alternative of open conflict among peers. The framework does not endorse hegemony as an ideal, but acknowledges that it has historically been one mechanism by which international order has been maintained.

These factors interact. An order that rests on only one of them is fragile; an order that combines several is more robust. The post-WWII order, whatever its flaws, has combined all of these factors to produce the longest period without major great-power war in modern history. This is a real achievement, and dismissing it as "neoliberal" or "imperialist" misses what it accomplished.

## The Challenges of the Current Order

The post-1945 order is showing strain. Several challenges deserve honest acknowledgment:

**1. The rise of China and the shifting balance of power.** The US-led order was designed when the US was dominant. As power becomes more distributed, the assumptions of the old order are tested. Whether the transition happens peacefully or through conflict is perhaps the single most consequential question of the 21st century.

**2. Climate change.** A genuinely global problem that the state-centric international system is poorly equipped to handle. Each state has incentive to free-ride on others' emissions cuts, and enforcement mechanisms are weak. The Paris Agreement represents a real attempt to coordinate but is likely insufficient to the scale of the problem.

**3. Pandemic and biosecurity risks.** COVID-19 demonstrated that pandemic response is still largely national, with international coordination too slow and too weak to handle rapid-onset global health emergencies. Biological weapons and gain-of-function research create ongoing existential risks.

**4. AI development.** The framework will address this more fully in Chapter 32, but international aspects belong here: AI development is happening in multiple countries simultaneously, and the coordination problem of ensuring safe development is among the hardest the international system has ever faced.

**5. Erosion of norms.** Some of the norms that have structured the post-war order (prohibitions on territorial conquest, the sovereign equality of states, human rights standards) are being eroded by powerful states that find them inconvenient. This erosion is partial and uneven but real.

**6. Inequality within the global order.** The current international order benefits some countries and populations far more than others. Complaints that it is unjust are partially correct and deserve engagement rather than dismissal.

The framework does not have complete solutions to these challenges. It has principles for approaching them: continue the slow work of building coordination mechanisms, defend the norms that prevent the worst outcomes, accept that imperfect institutions are usually better than no institutions, invest in the infrastructure that makes international cooperation possible even when specific problems seem unsolvable.

## The Ethics of Foreign Policy

What does the framework say about how states should actually conduct themselves in foreign affairs?

**1. Realism is not the whole story.** The realist tradition (Morgenthau, Mearsheimer, Waltz) holds that states act on self-interest and that moral considerations are irrelevant to foreign policy. This is empirically false — states do sometimes act on moral considerations. But realism captures something real: states that ignore their interests will not survive to pursue moral goals.

**2. Idealism is not the whole story either.** The liberal internationalist tradition holds that values should guide foreign policy. This has produced both genuine successes (the post-war international order) and genuine disasters (interventions that assumed liberal institutions could be installed by force). Values matter, but so do constraints on what can be achieved.

**3. The framework's position: enlightened self-interest with genuine concern for coordination.** States should act in their own interest, but that interest is usually served by cooperation, reputation, and stable international order. Long-term self-interest coincides with much of what morality requires — not all of it, but more than cynics acknowledge.

**4. Specific commitments.** States should honor treaties. Should avoid unprovoked war. Should accept constraints on weapons and tactics that would cause disproportionate harm. Should participate in international institutions even when specific decisions go against them. Should take refugees when they can. Should fund global public goods (disease surveillance, climate monitoring, scientific research). Should restrict arms sales to regimes likely to use them against civilians. Should criticize human rights violations, including by allies. These are not demanding requirements; they are minimum standards for states that want to be reliable international partners.

**5. Sovereignty is not absolute.** Traditional international law treated sovereignty as nearly absolute — states could do what they wanted within their borders, and other states could not legitimately intervene. The framework holds that sovereignty is valuable but not absolute. States that engage in genocide or mass atrocities against their own populations forfeit some of the protections sovereignty provides. The "Responsibility to Protect" doctrine, developed after Rwanda, codifies this principle. Its implementation has been inconsistent, but the principle is correct: sovereignty is a political convention, not a moral license.

## The Limits of International Politics

We should end this chapter with a note of humility. The framework has tools for analyzing coordination problems, and international politics is a coordination problem. But international politics is also the domain in which our tools work *worst*, because the enforcement mechanisms that make domestic politics tractable are absent at the international level.

We do not know how to reliably prevent war. We do not know how to stop genocide when it begins. We do not know how to coordinate effective action on climate change. We do not know how to ensure safe AI development globally. These are not failures of the framework; they are failures of human institutional capacity. The framework provides the analysis that lets us see the problems clearly, but it cannot, by itself, solve them.

What it can do is recommend specific principles — the just war conditions, the strong preference for diplomacy, the importance of international institutions, the defense of norms that prevent the worst outcomes, the combination of realism about interests with genuine concern for coordination. Following these principles produces better outcomes than ignoring them. Not perfect outcomes — the system is too complex for perfection — but better, and over time, the difference between "better" and "perfect" becomes the difference between civilization and catastrophe.

## Summary

1. **War is usually irrational** for the parties involved, when costs and benefits are honestly assessed. The presumption is against war.

2. **Just War Theory** provides useful criteria: just cause (usually only self-defense or defense of others), legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, reasonable chance of success, proportionality, public declaration.

3. **Jus in bello** constraints: discrimination between combatants and civilians, proportionality in attacks, prohibition on certain weapons, humane treatment of prisoners, no perfidy, accountability for violations.

4. **Nuclear weapons** create a dilemma. First use is almost always unjustifiable. Deterrence has saved more lives than it has cost but remains morally problematic. Non-proliferation and arms reduction are priorities; abolition is a long-term goal with unclear path.

5. **International order is held together** by reputation, economic interdependence, institutions, law, norms, and sometimes hegemonic stabilization. Each is imperfect; together they produce a partial order.

6. **The current order faces challenges**: shifting power, climate change, pandemic risks, AI, norm erosion, inequality. Honest engagement is needed.

7. **Ethical foreign policy** combines realism about interests with genuine concern for coordination and moral constraints. Sovereignty is valuable but not absolute.

8. **Humility about limits.** International politics is where the framework's tools work worst. We have analysis but no reliable solutions to the hardest problems.

The final chapter of Book V addresses how to take all these tools and apply them to concrete policy questions — the practical discipline of policy evaluation that citizens and officials must engage in to make the framework operational.
