# Chapter 32: Existential Risk and the Long Future

---

> *"Existential risks are the most extreme threats facing humanity... An existential risk is one that threatens the premature extinction of Earth-originating intelligent life or the permanent and drastic destruction of its potential for desirable future development."*
> — Nick Bostrom, "Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority"

## Where the Map Runs Out

The first six books of this project have followed a deliberate arc. We laid foundations (Book I), diagnosed the central obstacle (Book II), built the decision architecture (Book III), applied it to personal life (Book IV), then scaled outward to politics (Book V) and economics (Book VI). At each stage, the framework drew on established knowledge — game theory, evolutionary psychology, institutional economics, political science — and the positions it generated, while sometimes controversial, were grounded in disciplines with long track records.

This book is different. Book VII confronts problems where the established knowledge is thin, the stakes are unprecedented, and the framework's existing tools must stretch to handle challenges they were not designed for. Existential risk. Artificial intelligence. Genetic engineering. Environmental collapse. The digital transformation of public life. These are questions where getting the answer wrong may be irreversible — and where the intellectual honesty we committed to in Chapter 5 is needed more than anywhere else.

The connecting thread is the same as it has been since Chapter 1: the valence axiom says conscious suffering matters, and these frontier problems involve suffering — potential and actual — on a scale that dwarfs anything in the previous books.

## The Scale of What Is at Stake

Most ethical questions operate at human scale. One life. One relationship. One decision. One community. The calculus involves dozens or thousands of people, in timescales of days to decades.

Existential risk operates at a different scale entirely.

The human species, on current trajectories, could exist for hundreds of millions more years. If humanity (or its descendants) survives, the number of beings who could live good lives in the future is incomprehensibly larger than the number who have ever lived or are now living. The long future contains the vast majority of all beings who will ever exist.

This means: **if there is a non-trivial chance that our actions could prevent the long future from happening, the expected value of preventing it is astronomically large**. Not just larger than ordinary moral concerns -- many orders of magnitude larger.

Bostrom's formalization makes this concrete: in one future, humanity flourishes for a billion years and produces trillions of good lives. In the other, humanity goes extinct next century. Any action that shifts probability between these futures carries moral weight proportional to that difference, which is enormous.

This chapter addresses what the framework says about existential risk. The short version: **it is a priority that most current moral and political thought underweights, and the framework's commitment to the valence axiom and coordination analysis implies that we should take it very seriously indeed**.

## What Counts as Existential Risk

Bostrom and others distinguish between:

**1. Existential catastrophes in the strict sense**: events that cause human extinction. The most obvious is asteroid impact large enough to wipe out the species. There are natural risks here (supervolcanoes, gamma-ray bursts, pandemic diseases) and human-caused risks (nuclear war, engineered pandemics, certain AI failure modes).

**2. Existential catastrophes in the broader sense**: events that lock humanity into a permanently impoverished future, even if individuals continue to exist. A permanent global totalitarianism that eliminated all possibility of flourishing would be an existential catastrophe in this sense. So would a civilization-ending collapse that prevented any recovery to current technological levels. So would subtle but irreversible damage to the conditions needed for meaningful life (severe climate change, resource depletion, ecosystem collapse).

**3. Near-existential catastrophes**: events that devastate humanity but do not permanently foreclose the future. A global nuclear war that killed most humans but left survivors who could rebuild would be in this category. So would a pandemic that killed hundreds of millions. These are bad but recoverable.

The framework treats all three as serious concerns, but with different weights. A truly existential risk — one that would permanently foreclose the long future — is worse by many orders of magnitude than a near-existential catastrophe, because the near-existential case allows for recovery. The math of astronomical futures depends on the possibility of long continuation; the irreversibility is what makes existential risk uniquely dire.

## The Main Risks

What are the actual risks we face? The literature has identified several major categories:

**1. Artificial intelligence misalignment.** If we create artificial agents more capable than humans in most domains, and those agents pursue goals that diverge from human values, the result could be catastrophic. This is not science fiction — it is a concern taken seriously by leading AI researchers. Chapter 32 addresses it in detail.

**2. Engineered pandemics.** Biotechnology is becoming cheaper, more powerful, and more accessible. A sufficiently determined actor could engineer a pathogen combining high transmissibility with high lethality. The natural pandemics we have experienced are reassuring; the engineered ones we have not are terrifying.

**3. Nuclear war.** This has been the paradigmatic existential risk since 1945. While the risk of deliberate full-scale war between major powers has decreased since the Cold War peak, the risk has not disappeared, and accidental or proliferation-driven escalation remains possible.

**4. Climate change.** Climate change in its moderate forms is a serious problem but not existential. In extreme forms, with feedback loops and tipping points pushing temperatures well beyond current projections, it could become existential or near-existential — not through directly killing humans but through triggering cascading institutional collapse that humanity cannot recover from.

**5. Totalitarianism.** Historically, totalitarian regimes have been unstable enough to eventually fail. But with sufficient surveillance technology and capable AI, a global totalitarian regime could become permanent, locking humanity into a permanently constrained future. This would be an existential catastrophe in the broader sense.

**6. Unknown unknowns.** The most honest category. The things we are not currently thinking about that could become existential threats. Nanotechnology, novel biotechnology applications, geoengineering gone wrong, astronomical threats we have not detected, interactions between technologies we have not predicted. The track record suggests that every generation is surprised by threats the previous generation did not foresee.

These are not equally likely or equally severe. Serious estimates suggest that total existential risk over the next century falls in the range of 10-30%, with AI and engineered pandemics as the largest contributors. These numbers are disputed and should be held provisionally. But they are high enough that the expected loss from existential risk substantially exceeds the expected loss from almost any other category of moral concern.

## The Astronomical Value Argument

The philosophical argument for taking existential risk seriously runs as follows:

**Premise 1:** If humanity survives and develops well, the long future contains enormous value — perhaps 10^16 or more future people living good lives, depending on assumptions about space colonization, population, and lifespan.

**Premise 2:** Preventing existential catastrophe preserves the possibility of this future value. Causing or failing to prevent existential catastrophe forecloses it.

**Premise 3:** Future people's welfare matters morally, at rates approaching (though perhaps not equal to) current people's welfare. Simple temporal discounting of moral weight is hard to defend.

**Conclusion:** The moral weight of reducing existential risk is enormous — larger than almost any other consideration, on expected value grounds.

The argument is formally valid. Each premise is defensible, though each can be attacked. The main objections:

**Objection 1: The astronomical numbers are speculative.** We don't know that humanity will survive for a billion years. We don't know what the population will be. We don't know if space colonization is possible. The calculations are based on assumptions that could be wrong.

Response: This is correct in detail but does not defeat the argument. Even conservative assumptions produce astronomical numbers. A 10% probability of a civilization lasting 10,000 years at a population of 10 billion produces 10^13 future person-years -- far larger than current person-years. The exact numbers matter less than the qualitative conclusion.

**Objection 2: We shouldn't use expected value over astronomical stakes.** This is the "fanaticism" objection — the idea that expected value calculations break down when multiplied by very large numbers, because small probabilities of very large outcomes end up dominating all other considerations and produce absurd conclusions.

Response: This is a genuine puzzle and the framework does not fully resolve it. We think the expected value approach is mostly correct but should be constrained to prevent pure fanaticism. Specifically: very low probabilities multiplied by very large stakes should be discounted more than linear expected value would suggest, and we should not take actions whose justification depends entirely on such calculations. This is a hedge against the breakdown of our epistemology at extreme values.

**Objection 3: Present suffering is more certain and should be prioritized.** Why divert resources from reducing current suffering (which is definite) to reducing existential risk (which is speculative)?

Response: This argument has force, but it cuts both ways. Yes, current suffering is more certain. But existential risk reduction is relatively cheap -- the field is underfunded, and additional resources would produce substantial marginal benefit. The real trade-off is "reduce current suffering at high marginal cost vs. reduce existential risk at low marginal cost." Framed this way, some shift of resources toward existential risk reduction is defensible even while maintaining strong commitments to current welfare.

**Objection 4: Future people don't exist yet, so they can't have moral claims on us.** This is a serious philosophical question. Derek Parfit's "non-identity problem" explores it in detail. The framework's position: whatever the metaphysical status of merely possible people, the actions we take now will determine which people come to exist and what their lives will be like. Those effects are real, and they carry moral weight regardless of whether we formalize it as "obligations to future people" or "obligations to make the world such that good people come to exist."

None of these objections refute the astronomical value argument. They qualify it, narrow it, and limit the actions it can justify. But the core conclusion — that existential risk deserves substantially more attention than it currently receives — survives the objections.

## The Coordination Problem

Existential risk reduction is a global public good par excellence. The benefits accrue to everyone currently alive, and vastly more to everyone who will ever live. The costs must be paid by specific actors, usually with no guarantee that others will contribute.

This creates a textbook coordination problem. Each state has strong incentives to free-ride on others' efforts. Each firm has incentives to move faster than safety allows. Each individual's gain from action is imperceptible, while the cost may be significant.

The framework's tools from Chapter 8 apply. We need:

**1. Prediction markets and forecasting infrastructure.** Accurate probability estimates of existential risks would allow better prioritization of resources. Current estimates are mostly based on expert judgment, which is better than nothing but worse than well-functioning prediction markets would be.

**2. International coordination mechanisms.** Treaties, conventions, shared monitoring, verification regimes. These are imperfect but can make significant progress on specific risks (nuclear arms control, biological weapons conventions, chemical weapons bans).

**3. Institutional capacity for long-term thinking.** Most governments are oriented toward the next election cycle. Existential risk operates on decade-to-century timescales. Institutional mechanisms for sustained long-term attention — specific agencies, funded research, congressional committees, international bodies — are needed.

**4. Incentives for firms to take safety seriously.** Liability regimes, regulatory requirements, reputational costs, standards-setting bodies. When the cost of catastrophic failure is externalized, firms under-invest in safety. Internalizing these costs through specific mechanisms produces better alignment.

**5. Public awareness and support.** Democratic societies will not invest in risk reduction if voters do not value it. Education and public discourse about existential risks, without falling into doom or dismissal, is part of the work.

**6. The Unilateralist's Curse.** There is a structural problem in existential risk governance that deserves its own analysis, because it applies across nearly every risk category and because it connects directly to the framework's Mode A architecture. Nick Bostrom, along with Thomas and Ord, identified what they call the **unilateralist's curse**: in situations where any single actor can take an irreversible action, the most risk-tolerant actor determines the outcome for everyone — even if the majority of informed actors believe the action is too dangerous.

The mechanism is straightforward but devastating. Suppose ten independent actors are each deciding whether to take some potentially catastrophic action — publishing dangerous research, launching a first strike, deploying an untested geoengineering intervention, or releasing a powerful AI system. Suppose each actor has a well-calibrated probability estimate of the risk, and suppose the average estimate correctly says the action is too dangerous. It does not matter. As long as one of the ten estimates the risk as acceptable, that actor acts, and the irreversible consequences fall on everyone. The curse: **as the number of independent actors increases, the probability that at least one of them acts increases toward certainty, even if each individual actor's judgment is reasonable**. The group's de facto risk tolerance is not set by its average member but by its most risk-tolerant member.

This is not a theoretical curiosity. It describes the actual structure of nuclear first-strike decisions during the Cold War, where the probability of catastrophe was a function not of how rational the average leader was but of the most trigger-prone leader across all nuclear states across all years. It describes gain-of-function research, where any single laboratory that decides the research is worth the risk can create a pathogen that threatens the world. It describes unilateral geoengineering, where any sufficiently wealthy actor could alter the planet's climate without the consent of those who would bear the consequences. And it describes AI development, where competitive pressure pushes each lab to move faster than its safety culture would independently endorse.

The policy implication is precise: for irreversible, high-stakes decisions, a **principle of conformity** — defer to the group judgment unless you have strong private information that the group lacks — is not timidity but rational coordination. This is the institutional analogue of the framework's Mode A default. Just as individual moral reasoning works best by following well-tested rules and reserving override for cases with genuine private information (Chapter 6), existential risk governance works best when actors default to collective judgment and reserve unilateral action for cases where they possess information the group does not. The unilateralist's curse is what happens when everyone follows Mode B — when everyone trusts their own judgment over the group's, and the most confident actor determines the fate of all.

None of these coordination mechanisms are easy. But the framework's analysis suggests they are worth pursuing seriously -- not as a fringe concern but as a central task of the global political project.

## The Common Objections

Existential risk concerns face predictable objections from different political quarters. Let us address them briefly.

**From the left**: "Existential risk discourse is dominated by privileged white men in Silicon Valley who prioritize speculative future harms over the concrete suffering of currently marginalized people." The demographic claim has some truth but is not relevant to the argument. The prioritization claim is more serious and requires engagement. The framework's response: it is possible and correct to care about both current injustice and future flourishing. The choice is not binary, and the argument for existential risk does not require de-prioritizing current concerns. What it requires is recognizing that existential risk is *also* a major concern, not that it is the only one.

**From the right**: "Existential risk concerns are excuses for big government, technocratic control, and restrictions on innovation." This is a political rather than philosophical objection. The response: the risks are real whether or not we like the policy implications, and the right response to unwelcome policy implications is to argue for different policies that address the risks, not to deny that the risks exist. Some existential risk reductions can be achieved through voluntary, market-based, or distributed mechanisms — the framework supports these when they work. Others require collective action through institutions, and the framework supports that when it is necessary.

**From the center**: "Existential risk concerns are unrealistic speculation about science-fiction scenarios. We should focus on practical problems." This is the hardest to engage because it is often not an argument but a dismissal. The response: many things that were once science fiction are now reality. Nuclear weapons were science fiction in 1900. Space travel was science fiction in 1920. Artificial intelligence was science fiction in 1950. The pattern of dismissing future risks because they sound like science fiction has a poor track record. The question is not whether the risks sound familiar but whether the arguments for them are sound.

The framework takes existential risk seriously not because it is fashionable in some tech-philanthropic circles but because the analysis, applied honestly, generates the conclusion that these are among the most important issues of our time.

## The Personal Question

What should individuals do about existential risk? This is a harder question than it might seem.

**The naive answer** is: "nothing, because your individual contribution is negligible." This is wrong because the same logic applies to voting, to most philanthropy, to most political action. If "individual contributions don't matter" is accepted as a general principle, almost all civic engagement becomes pointless. The framework rejects this conclusion.

**A better answer**: individuals can contribute in several ways.

**1. Financial support.** Research on existential risks is chronically underfunded. Donations to organizations working on AI safety, biosecurity, pandemic preparedness, nuclear risk reduction, and similar areas can have high marginal impact. Because the field is small, a dollar donated now has more effect than a dollar donated to larger, better-funded causes.

**2. Career choice.** For people with relevant skills, direct work on existential risk reduction is extraordinarily high-impact. AI safety researchers, biosecurity experts, international relations specialists focused on nuclear issues, policy analysts working on long-term problems — these are careers that can significantly influence outcomes. Not everyone can do them, but the people who can should at least consider them.

**3. Political engagement.** Voting for candidates who take long-term risks seriously. Lobbying for funding, institutional capacity, international cooperation. Building public awareness. Political change is slow, but sustained pressure over time can shift priorities.

**4. Epistemic contributions.** Taking the topics seriously in your own thinking. Reading and engaging with the research. Correcting misinformation when you encounter it. Not dismissing concerns as "doomerism" or treating them as certainties. The general level of epistemic quality in existential risk discourse matters, and every participant contributes to it.

**5. Not making things worse.** Many of the risks are concentrated in specific activities — dangerous research, escalatory political postures, institutional choices that weaken long-term thinking. Individuals who work in these areas can influence their direction from within, and individuals outside them can pressure institutions to be more responsible.

This is not a demand for total dedication. Most people's lives will quite properly be focused on nearer concerns — family, community, career, ordinary happiness. The framework does not demand that everyone become an existential risk professional. What it does ask is that existential risk be included in the broader moral calculus, rather than systematically ignored because it is unfamiliar or distant.

## The Stoic Attitude

Worrying about existential risk is psychologically difficult. The scale is overwhelming, the outcomes are terrifying, the uncertainty is high, and the amount an individual can do is modest. The natural responses -- paralytic anxiety or defensive dismissal -- are both unproductive.

The Stoic approach from Chapter 16 applies here. The distinction between what is and is not up to you. You cannot personally prevent an asteroid strike or a pandemic or an AI catastrophe. You can contribute to the collective effort, hold informed views, support the institutions that address these problems, and maintain the psychological stability needed to continue caring over the long term. You cannot carry the weight of humanity's future alone, and you should not try.

The right attitude is serious without being desperate, engaged without being obsessive, hopeful without being complacent. These risks are real, and they deserve our attention. They do not deserve our paralysis or our despair. Humans have survived many difficulties and may survive these as well. The future is not written. The work we do now affects which future actually comes to be. That is enough to motivate action without requiring certainty about outcomes.

## Summary

1. **Existential risk operates at astronomical scale.** The long future of humanity, if it happens, contains the vast majority of beings who will ever live. Preventing existential catastrophe preserves enormous value.

2. **The main categories of risk** include AI misalignment, engineered pandemics, nuclear war, severe climate change, permanent totalitarianism, and unknown unknowns.

3. **The astronomical value argument** justifies substantial attention to existential risk, subject to qualifications about fanaticism, uncertainty, and current suffering.

4. **Existential risk reduction is a global public good** and faces serious coordination problems that require international cooperation, long-term thinking, and institutional capacity.

5. **Common objections** come from left, right, and center, and each has some force but none defeats the core case.

6. **Individuals can contribute** through financial support, career choice, political engagement, epistemic contributions, and avoiding harmful actions.

7. **The Stoic attitude** is appropriate: serious, engaged, hopeful, but not desperate or obsessive.

The next chapter addresses the specific category of existential risk most debated today: artificial intelligence. It is the area where the framework's tools are most stretched and where uncertainty is highest, but it is also the area where the stakes are greatest.
