# Chapter 38: Against Nihilism and Moral Anti-Realism

---

> *"If there is no God, everything is permitted."*
> — attributed to Dostoevsky's *Brothers Karamazov* (an accurate compression of a more nuanced position)

---

## The Rival

Nihilism is the view that nothing really matters. Values are illusions. Moral claims are either false or meaningless. What we call "morality" is just human preference, cultural convention, or evolutionary residue — not something that tracks any objective feature of reality.

Moral anti-realism is the philosophical family to which nihilism belongs. Anti-realists hold that there are no mind-independent moral facts. Different forms of anti-realism vary in their specifics:

- **Error theory** (J.L. Mackie): Moral statements are truth-apt (they can be true or false) but they are systematically false, because they purport to refer to objective moral facts that do not exist.
- **Expressivism / non-cognitivism**: Moral statements are not truth-apt at all. When you say "murder is wrong," you are not making a factual claim; you are expressing an attitude, issuing a command, or displaying an emotion.
- **Moral fictionalism**: We should engage in moral discourse as a useful fiction, knowing that the claims are not literally true but finding value in the fiction.
- **Constructivism**: Moral facts exist but are constructed by rational agents (Rawls, Korsgaard), not discovered. This is often positioned between realism and anti-realism.

This chapter engages with the strongest versions of anti-realism. The framework's own position — which we have called Constructivist Realism (Chapter 1) — is closer to some forms of constructivism than to strict realism, but we take seriously the challenge that even constructivism may not be enough to ground morality robustly.

If any form of moral anti-realism is correct, the framework we are building is a kind of useful fiction at best. We would then need to face the nihilistic question: *if nothing really matters, why spend so much effort on any of this?*

This chapter argues that nihilism is false -- not that it is emotionally uncomfortable (which would be a weak argument), but that it is philosophically incoherent and empirically wrong about features of reality that matter in a sense that cannot be dismissed.

## Steel-Manning Mackie's Error Theory

The strongest and most interesting form of moral anti-realism is J.L. Mackie's error theory from *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977). Mackie was not an emotive nihilist; he was a sophisticated philosopher who thought carefully about what moral claims purport to mean and concluded that they could not be true.

Mackie's argument has two parts:

**1. The argument from queerness.** If moral properties existed, they would be "queer" — utterly unlike any other properties in the universe. They would have to be simultaneously:
- Descriptive (telling us how things are): "this action is wrong"
- Prescriptive (telling us what to do): "therefore, do not do it"
- Motivational (actually moving us to act)
- Intrinsic to certain acts or states (not just in the eye of the beholder)
- Knowable somehow (we have moral knowledge)

Mackie argues that nothing else in the universe has this combination of features. Physical properties (mass, charge, position) are descriptive but not prescriptive. Preferences are motivational but not intrinsic. Logical truths are objective but not action-guiding. The combination that moral properties supposedly have appears nowhere else, which is evidence that it doesn't exist.

**2. The argument from relativity.** Moral disagreement is widespread and persistent. Different cultures, different eras, different individuals reach very different moral conclusions, often with equal confidence. If there were objective moral facts, we would expect convergence over time as people investigated them more carefully. Instead, we see enduring disagreement. The best explanation is that moral claims are not tracking anything mind-independent.

These are serious arguments. They have forced generations of philosophers to think carefully about whether moral realism can be defended.

**Mackie's own position**: moral claims are false because they purport to refer to objective properties that do not exist, but we can still engage in moral reasoning as a kind of collective project — constructing useful norms, without pretending they correspond to a moral reality outside ourselves.

This is more sophisticated than popular nihilism. It does not say "do whatever you want." It says "you can still reason about how to live, but don't confuse your conclusions with discoveries about objective moral facts." The practical effect may be similar to moderate moral reasoning, but the philosophical status is very different.

## The Framework's Response to Queerness

The argument from queerness has force if moral properties really would have to be utterly unlike anything else in the universe. The framework's response: **in our version, they aren't**.

Recall our position from Chapter 3: **valence is a physical property of conscious systems**. Suffering and joy are not free-floating normative entities hovering above reality. They are specific states of information-processing systems that happen to be conscious, with physical correlates that can be studied scientifically. They are as real as temperature or pressure.

What makes valence *morally* significant is that conscious beings intrinsically care about their own valence. Suffering is a state the being experiencing it wants to escape. Joy is a state the being wants to continue. This "intrinsic wanting" is not a mystical property; it is a fact about how conscious experience works. Pain hurts; hurting motivates; motivation produces action. These are ordinary facts about biology and psychology, not queer metaphysical claims.

Now, the move from "conscious beings intrinsically want to avoid their own suffering" to "suffering in general ought to be reduced" is the crucial step. This is where the framework's axiom enters. We make that move explicitly, recognizing that it is an axiom that must be chosen (Chapter 3). But the axiom is not a queer metaphysical entity; it is a normative commitment grounded in the empirical fact that suffering is a real, aversive state of conscious beings.

The Mackie counter-argument would be: "You are still using 'ought' and 'reduce.' Where does the 'ought' come from? If it is just a human decision, then it has no objective force, and your axiom is just a preference dressed up as a foundation."

The framework's response: **the axiom is not purely subjective**. It is a commitment, yes, but it is a commitment to something that every conscious being already demonstrates through its behavior. Every conscious being treats its own suffering as mattering. We generalize this through reflection — if my suffering matters to me, by what principle do other beings' suffering matter less? — and the generalization does not require any additional metaphysical fact. It requires only consistency and the recognition that the feature that makes my suffering matter (its phenomenal aversiveness) is present in other beings too.

This is not fully objective in the sense Mackie demands. But it is also not fully subjective. It is a form of grounding that sits between Mackie's two options, and the framework believes it is sufficient to defeat his argument as applied to us.

## The Argument from Relativity, Revisited

Mackie's second argument — that persistent moral disagreement is evidence against moral objectivity — deserves separate response.

The argument has some force. If there were straightforward moral facts that careful inquiry would reveal, we would expect more convergence than we observe. But the argument proves less than Mackie thought:

**1. Disagreement about empirical facts is also widespread and persistent.** People disagree about climate science, the causes of economic recessions, the historical details of events they witnessed. Disagreement does not prove that the underlying questions lack answers; it proves that the questions are hard or that the reasoning is difficult or that motivated reasoning is pervasive. The same applies to moral disagreement.

**2. There is more moral agreement than Mackie allowed.** Across cultures and traditions, we find remarkable convergence on core moral claims: don't murder, don't steal, don't lie gratuitously, care for your children, keep your promises, reciprocate kindness, avoid cruelty, protect the vulnerable. The disagreement is mostly about edge cases, applications, and priorities -- not about foundations. This is more convergence than we would expect if moral claims were entirely made up.

**3. Moral progress has happened.** Slavery is almost universally condemned now. Women's basic rights are almost universally accepted (in principle if not always in practice). Torture, arbitrary imprisonment, and deliberate cruelty are more widely rejected than in the past. If moral claims were purely subjective, this progress would be unintelligible — what would "progress" even mean? But if there are moral facts, even uncertain ones, moral progress becomes the process of getting closer to them. The existence of progress is itself evidence for some form of moral realism.

**4. The specific pattern of disagreement is explicable.** People disagree more when self-interest is at stake, when tribalism is activated, when information is poor, when moral reasoning is bypassed by fast-system intuitions. This is exactly what we would expect if there are real moral facts that people often fail to see because of predictable cognitive failures. It is not what we would expect if disagreement were intrinsic to the domain.

**5. Where moral disagreement is deep, it is often about the weights of different considerations, not about what counts at all.** People disagree about how much weight to give the individual versus the community, about the trade-offs between liberty and equality, about the status of animals or future generations. But they often agree that these are the relevant considerations. The disagreement about weights is real, but it is not the same as disagreement about what exists.

The framework's position: moral disagreement is real, significant, and worth taking seriously, but it does not establish that morality is not objective. It establishes that moral reasoning is difficult and that human psychology is often hostile to it. Both conclusions are consistent with the framework, which has been arguing exactly this from Chapter 2 onward.

## The Performative Incoherence Argument

A different line of argument against nihilism is the one introduced in Chapter 3 and worth developing here: **nihilism cannot be sincerely lived**.

The argument: suppose you accept that nothing really matters. What do you do with that belief? The options:

**1. Ignore the belief and continue living as if things matter.** Many soft nihilists take this route. They intellectually affirm nihilism but practically act as if suffering, love, justice, and truth are important. This is possible but philosophically unstable — they are not really living by their stated belief.

**2. Try to genuinely live as if nothing matters.** Some have tried. The results are not encouraging. People who sincerely internalize radical nihilism tend toward either paralysis (why bother doing anything?) or pure hedonism (why not just take whatever pleasure I can?) or destruction (why should others be happier than I am if nothing matters?). None of these outcomes is stable, and the people who reach them typically either change their view or deteriorate psychologically.

**3. Claim that nihilism is the correct view but that rational choice still leads to moral-looking behavior.** This is Mackie's position, more or less. "Nothing objectively matters, but we can construct norms that serve our interests and call them 'morality.'" But notice: this is essentially the framework's position, just with different terminology. We say moral facts are grounded in features of reality (valence, coordination) that we care about. The sophisticated nihilist says we can construct norms around things we care about. The practical difference is minimal.

The framework's claim is that moral discourse, correctly understood, is compatible with the sophisticated nihilist's view. What we are calling "moral facts" are facts about what produces welfare and what defeats coordination failure — facts that any rational agent would care about, whether or not they want to call their caring "moral realism." The debate becomes terminological rather than substantive.

But the strong nihilist — the one who really denies that anything matters — cannot live by the view. It is performatively incoherent: the act of taking any action is incompatible with the belief that nothing matters. The nihilist who eats, who sleeps, who talks, who walks is demonstrating through behavior that at least something matters to them — their own welfare, their own continuation, their own experience. Denying this is possible only in the abstract, not in lived practice.

This is not a proof that nihilism is false. A sufficiently determined philosopher can maintain the view intellectually while acting as if it were false. But it is strong evidence that nihilism is not a *livable* position. A view that undermines the activity it is part of has something wrong with it.

## The Asymmetry Argument

Here is another line. Consider the two possibilities:

**Possibility A: Nihilism is true.** Nothing really matters. Our actions have no moral weight. What we do is, in an objective sense, indifferent.

**Possibility B: Morality is real.** Valence matters. Suffering is bad. Flourishing is good. What we do has real moral weight.

Now consider the expected consequences of believing each possibility:

**If you believe A (nihilism) and A is true**: you live consistently with reality. But you also live without meaning, without moral commitments, without reasons to resist evil or pursue good. The life is internally consistent but phenomenologically impoverished.

**If you believe A (nihilism) and B is true**: you are making a serious mistake. You are failing to act on things that really matter. You are allowing suffering you could prevent, failing to pursue goods you could create. The error is serious and affects real beings.

**If you believe B (morality is real) and B is true**: you are living in accordance with reality. Your moral efforts are directed at real features of the universe. Your life has meaning and your actions have weight.

**If you believe B (morality is real) and A is true**: you are making an intellectual mistake, but your behavior is indistinguishable from the behavior you would have under sophisticated nihilism. You are constructing norms that serve what you care about, just under a different label.

The asymmetry: the cost of wrongly believing in nihilism is much higher than the cost of wrongly believing in morality. If morality is real and you deny it, you miss something important. If nihilism is true and you believe in morality, you lose little — your behavior would have been similar either way, and the "belief" in morality carries no extra cost beyond the terminology.

This is a Pascal-style argument, and it has the usual weaknesses of such arguments. It is not a proof. It does not tell you that morality is objective; it tells you that acting as if it were is a better bet than acting as if it were not. But for practical purposes, that is enough. The framework's claim is not that the metaphysical question is settled. The claim is that **even under serious uncertainty about the metaphysics, the practical conclusion remains the same: act as if morality is real, because the asymmetric costs make that the better bet**.

## The Framework Is Not Threatened by Anti-Realism

Let us face something directly. Several of the most serious forms of moral anti-realism do not actually threaten the framework as much as the rhetoric suggests.

**Expressivism**: the view that moral statements express attitudes rather than facts. The framework's analysis can accommodate this. Our moral claims *do* involve attitudes — attitudes toward suffering, toward flourishing, toward coordination and cooperation. The disagreement with expressivism is about whether these attitudes can be rationally evaluated and whether they track objective features of reality. On both questions, the framework's answer is "yes, at least partly" — but the practical implications are similar to what a sophisticated expressivist would endorse.

**Constructivism**: the view that moral facts are constructed by rational agents. The framework is explicitly constructivist in certain respects. We say morality is constructed in response to objective features of reality (Chapter 1). A constructivist who understood our position would have little to object to.

**Moral fictionalism**: the view that moral discourse is a useful fiction. The framework disagrees with the "fiction" framing but can accept the "useful" part. If someone wants to call the framework's moral claims "useful fictions," the practical content remains.

The form of anti-realism that genuinely threatens the framework is the strong version — the view that moral claims are simply false, that nothing matters, that any construction is arbitrary. This version is harder to make work than it looks, for the reasons above. Most serious "anti-realists" in contemporary philosophy actually hold positions that are closer to the framework than to strong nihilism, and the disagreement with them is often terminological.

## The Practical Stakes

This chapter is not purely academic. Nihilism has real-world consequences, and they are worth acknowledging.

People who believe nothing matters act differently from people who believe things matter. Not always — some strong nihilists maintain moral behavior through habit, social pressure, or inconsistency. But on average, across populations, belief in objective morality correlates with more prosocial behavior than belief in its absence. This is not surprising; it is what we would expect if beliefs affect behavior.

The 20th century provides grim examples. Ideologies that abandoned the idea of objective moral constraints — whether Nazism's appeal to will and power, Stalinism's willingness to subordinate all ethics to historical materialism, or various forms of cultural nihilism — produced atrocities on scales that had not been seen before. This is not proof that morality must be objective, but it is evidence that abandoning moral objectivity has practical costs.

The framework's practical position: **even if the metaphysical question is unsettled, we should teach, practice, and defend moral objectivity, because the alternative has a bad track record**. This is not anti-intellectual; it is practical wisdom informed by history.

## The Strongest Contemporary Challenge: Street's Darwinian Dilemma

The most sophisticated contemporary anti-realist argument comes from Sharon Street's "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006). Street argues that natural selection has shaped our evaluative attitudes — our sense of what is good, bad, fair, cruel — and that this creates a dilemma for anyone who claims these attitudes track mind-independent moral truths.

**Horn 1:** If natural selection *did* track moral truth in shaping our evaluative attitudes, this is a massive coincidence that realists cannot explain — why would a process optimizing for reproductive fitness happen to install beliefs that track a completely separate domain of moral facts?

**Horn 2:** If natural selection *did not* track moral truth, then our evaluative attitudes are unreliable guides to the moral facts, and realism collapses into skepticism — we have no access to the moral truths we claim exist.

This is a more precise version of the evolutionary debunking argument we engaged in Chapter 4, and it targets the framework's valence realism directly. The framework's response (developed fully in CH-04's debunking dilemma section): the valence axiom is not an evaluative attitude in Street's sense — it is a claim about the physical reality of conscious states. Evolution explains *why* we have access to suffering (consciousness was selected for), without undermining the reality of what we access. The thermometer analogy: the thermometer was built by a process that doesn't care about temperature, but the readings are still accurate because they are causally connected to the phenomenon they measure. Similarly, our awareness of suffering is causally connected to suffering itself. Street's dilemma applies to free-floating evaluative attitudes; it does not apply to claims about the physical properties of conscious systems.

This response works if valence realism is correct — if suffering really is a physical property. If the anti-realist rejects this (as illusionists like Dennett and Frankish do), the argument gets harder. Chapter 3 and Chapter 5 address that contingency.

## What Anti-Realists Get Right

As with DCT, we should acknowledge what serious anti-realists get right, because the insights are worth preserving.

**1. Moral claims often exceed their epistemic warrant.** People express confident moral judgments about complex questions with inadequate evidence and reflection. The anti-realist critique that much moral discourse is unsupported is often correct. Chapter 2's epistemic discipline is one response.

**2. Moral intuitions are influenced by non-moral factors.** Culture, tribalism, self-interest, motivated reasoning. These shape our moral views in ways we often don't notice. Anti-realists are right to point this out. The framework agrees (see Chapters 2 and 4).

**3. "Moral facts" have a peculiar status.** They are not like physical facts. They are not discovered the way scientific facts are. They have an irreducibly normative dimension that makes them different from ordinary facts. Anti-realists are right to flag this difference, even if we disagree about its implications.

**4. Much of what is called "morality" is tribalism in disguise.** Group loyalty, outgroup hostility, status signaling. A lot of moral discourse is about these things rather than about genuine moral questions. Anti-realists are right to be suspicious.

**5. Dogmatic moral certainty is unearned.** The confidence that some moralists bring to contested questions is not justified by the evidence or reasoning they can produce. Anti-realists correctly expose this overreach.

The framework accepts all of these points and builds them into its epistemology. What it rejects is the conclusion that moral discourse is therefore meaningless or that nothing matters. The correct response to these insights is better moral reasoning, not moral abandonment.

## Summary

1. **Moral anti-realism comes in many forms**, from strong nihilism to sophisticated constructivism. The strongest challenge is Mackie's error theory.

2. **The argument from queerness** loses force against the framework's version of realism, in which moral facts are grounded in the physical property of valence rather than in free-floating normative entities.

3. **The argument from relativity** shows that moral reasoning is hard, not that it is impossible. Moral disagreement has explanations consistent with moral realism, and there is more convergence than Mackie allowed.

4. **Nihilism is performatively incoherent.** It cannot be sincerely lived. The act of taking any action is incompatible with the belief that nothing matters.

5. **The asymmetry argument** favors belief in moral realism over nihilism on practical grounds: the cost of wrongly believing in nihilism is much higher than the cost of wrongly believing in morality.

6. **Most sophisticated anti-realism is actually close to the framework's position.** Expressivism, constructivism, and moral fictionalism can often be accommodated with terminological adjustments. The framework's serious disagreement is with strong nihilism only.

7. **Nihilism has bad practical consequences.** Belief in objective morality correlates with prosocial behavior, and abandoning moral objectivity has historical costs.

8. **Anti-realists get real things right** about the limits of moral knowledge and the influence of non-moral factors on moral beliefs. These insights should be absorbed into better moral reasoning, not used as grounds for moral abandonment.
