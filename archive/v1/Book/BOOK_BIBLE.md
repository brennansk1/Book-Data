## Source Vision

The book builds the **best possible moral system** — called **Constructivist Realism** (working name, subject to revision). The Framework (v12.0) is the seed, not the ceiling. The system:

1. **Grounds ethics** in epistemic duty, valence realism, and game theory
2. **Provides a decision architecture** — heuristic rules by default, consequentialist override in crisis
3. **Derives comprehensive political philosophy** from first principles (8 chapters, not a sidebar)
4. **Derives comprehensive economic philosophy** from first principles (6 chapters, not a sidebar)
5. **Addresses frontier problems** (AI, bioethics, environment, digital rights)
6. **Defeats rival systems** in head-to-head debate (10 dedicated chapters)
7. **Self-corrects** via the Position Registry swap mechanism

The system wins by being the most honest, comprehensive, and empirically grounded — not by ignoring the best ideas from rival traditions.

### CH-01: The Third Way — Why We Need a New Moral System
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-03-28 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** The two dominant moral frameworks — theistic grounding and nihilistic rejection — have both failed. Theism provides structure but rests on unverifiable foundations with no error-correction mechanism. Nihilism correctly diagnoses theism's weakness but offers no replacement, licensing atrocity through indifference. Constructivist Realism is the proposed Third Way: values are constructed by conscious agents but constrained by three objective features of reality — the physics of consciousness (valence), the mathematics of coordination (game theory), and the biology of the agent (evolutionary psychology).

**Key Terms Introduced:** Constructivist Realism, Theistic Map, Nihilistic Map, valence, Moloch (previewed), dual-process ethics (previewed), Mode A/Mode B (previewed), the seven specifications

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-M01 (Constructivist Realism) — introduced and given its foundational argument via the bridge-building analogy and the three constraint sources.

**Key Claims:**
- Every moral system requires at least one axiom; ours is "the suffering of a conscious being matters"
- This axiom is the most defensible available because denying it is performatively incoherent
- The system has seven minimum specifications: grounding, decision procedure, coordination, error-correction, livability, comprehensiveness, and competitive robustness
- No existing system meets all seven simultaneously

**Depends On:** None (this is the entry point)

**Depended On By:** All subsequent chapters — sets the frame, the axiom, the ambition, and the intellectual contract with the reader

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** The is-ought gap (OQ-001). Chapter concedes Hume was right that you can't derive ought from is, then argues the system doesn't attempt this — it is explicit about where the normative axiom enters and defends it via performative incoherence of denial.

**Known Weaknesses:** The performative incoherence argument is suggestive but not a strict logical proof. A sophisticated error theorist (Mackie) could accept that people act as if suffering matters while denying it is objectively normative. This needs deeper treatment in CH-03.

**Open Threads:** The valence axiom is previewed but not fully defended (CH-03's job). The Moloch diagnosis is previewed but not developed (CH-06). The dual-process architecture is previewed but not argued for (CH-09/10).

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-02: The Map and the Territory — Epistemology as Moral Duty
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-03-28 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** If suffering matters (Ch. 3's axiom), then seeing reality clearly is a moral obligation, because actions based on false beliefs produce unnecessary suffering. Human cognition is systematically biased (Kahneman's System 1/2), strategically self-deceiving (Simler & Hanson's hidden motives), and post-hoc rationalizing (Haidt's elephant and rider). The scout mindset (Galef) — treating beliefs as maps to be refined rather than fortresses to be defended — is the required epistemic posture. Bayesian updating is the formal method: hold beliefs as calibrated probabilities and update on evidence.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Epistemic duty, System 1/System 2, WYSIATI, anchoring, availability, representativeness, scout vs. soldier mindset, Bayesian updating, calibration, the planning fallacy, the outside view, the premortem, the identity trap, the twelve epistemic virtues

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-E01 (Epistemic Duty) — fully argued. POS-E02 (Bayesian Updating) — defended against three objections (subjective priors, impracticality, and inapplicability to axioms).

**Key Claims:**
- Truth-seeking is a moral obligation, not an intellectual preference, because false beliefs cause unnecessary suffering
- Human cognition is systematically biased in predictable directions (not random error)
- Self-deception is an evolved strategy, not a bug — making it harder to overcome
- The modern world has changed the payoff structure: the cost of false beliefs is now catastrophic
- Beliefs fused with identity become unfalsifiable — the identity trap is the deepest obstacle to epistemic duty
- The Position Registry is an institutional antibody against the identity trap at the system level

**Depends On:** CH-01 (project frame), CH-03 (valence axiom — suffering matters is the premise for epistemic duty)

**Depended On By:** CH-04 (needs epistemic framework to evaluate evolutionary instincts), CH-09/10 (dual-process ethics draws on dual-process cognition), all later chapters (epistemic standards apply throughout)

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** The objection that Bayesian priors are subjective — rebutted by showing that Bayesian updating is self-correcting over time (convergence property). Also addresses the "cold Vulcan" objection: epistemic duty does not require emotionlessness; emotions are data.

**Known Weaknesses:** The argument that "the modern world rewards accuracy" (Galef) may be overstated — in many social contexts, soldier mindset still pays. The twelve virtues list is inherited from the Framework and could benefit from deeper grounding. The chapter assumes readers accept the valence axiom from Ch. 3 — hostile readers may find the moral case for epistemology circular.

**Open Threads:** The relationship between individual epistemic duty and institutional epistemology (prediction markets, free speech, scientific method) is previewed but not developed — these are for Book V (CH-20, CH-21) and Book II (CH-08).

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-05: The Hard Problems — Consciousness, Free Will, and What We Don't Know
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-04 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Book I ends with a deliberate confession of ignorance on three hard problems: (1) The hard problem of consciousness — we have built our moral system on consciousness-based value but we cannot explain, detect, or reliably identify consciousness in systems that differ from our own. (2) Free will — metaphysical libertarian freedom is probably incompatible with physics, but compatibilist and consequentialist rescues preserve enough of our moral practice to proceed. (3) Our own moral blindness — the historical track record shows that every moral framework in history has been blind to something its successors could see. The response to all three is not manufactured confidence but institutional humility: asymmetric caution under uncertainty, confidence-weighted moral consideration, and a system architecture (the Position Registry, the swap mechanism) designed to outgrow its current limitations.

**Key Terms Introduced:** The hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers), philosophical zombies, Integrated Information Theory (IIT), Global Workspace Theory, biological naturalism, higher-order theories, substrate-independence, asymmetric cost reasoning, compatibilism, Bostrom's "Disneyland without children," motivated unconsciousness-attribution, the three hard problems as the edges of Book I

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-F03 (Consciousness-Based Moral Circle) — deepened with the uncertainty principles (asymmetric cost, confidence-weighted consideration, motivated-denial caution, provisional substrate-independence). No new positions established — this chapter is largely a confession of what the system does not yet know.

**Key Claims:**
- The hard problem of consciousness is unsolved and may remain so for a long time
- No existing theory of consciousness (IIT, GWT, biological naturalism, higher-order) is established well enough to ground moral practice
- Under consciousness uncertainty, asymmetric cost reasoning favors inclusion
- Compatibilism and forward-looking consequentialism preserve enough of moral practice to proceed without resolving free will metaphysically
- Free will's probable non-existence in the libertarian sense softens both judgment of wrongdoers and self-congratulation about virtue
- Historical track record of moral blindness (slavery, women's subordination, animal suffering) shows current moral frameworks almost certainly have analogous blind spots
- Candidate current blindnesses: non-charismatic animals, future generations, digital minds, unknown categories
- Intellectual honesty about the limits of the system is a structural feature, not a weakness

**Depends On:** CH-01 (the project's intellectual contract includes admitting uncertainty), CH-02 (epistemic humility as a virtue), CH-03 (the valence foundation is what makes consciousness uncertainty consequential), CH-04 (the Alien God framing suggests we should expect to have been misled)

**Depended On By:** All frontier chapters (CH-31 through CH-35), especially CH-32 (AI ethics) and CH-34 (environmental ethics / animal suffering). The confessed uncertainty here is what gives later chapters permission to handle provisional positions.

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "A moral system that admits it doesn't know the answers to basic questions (what is consciousness, is free will real) cannot ground action." Rebutted by: (a) operation under uncertainty is possible via asymmetric cost reasoning and confidence-weighted consideration; (b) pretending to have answers creates catastrophic brittleness; (c) the practical consequences of the hard problems are tractable even while the metaphysical problems remain open.

**Known Weaknesses:** This chapter offers no positive theory of consciousness. It leaves the free will question formally unresolved. It cannot enumerate our current blindnesses because by definition we cannot see them. The asymmetric cost principle depends on value-vector tradeoffs that become unstable at extreme probabilities. The relationship between compatibilism and retributive justice is gestured at but not worked out (deferred to CH-22).

**Open Threads:** Full treatment of animal ethics (CH-34 or new chapter). AI consciousness criteria (CH-32). The implications of diminished free will for criminal justice (CH-22). The capability-based expansion of moral circle to include future generations and distributional questions across time (CH-30, CH-31).

**Position Swaps:** None

**Book I Closing Note:** With this chapter, Book I (Foundations) is fully drafted. All five chapters now exist in Drafts/: CH-01, CH-02, CH-03, CH-04, CH-05. The metaphysical and epistemic bedrock is laid. Next phase: Book II (The Enemy — game theory and coordination failure), starting with CH-06 (Moloch).

### CH-06: Moloch — The God of Coordination Failure
**Status:** REVISED | **Date:** 2026-04-11 | **Revision:** 2

**Core Argument:** Most unnecessary suffering is not caused by bad people but by ordinary people trapped in coordination failures. This phenomenon has a formal structure (Prisoner's Dilemma, Tragedy of the Commons, Stag Hunt) and a name: Moloch. Morality is the technology we invent to defeat Moloch. **Second edition addition: "What Moloch Cannot Explain"** — a major new section explicitly bounding the game-theoretic frame. Three categories of moral phenomena operate outside game theory: (1) **Care and dependency** — asymmetric relationships (parent-child, caregiver-dependent) where one party cannot play the game; Tronto's four elements of care and Kittay's derivative dependency establish care as the substrate on which coordination operates. (2) **Supererogation** — acts beyond what strategic analysis can generate, explained by the virtue layer. (3) **Expressive rationality** — action aimed at expressing values rather than producing outcomes (Brennan & Lomasky). The full moral system requires game theory for coordination, care ethics for dependency, virtue for supererogation, expressive rationality for meaning.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Moloch, Nash equilibrium, PD, Tragedy of the Commons, Stag Hunt, shadow of the future, morality as anti-Moloch technology, **care and dependency (Tronto, Kittay), derivative dependency, supererogation, expressive rationality (Brennan & Lomasky)**

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-A02 (Morality as Coordination Technology — Bounded) — v2.0. Game theory is the primary tool for coordination failures but explicitly bounded; three non-coordination categories named. Vulnerability LOW.

**Key Claims:**
- Most suffering is structural — Moloch, not malice
- Core moral rules are coordination devices
- **Game theory does not capture care, supererogation, or expressive action**
- Care is the substrate on which coordination operates (caring agents before cooperating agents)
- A system that claims one tool explains everything is lying; honest bounding makes the system stronger

**Depends On:** CH-01-05 (Book I foundations), CH-03 (valence axiom)

**Depended On By:** CH-07, CH-08, **CH-11 (care virtues connect back to CH-06's care discussion), CH-14 (dependency section connects back)**, all applied chapters

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Game theory reduces morality to strategic calculation." Now directly addressed: three categories of moral life are explicitly outside the game-theoretic frame. The claim is bounded honestly.

**Known Weaknesses:** "Most" suffering as coordination failure remains unquantified. The three non-coordination categories could potentially be expanded (aesthetic duties, duties to the dead). The care/coordination interface needs further development in applied chapters.

**Narrative Bridge (2026-04-11):** Added opening transition paragraphs connecting Book I foundations to Book II's central question — explicitly framing why understanding the cause of suffering is the necessary next step after establishing that suffering matters.

**Position Swaps:** POS-A02 v1.0 → v2.0 (vulnerability MEDIUM → LOW)

### CH-07: The Physics of Trust — Cooperation, Defection, and the Shadow of the Future
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-04 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Axelrod's iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments demonstrated that TIT FOR TAT — the simplest possible reciprocal strategy — beats every more sophisticated strategy across a wide range of environments. Its four properties (nice, retaliatory, forgiving, clear) are not arbitrary virtues but the mathematically necessary features of any strategy that can sustain cooperation in repeated interactions. The "shadow of the future" (Axelrod's parameter w) determines when cooperation is stable. Schelling's focal points explain how coordination succeeds without explicit communication. Costly signaling (Zahavi) explains why integrity must hurt to be credible. Together, these give us the physics of trust: a technical account of how cooperation emerges, stabilizes, and defends itself against invasion.

**Key Terms Introduced:** TIT FOR TAT, four properties (nice/retaliatory/forgiving/clear), shadow of the future, discount parameter w, collective stability, Stag Hunt, assurance, Schelling point / focal point, costly signaling, handicap principle, integrity as commitment device

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-A02 (continued development) — moves from the bare claim that morality solves coordination to the specific game-theoretic mechanisms by which it does so.

**Key Claims:**
- Simple reciprocal strategies beat complex exploitative ones in iterated games
- The four properties of successful strategies explain why certain virtues (honesty, measured retaliation, forgiveness, integrity) are universal across successful moral traditions
- The shadow of the future is the single most important variable determining whether cooperation is sustainable
- Integrity is a costly signal — the cost is the feature, not the bug
- Rules (in the Mode A sense to be developed in Book III) are game-theoretically necessary for trustworthiness, not merely concessions to cognitive limits

**Depends On:** CH-06 (introduces Moloch and the coordination problem), CH-02 (epistemic framework), CH-04 (evolutionary grounding for why reciprocity instincts exist)

**Depended On By:** CH-08 (mechanism design implements these principles), CH-09/10 (dual-process ethics uses the rule-based cooperation argument as core justification for Mode A), CH-11 (the virtues), all applied chapters

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Rules are just for cognitively limited agents — a perfect reasoner would always calculate from scratch." Rebutted via Schelling: even perfect reasoners cannot be trusted if they are opaque. Rules are how you become coordinable with others, not merely how you cope with your own computational limits.

**Known Weaknesses:** The tournament results are robust but not universal — some environments favor different strategies (e.g., where memory is noisy, GENEROUS TIT FOR TAT outperforms strict TIT FOR TAT). The WWI example is dramatic but atypical. The Schelling point analysis could be accused of being more descriptive than prescriptive.

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-08: Mechanism Design — Engineering the Escape from Moloch
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-04 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Mechanism design is the core engineering discipline for defeating Moloch. The classic market-vs-state dichotomy is a false choice — Elinor Ostrom demonstrated empirically that community self-governance is a viable third option, with eight design principles characterizing the successful cases (boundaries, congruence, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, rights to organize, nested enterprises). The Valencia huertas achieved astonishing compliance rates (infraction rate 0.008) over 600 years without central enforcement. No single mechanism is universal — different coordination problems require different tools: markets, state regulation, community governance, commitment devices (Schelling), prediction markets. The skill of mechanism design is diagnosis — matching the tool to the problem.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Mechanism design, Pigovian tax, internalization of externalities, Ostrom's eight design principles, common-pool resources (CPRs), huertas, Tribunal de las Aguas, metis (James C. Scott), high modernism, commitment devices, prediction markets, polycentric governance, nested enterprises

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-P02 (Institutional Mechanism Design over Ideology) — fully argued with empirical grounding in Ostrom's work. POS-EC02 (Pigovian Correction) — introduced here and developed further in Book VI. POS-EC04 (Prediction Markets) — introduced.

**Key Claims:**
- Mechanism design reframes moral/political philosophy as engineering of incentive structures
- Hardin's Tragedy of the Commons is empirically refuted by Ostrom's case studies of long-enduring self-governing systems
- The eight design principles are empirical regularities distilled from centuries of institutional wisdom
- Scott's critique of high modernism shows why state-imposed simplification destroys the local knowledge systems depend on
- Schelling's commitment devices show that constraints can be sources of power, not just limitations
- Prediction markets are epistemic mechanisms for extracting accurate information from populations

**Depends On:** CH-06 (Moloch), CH-07 (physics of trust), CH-02 (epistemic framework for prediction markets)

**Depended On By:** All applied chapters, especially Book V (political philosophy), Book VI (economics), and CH-30 (fiscal policy). CH-17 and CH-18 depend heavily on the mechanism design framework. CH-27 (externalities) applies the Pigovian analysis.

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** The libertarian objection "only markets and property rights" and the statist objection "only central regulation" — both rebutted by Ostrom's empirical demonstration of a third way.

**Known Weaknesses:** Ostrom's eight principles are not a complete theory — she explicitly warned against treating them as sufficient conditions. Scale is a genuine problem: Ostrom systems tend to work at village scale and face challenges at national/global scale (though nesting helps). The chapter does not fully engage with anarcho-capitalist arguments (Friedman) — deferred to CH-25/26. The integration of mechanism design with democratic legitimacy is gestured at but not worked out.

**Open Threads:** How to apply Ostrom's principles at the national and global scales (CH-17, CH-23). The relationship between prediction markets and electoral democracy (CH-20). The political economy of mechanism reform (CH-18).

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-09: The Chesterton Fence — Rules, Heuristics, and the Wisdom of Constraints
**Status:** REVISED | **Date:** 2026-04-11 | **Revision:** 1

**Core Argument:** Pure consequentialism fails in practice for five reasons: self-serving bias, computational cost, the coordination requirement, prediction uncertainty, and the slippery slope of exceptions. Rules solve these problems by being bias-resistant, cognitively cheap, predictability-producing, wisdom-encoding, and categorically defensible. The Chesterton Fence principle — don't destroy institutions you don't understand — captures the proper epistemic humility toward inherited moral rules. Rules are Schelling points, credible commitments, cognitive firmware, and wisdom-transmission devices. The best current rules are those that persist across traditions (honesty, non-aggression, property, promise-keeping, reciprocity, care for the vulnerable, avoiding cruelty). The second-order rule: follow your tradition's rules because they encode wisdom you don't have, but revise when you can articulate why they exist.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Chesterton Fence, Mode A, rules as Schelling points, rules as commitment devices, rules as cognitive firmware, second-order rule, indirect consequentialism (rule utilitarianism), five failure modes of consequentialism, four failure modes of rules

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-A01 (Dual-Process Decision Making) — first half developed with the positive case for rules.

**Key Claims:**
- Open-ended consequentialist calculation is the most powerful self-deception engine ever invented
- Rules make you coordinable in a way calculators cannot be
- Pre-committed rules are game-theoretically necessary even for perfectly rational agents
- The rules that persist across traditions are the reliable ones — not because tradition is infallible but because selection has filtered for them
- Rules have failure modes (wrong, conflicting, brittle, captured) that motivate Mode B

**Depends On:** CH-02 (biased cognition), CH-06-08 (game theory of coordination), CH-04 (reflective equilibrium process for evaluating inherited rules)

**Depended On By:** CH-10 (the override), CH-11 (virtues as rule-following character), all applied chapters (which depend on specific rules being defensible)

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Pure consequentialism is theoretically superior and rules are just concessions to cognitive limits." Rebutted by showing that even perfect reasoners would adopt rules for coordination reasons.

**Known Weaknesses:** The chapter defends rules-in-general but does not resolve which specific rules to prefer when traditions conflict. The claim that "core rules are similar across traditions" is roughly true but hides important variation. The Chesterton principle can be used to defend any inherited rule, including bad ones.

**Narrative Bridge (2026-04-11):** Added opening transition explicitly framing the shift from Book II (institutional diagnosis) to Book III (individual decision architecture) — "how should the game be designed?" gives way to "how should the player play?"

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-10: The Crisis Override — When to Break the Rules
**Status:** REVISED | **Date:** 2026-04-07 | **Revision:** 1

**Core Argument:** Mode B (consequentialist override) requires passing a **two-layer architecture**: First, three procedural gates (the Gating Protocol) that must be passed before the substantive conditions are even consulted: (i) bright-line trigger identification — the situation must involve imminent large-scale loss of life, irreversible systemic damage, or institutional capture disabling error-correction; (ii) mandatory deliberation period — 24h personal, longer institutional, exempt only for imminent physical danger; (iii) mandatory post-hoc review commitment — documentation and retrospective analysis, with pattern tracking. Only after all three gates are passed are the five substantive conditions consulted: (1) catastrophic irreversible harm, (2) attributable to rule, (3) last resort, (4) articulable to a Reasonable Reviewer (Yudkowsky), (5) self-interest absent. Historical test cases produce correct answers. The ticking-bomb case is rejected. The Gating Protocol resolves the mode-switching regress ("who decides?") by making the entry conditions bright-line and verifiable rather than dependent on the agent's self-assessment.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Gating Protocol, three gates (bright-line trigger, deliberation period, post-hoc review), Reasonable Reviewer (Yudkowsky), Inadequate Equilibria criteria, pattern tracking, Mode B, five conditions, skeptical third party test, public justification standard, asymmetric caution, moral luck

**Positions Established/Modified:** POS-A01 (Dual-Process Decision Making) — v2.0. Mode-switching regress resolved via Gating Protocol. Vulnerability LOW.

**Key Claims:**
- The mode-switching regress is resolved: bright-line gates decide whether Mode B consideration is available; conditions decide whether the override is warranted; post-hoc review provides accountability
- Emotional urgency mimics genuine crisis — the mandatory deliberation period is a debiasing mechanism
- Pattern tracking of Mode B invocations is diagnostic data (persistent overrides indicate either genuine crisis or rationalized rule-breaking)
- Trust your deviation from consensus only when you can identify the specific mechanism producing the inadequacy (Yudkowsky)
- The ticking-bomb case fails; civil disobedience, whistleblowing, hiding refugees pass
- Three independent checks (gates, conditions, review) reduce false positives far more than conditions alone

**Depends On:** CH-02 (motivated reasoning), CH-07-08 (why rules matter), CH-09 (Mode A)

**Depended On By:** CH-11, CH-22, all applied chapters

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Who decides when Mode B conditions are met?" — now answered by the Gating Protocol. Also: "The override conditions are too demanding" — rebutted by historical cases that pass.

**Known Weaknesses:** Sophisticated rationalizers can still potentially game the gated system. This is acknowledged as irreducible. The deliberation period creates genuine costs in fast-moving situations.

**Position Swaps:** POS-A01 v1.1 → v2.0 (vulnerability MEDIUM → LOW)

### CH-11: The Virtues — Character Specifications for the Rational Agent
**Status:** REVISED | **Date:** 2026-04-07 | **Revision:** 1

**Core Argument:** Virtues are installed patterns of response — the firmware the dual-process architecture runs on. **Second edition: expanded from twelve to sixteen virtues** across three categories: eight epistemic (scout) virtues, four instrumental (soldier) virtues, and **four care virtues** — attentiveness (noticing the needs of those who cannot articulate them), responsiveness (being changed by encounters with vulnerability), practical compassion (making care effective, not merely sincere), and solidarity (sharing the costs of another's vulnerability). The care virtues address the moral phenomena that CH-06 identified as outside the game-theoretic frame: asymmetric dependency, unrequited obligation, and the relational moral work that strategic rationality cannot generate. Gilligan's insight that moral reasoning oriented toward relationships and responsibility is a distinct and complementary capacity, not a less mature form of justice-based reasoning, grounds the addition.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Virtues as firmware, **sixteen virtues** (8 epistemic + 4 instrumental + 4 care), care virtues (attentiveness, responsiveness, practical compassion, solidarity), Gilligan's relational ethics, Kittay's derivative dependency, MacIntyre engagement

**Positions Established/Modified:** No new Registry positions, but the virtue framework is substantially expanded to integrate care ethics.

**Key Claims:**
- Virtues are not feelings, not all-or-nothing, not private, not replacements for rules or calculation
- Humility is the keystone; perfectionism is the engine; **attentiveness is the bridge** between seeing clearly and responding humanely
- **Without care virtues, a rationalist agent may be effective but blind to vulnerability**
- The care virtues prevent the framework from reducing moral life to optimization problems
- MacIntyre's critique incorporated; Gilligan's care ethics integrated as third virtue category

**Depends On:** CH-02, CH-06 (care section), CH-09-10

**Depended On By:** CH-12, CH-13-16 (especially **CH-14's dependency section**), CH-42

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "The framework has no account of care, only strategy." Now directly addressed: four care virtues are grounded in Gilligan and Tronto, integrated as a third category alongside epistemic and instrumental virtues.

**Known Weaknesses:** The sixteen-virtue list is now more comprehensive but could still be challenged as incomplete. The care virtues may overlap with certain epistemic virtues (attentiveness/curiosity). The grounding in Gilligan's empirical work could be deepened.

### CH-12: Moral Development — From Instinct to Reflection to Habit
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-04 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Human moral development moves through three stages: instinct (inherited evolutionary responses), reflection (explicit rule learning and questioning), and habit (informed automaticity). The trajectory is instinct → reflection → habit, with the third stage being not a regression but a return to automaticity enriched by reflection. Stage 3 habits are *informed* habits — automatic responses backed by reasons that can be articulated when needed. Practice is the mechanism for building Stage 3: environments, role models, failure, feedback, and narrative all play essential roles. Community is necessary — moral development cannot happen in isolation. Adults can still develop: neuroplasticity and habit formation work throughout the lifespan. The chapter names common failure modes: arrested development at Stage 1 or 2, premature closure, and dissociation between stages.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Three-stage development (instinct/reflection/habit), Stage 3 as informed automaticity, practices and the community context, failure modes of development, tsuyoku naritai as lifelong orientation, adult developmental responsibility

**Positions Established/Modified:** No new Registry positions, but the chapter establishes the developmental requirements that the framework presupposes.

**Key Claims:**
- The goal is not rejection of instinct in favor of reason, nor vice versa — it is alignment of habit with reason through practice
- Adolescent questioning is the completion of Stage 2, not a danger to be suppressed
- Stage 3 habits are less effortful than Stage 2 deliberation — this is what makes virtue livable
- Community, role models, and feedback are essential and cannot be replaced by individual effort
- Moral development is lifelong; adults retain responsibility for their own growth

**Depends On:** CH-04 (evolutionary instincts as Stage 1), CH-09-11 (the architecture and virtues that development builds)

**Depended On By:** CH-13-16 (personal ethics), CH-17 (community-building as political concern), all applied chapters (which presuppose developed agents)

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** MacIntyre's objection that moral theory divorced from community is hollow. Rebutted by making community central to the developmental process.

**Known Weaknesses:** The three-stage model is a simplification — real development is messier. The chapter does not engage deeply with the empirical literature on moral psychology (Kohlberg, Haidt, etc.). The role of trauma, mental illness, and atypical development is not addressed. The community requirements create a tension with the framework's universalism that is flagged but not fully resolved.

**Open Threads:** Deeper engagement with developmental psychology (could warrant expansion zone). The role of ritual and liturgy in moral formation (connection to religion and sanctity). Atypical development and disability (flagged for expansion).

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-04: The Alien God — Evolution, Instinct, and Reflective Rebellion
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-04 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Our moral instincts were engineered by natural selection — the "Alien God" — which optimizes for gene replication, not truth or goodness. Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory maps six evolved moral "taste receptors" (Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity, Liberty) that constitute the "first draft" of human morality. Because origin and validity are separate questions (the Separation of Origins), each instinct must be evaluated against external criteria: the valence axiom, the coordination test, and the epistemic test. Some instincts (empathy, fairness, the hive switch) track genuine moral reality and should be kept. Others (tribal xenophobia, blind deference, disgust-based dehumanization) are destructive and should be discarded with replacements built. ### CH-37: Against Nihilism and Moral Anti-Realism

- Moral disagreement is compatible with moral realism -- hard questions produce disagreement even when answers exist

- Constructivist Realism is neither fully objective nor fully subjective but sufficient to defeat nihilism
- The nihilist cannot consistently live their position -- they still flee burning buildings and avoid pain

### CH-39: Against Pure Deontology
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-05 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Kant gets much right: morality is about more than consequences, persons are ends not merely means, universalizability, motives matter, and some actions are absolutely prohibited. - The murderer-at-the-door case clearly shows absolutism fails in edge cases
- Hegel's emptiness objection has force: the Categorical Imperative needs substantive content from outside itself
- The framework absorbs Kant's strengths (universalizability, persons as ends, strong defaults) within a better architecture
- Mode A/B handles conflicting duties and extreme cases that pure deontology cannot

### CH-40: Against Moral Relativism
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-05 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Relativism captures legitimate insights (anthropological diversity, history of moral imperialism, difficulty of justification) but fails as a metaethical position. Three decisive objections: self-refutation (the claim that all moral truth is relative is itself either absolute or relative -- both horns fail), the moral progress problem (relativism makes abolition of slavery merely a change, not an improvement), and the cross-cultural critique problem (relativists cannot coherently condemn genocide in other cultures). The framework preserves relativism's legitimate insights (epistemic humility, respect for diversity, anti-imperialism) within a realist framework that grounds moral claims in the valence axiom.

**Key Claims:**
- Descriptive relativism (cultures differ) is true and uncontroversial; metaethical relativism fails
- The self-refutation problem is old but persistent and not fully answered by sophisticated relativists
- Moral progress is real (abolition, women's rights) and relativism cannot make sense of it
- Relativism's best insights (humility, anti-imperialism) are preserved in the framework without abandoning objectivity
- Cultural diversity often reflects different empirical circumstances, not genuinely incompatible values

**Depends On:** CH-03 (valence realism provides the non-relative ground), CH-37 (anti-realism fails), CH-04 (evolutionary origins do not determine validity)

**Depended On By:** CH-45 (synthesis), CH-43 (Marxist critique of ideology intersects)

### CH-24: Policy Evaluation — Political and Economic Issues
**Status:** DRAFTED (EXPANDED) | **Date:** 2026-04-05 | **Revision:** 1

**Core Argument:** The framework's seven-step policy evaluation method applies equally to political and economic questions, with four additional tests foregrounded for economic policy: the Hayekian knowledge test (does the policy-maker have the knowledge needed for this intervention?), the Pigovian fit test (does the tool match the market failure?), the symmetric diagnostic (evaluate market failure and government failure at the same level of rigor), and distributional analysis (efficiency, equity, capability à la Sen-Nussbaum, intergenerational, risk allocation). Seven worked examples — three political (minimum wage, occupational licensing, climate policy) and four economic (antitrust breakup, housing policy, tariffs, UBI vs. EITC) — demonstrate the method generating specific conclusions that cross ideological lines.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Hayekian knowledge test, Pigovian fit test, symmetric market-failure/government-failure diagnostic, distributional analysis (capability/incidence/intergenerational/risk dimensions), the "portable method" claim

**Positions Established/Modified:** No new Registry positions; strengthens POS-P02 (Institutional Mechanism Design over Ideology) and gives POS-EC01 through POS-EC05 an explicit methodological lens.

**Key Claims:**
- The same method serves both political and economic questions
- Hayekian test filters out interventions that require knowledge the regulator cannot have
- Pigovian fit test requires that the intervention actually match the structure of the market failure
- Symmetric analysis of market and government failure prevents asymmetric skepticism in either direction
- Distributional analysis is a required step, not a footnote
- The method produces specific answers that cross ideological coalitions

**Depends On:** CH-17–23 (political philosophy), CH-08 (mechanism design), forward-references CH-25 (Hayek) and CH-27 (Pigou)

The key discipline is asking the seven questions, even briefly.

**Known Weaknesses:** The four tests are interrelated and the chapter does not fully formalize their interactions. The worked examples, while informative, could be accused of cherry-picking. Distributional analysis is more an orientation than a formal method here.

### CH-44: Against Natural Law Theory
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-05 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** Natural Law — in both its Thomistic form (Aquinas) and its "new natural law" form (Finnis) — is the most sophisticated rival the framework faces after Rawlsian contractualism, and it shares significant ground with the framework (moral objectivity, flourishing as multidimensional, integration of moral and political theory, strong defaults). The framework diverges on six specific points: the strong teleological premise is modernly indefensible; the is-ought gap lands harder than natural law admits; content-generation from nature has produced shifting results in ways that reveal the reasoning is reflective construction rather than pure reading; absolutism without an override mechanism produces bad edge cases; the fixed-nature account cannot accommodate moral progress cleanly; and the "common good" concept, in natural law hands, has historically licensed imposition. The framework's grounding is naturalistic valence realism + game theory, which is thinner than Aquinas's but more honest about where normativity enters.

**Key Terms Introduced:** Thomistic natural law, new natural law theory (Finnis/Grisez), basic goods, incommensurability, requirements of practical reasonableness, "never directly attack a basic good," lex iniusta non est lex, the first precept of practical reason ("good is to be done and pursued"), the three levels of natural inclination (self-preservation, animal nature, rational nature)

**Positions Established/Modified:** No new positions; uses Q.90, A.4 and Q.94, A.2 from Aquinas's Treatise on Law as primary source material. Strengthens the framework's grounding claims by showing how they survive the strongest non-theistic realist alternative.

**Key Claims:**
- Aquinas defines law as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated"
- The first precept of practical reason is "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided," from which specific content derives via reflection on natural inclinations
- The framework owes natural law substantial debts (objectivity, multidimensional flourishing, legal/moral integration, strong defaults)
- Modern biology undermines the strong teleological premise
- The is-ought gap lands harder than natural law admits; the framework handles the bridge more honestly via explicit axiom
- Content generated by natural law has shifted over time in ways the method's self-presentation cannot explain
- Absolutism without override produces bad edge cases that natural law theorists increasingly try to mitigate

**Depends On:** CH-03 (valence grounding), CH-04 (evolutionary account against teleology), CH-09–10 (dual-process alternative to absolutism), CH-37 (DCT critique applies to theistic foundations), CH-41 (comparison to Rawlsian contractualism)

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Your naturalistic grounding is too thin to sustain the moral seriousness you want." The chapter acknowledges this as an open bet rather than a solved problem — the framework is betting that the thinner grounding is more defensible in the long run, but admits the bet could lose.

**Known Weaknesses:** The chapter engages Thomistic and new natural law but not the full breadth of the tradition (e.g., not Finnis's specific list of basic goods in depth, not George on specific applications). The treatment of moral progress relies on examples that natural law theorists would dispute. The "common good" critique could be accused of targeting specific historical failures rather than the concept in its best form.

### CH-47: The Syllabus — A Reading Path to Mastery
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-05 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** The framework stands on a specific reading tradition, and a serious reader should eventually engage the primary sources. This chapter organizes the relevant books into six core tracks (epistemology, moral psychology, game theory, moral architecture, political/economic philosophy, rival moral systems) plus an optional seventh track (frontier and applied problems). Each track has a core (load-bearing) and further reading (for depth), ordered roughly from accessible to difficult. The syllabus is a starting point, not a canon. The chapter closes with a defense of sustained, structured reading itself as the practice that produces the kind of mind the framework asks for.

**Key Terms Introduced:** The six tracks, core vs. further reading, reading in conversation with the framework, the distinction between short-form information and durable structured understanding

**Positions Established/Modified:** None; practical chapter.

**Key Claims:**
- Serious engagement with the framework's rivals requires primary sources, not summaries
- Sustained reading is what produces calibrated long-form moral reasoning
- A book a month or every three months is a realistic pace
- The syllabus is organized by the framework's architecture, so tracks can be read in parallel
- Note-taking and conversation with the framework are essential to turning reading into understanding

**Depends On:** CH-01–46 (the tracks reference every prior chapter's source material)

**Depended On By:** CH-48 (the Oath's eleventh clause on self-correction depends on the reader having actually read sources)

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Most people won't read this much." The chapter acknowledges this and pitches the syllabus as a starting point, not a gatekeeping requirement. The book stands without the syllabus; the syllabus is for those who want to go further.

**Known Weaknesses:** Specific book recommendations will date. Some important authors are omitted (e.g., Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other* is only implicit, several non-Western sources beyond Confucius and Buddhism are missing). The defense of long-form reading may feel nostalgic to some readers, though the empirical case is strong.

**Position Swaps:** None

### CH-48: The Oath of the Rational Agent
**Status:** DRAFTED | **Date:** 2026-04-05 | **Revision:** 0

**Core Argument:** A moral framework is not a body of argument but a way of living. This final chapter offers the reader a twelve-clause oath — a set of specific commitments corresponding to the framework's major claims — as a way of marking the transition from studying the framework to living it. The clauses cover: truth-seeking (Ch. 2), suffering as moral starting point (Ch. 3), rule discipline with five-condition override (Chs. 9–10), persons as ends (Ch. 19, 40), humility (Ch. 11), relationships as iterated games (Chs. 7, 14), community as required context (Ch. 12), political engagement via the seven-step method (Ch. 24), economic engagement with the four tests (Ch. 24), frontier problems with proportionate seriousness (Book VII), personal Position Registry maintenance, and the long game of compound investment. The chapter names the failure modes (performance without practice, rigidity, moral superiority, silent drift, burnout, isolation) and what the oath does not require (certainty, full agreement, withdrawal from existing traditions, public declaration).

**Key Terms Introduced:** The twelve clauses of the oath, the six failure modes, the four things the oath does not require, "a life that is more your own"

**Positions Established/Modified:** None; this is a practical commitment chapter, not a theoretical claim chapter.

**Key Claims:**
- The framework is lived, not merely studied
- The oath is taken in private, to oneself; no public declaration needed
- Each clause is grounded in a specific chapter's argument
- The oath is compatible with ongoing inquiry, partial agreement, and religious or prior traditions
- The failure modes are real and must be watched for (especially moral superiority and silent drift)
- The oath is not the only life worth living; it is the life the framework asks of those persuaded by its arguments

**Depends On:** Every prior chapter; explicitly references Chs. 2, 3, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 19, 24, 30, 31, 32, 40

**Depended On By:** The reader's life, if they take it

**Strongest Objection Addressed:** "Framing the commitments as an oath is manipulative — it uses ritual language to extract promises before the reader has fully examined the framework." Rebutted by making the oath explicitly revisable, by listing what it does not require, and by framing it as self-administered rather than externally imposed.

**Known Weaknesses:** The oath format may strike some readers as sentimental or performative. The twelve-clause structure is provisional and could be reorganized. The failure mode list is probably incomplete. The chapter's final tone leans earnest in a way that some readers will find moving and others off-putting.

**Open Threads:** How a reader should document their oath-taking for the purposes of clause XI (self-correction) is only gestured at. The relationship between the oath and formal institutional oaths (medical, legal, constitutional) is noted but not developed.

**Position Swaps:** None
